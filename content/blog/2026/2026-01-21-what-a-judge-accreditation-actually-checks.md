We get some version of the same question every January: "Is it too late to start on judge accreditation before this year's competition?" It's almost never too late, but it's also not something to start the week entries open. With national milk production running ahead of last year's pace and prices easing, we're expecting a fuller entry field than usual this cycle, and a fuller field needs a judging pool ready to score it consistently. The Competition Judge Accreditation is one of the more misunderstood credentials in the federation's lineup, partly because members assume it's an extension of the Sensory Evaluation Certificate. It isn't, even though the two share some ground.

The Sensory Evaluation Certificate tests whether you can describe and defend what you're tasting — flavor development, texture, aroma defects, the vocabulary that lets two people agree on what's in front of them. Judge Accreditation goes a step further and tests whether you can score cheese *against a category standard* consistently, under time pressure, alongside other judges whose scores need to be defensible when a member calls afterward to ask why their entry placed where it did. It's less about your palate and more about your calibration against a rubric — and against other judges scoring the same table.

## What the accreditation actually covers

The credential works through a combination of coursework and supervised judging. Candidates work through category standards for the major cheese families — fresh and lactic, natural rind, washed rind, blue, and the rest — and then sit in on actual competition tables as scoring shadows before they're accredited to judge independently. The Awards & Competition Committee runs the supervised sessions, and they're intentionally scheduled well ahead of the federation's own competition cycle so new judges get real reps before anything they score counts toward a placement.

That timing is the part members underestimate. If you want to be accredited in time to judge this year's competition, the supervised-judging sessions need to happen now, not in the weeks before entries close. Waiting until spring usually means you're accredited in time for *next* year instead.

## Why this matters beyond the trophy table

A credentialed judging pool is part of what makes a competition's results mean something to a buyer or a distributor deciding what to stock. When a wheel wins its category, that result is only as trustworthy as the judges who scored it — which is exactly why the accreditation exists as a standalone credential rather than a rubber stamp that comes with membership. Members who've gone through it also tell us it changed how they evaluate their own wheels before they ever get to a competition table; scoring other people's cheese against a rubric is a different skill than tasting your own, and it tends to sharpen both.

If you've been curious about judging, or you judged informally in the past and never made it official, reach out to the Awards & Competition Committee this month. The earlier you start, the more real judging time you get before anything is on the line.

If you're already accredited but haven't judged in the last cycle or two, check in with the committee anyway. Category standards get revisited periodically as the entry mix shifts — a category that was mostly cow's-milk washed rind a few years back might now see a meaningful share of mixed-milk or goat entries, and the standard adjusts to match what's actually being submitted. A quick refresher conversation is a lot cheaper than finding out mid-table that your calibration has drifted from where the rubric currently sits. It's also a good excuse to volunteer for a supervised session yourself; new judges learn as much from watching an experienced one work a flight as they do from the coursework itself.
