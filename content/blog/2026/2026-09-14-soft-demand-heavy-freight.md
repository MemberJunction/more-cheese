The federal weekly dairy report that landed on Friday had two lines we kept rereading. Domestic and foodservice cheese demand was described as below expectations, and seasonal holiday baking demand for butter was running lower than buyers had planned for this time of year. Soft demand is one story. Soft demand with a freight bill that won't come down is a different one, and that second story is the one landing on members' desks.

We've been hearing it for a few weeks now. Last week a distribution manager at Great Lakes Gourmet Distributors told us her reefer lanes are costing noticeably more per pallet than they did a year ago, and that she can't pass all of it through because her retail accounts are ordering a little tighter. Diesel has had a year of record-setting prices, and the trade press has been tracking how that shows up in hauling charges. Nobody we spoke to expects relief before the holidays, and the latest federal outlook raised its milk production forecast again, which does little to firm up cheese prices.

## Why this hits specialty makers harder

A commodity plant ships truckloads on contract and spreads the freight over a lot of pounds. A farmstead creamery ships a few cases to a distributor two states away, and the freight lands on a much smaller base. When one cheese moves at a modest price and the carrier adds a fuel surcharge and a liftgate fee, the freight can quietly become one of the biggest lines on the invoice.

Cheese prices are also drifting lower in the wholesale market as foodservice buying cools. So the squeeze is two-sided: the price you can get is flat or slipping, and the cost of getting the wheel to the buyer keeps climbing. For a member who has priced delivered cases the same way since spring, the margin may be thinner than the make sheet says.

We don't see this as a crisis. Costs for feed and energy eased this year compared with the squeeze of 2025, and plenty of creameries are in a steadier spot than they were twelve months ago. But steady isn't the same as comfortable, and freight is the line item most likely to surprise you in the fourth quarter, when volumes rise and carriers are busiest.

## Three things worth doing this week

First, pull your last ten outbound invoices and add up what you paid to move product, including fuel surcharges and delivery accessorials. Divide by pounds shipped. If you've never done that number, you may be startled by it, and you'll want it before any holiday price conversation with a buyer.

Second, look at where you ship. A buyer 400 miles away who orders eight cases may cost you more than two nearer buyers who each order five. Consolidating deliveries onto fewer, fuller drops is the least glamorous and most dependable way to lower freight per pound.

Third, talk to your distributor before the season, not during it. Several members have told us they got better terms by committing to a delivery schedule in September than by asking for a favor in November. A distributor that knows what's coming can plan its trucks, and it tends to remember who made that easy.

If your numbers say the freight is worse than you thought, don't just shave your own margin. Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese walks through building delivered price by lane, and our Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with members who have already reworked their routes. Several creameries in the same region have shared a truck for the fall rush, and that kind of arrangement works best when it's set up early.

We'll keep reading the weekly reports and tell you if the demand picture changes. For now, the practical read is simple: know your cost to deliver, and don't let it hide behind a flat price.
