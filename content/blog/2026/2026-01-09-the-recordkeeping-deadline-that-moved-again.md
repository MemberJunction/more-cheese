The federal traceability recordkeeping deadline a lot of our members have been quietly dreading was originally set to land this month — January 20, to be exact, just two weeks from this post. It's been pushed out again, this time to mid-2028, after a federal spending measure directed the regulator not to enforce it before then. If you make or handle anything on the government's covered-foods list, you now have roughly two and a half more years before full lot-level tracking needs to be in place at every step a product passes through your operation.

We understand the instinct to file this away and forget about it until the new deadline gets close. We'd ask you not to. This is the second time the compliance date has moved since the rule was finalized back in 2022, and the pattern each time has been the same: guidance keeps evolving, the compliance bar keeps getting sharper in the details, and the members who started building their systems early are the ones who aren't scrambling when a real date finally lands. The runway keeps getting longer. It won't be infinite.

## What this actually asks of a small creamery

The traceability rule's core idea is simple even though the paperwork isn't: for certain higher-risk foods, a business needs to be able to trace a lot forward and backward through its supply chain fast enough to support a recall investigation. For a small operation, the sticking points are rarely conceptual. They're practical — a wax-dipped wheel that started as three different vats, a farmstead selling direct that never numbered a batch because nothing required it, a distributor relationship that predates any of this and was never set up to pass lot data along in the first place.

The extension itself came out of exactly those practical concerns. Entities across the supply chain raised real objections to the original timeline, and the extra time is federal lawmakers and the regulator acknowledging that a rule written with large processors in mind takes longer to fit onto a small make room than it does onto a plant that already tracks lots for other reasons. That's a reasonable thing to have happened. It doesn't change what the rule will eventually ask of you.

We'd also flag a smaller, related item for members who sell into wholesale cheese categories: a proposed carve-out for certain Grade "A" products on the interstate shippers list is still working its way through the regulatory process. It hasn't landed yet, and we're not going to tell members to plan around a rule that isn't final. But if your product line includes anything close to that boundary, it's worth having the Food Safety Committee's summary on hand so you're not caught flat-footed either way once it does.

## What to do with the extra time

Our recommendation is unglamorous: use 2026 and 2027 to build the habit before the rule forces it. Start numbering batches consistently even where nothing requires it yet. Ask your suppliers now what lot information they can actually hand you, rather than assuming you'll sort it out later, closer to the deadline, when everyone else is asking the same question at once. If you handle anything on the covered list, the Food Safety Committee keeps an updated plain-language summary of what's in and what's out, and it's worth five minutes even if your first instinct is that none of this touches your operation.

The deadline moved. The direction of travel didn't. We'd rather you get ahead of it on your own schedule than someone else's.
