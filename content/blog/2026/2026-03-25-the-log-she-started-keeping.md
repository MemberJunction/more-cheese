Two years ago, a farmstead cheesemaker we'll call the maker at Crowfeather Creamery inherited a small aging operation from the previous owner along with a single ring binder of handwritten batch notes going back a decade. This spring, she's the one other members call when they want to know how to build a batch log that actually works.

Nothing about her system started out impressive. Her first winter, she kept notes the same way her predecessor had — a paper log by the vat, dates and quantities in pencil, good enough until a distributor asked for something more specific than "the second batch in March." She didn't have an answer. That was the moment she decided to rebuild the whole thing.

## Building it wrong twice before it worked

Her first attempt was a spreadsheet she copied from a template she found online, and it fell apart within a month because it assumed one batch per day, and her creamery sometimes runs three. Her second attempt tracked everything by date instead of by batch, which sounds like a small difference and caused her a genuine headache when two batches aged out on the same day but needed different labels. What finally worked was simple in hindsight: one row per batch, a lot code that's just the date plus a letter, and a column for exactly where in the cave that batch is sitting, because "somewhere on the middle shelf" turned out not to be a searchable field.

She'll tell you, without much prompting, that none of this was about compliance for its own sake. It was about being able to answer a buyer's question in thirty seconds instead of walking the cave with a flashlight. The recordkeeping conversation happening at the federal level right now — the push toward clearer lot tracking for aged products, and the guidance that's been coming out of it this quarter — is the kind of thing she already solved for herself, out of necessity, before anyone asked her to.

It also turned out to matter more than she expected the week a supplier called asking which delivery of milk had gone into a specific batch that a downstream buyer had questions about. Two years ago that call would have meant an afternoon of guessing. This spring it took her four minutes, because the lot code on the wheel pointed straight back to the row in her log.

## What she'd tell a newer member

Her advice for anyone starting from a ring binder: don't wait until an inspector or a buyer forces the issue. Build the log around your actual workflow, not around what a template assumes your workflow looks like, and expect to redo it at least once. She's also quick to say she didn't do this alone — a Food Safety Committee volunteer talked her through her second rebuild over the phone, and the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate coursework gave her the vocabulary to know what fields actually mattered.

She's also candid that the first version of her log was, in her words, "technically fine and completely useless," because it captured everything the rule might eventually ask for and nothing she herself needed day to day. The version that stuck is the one built for her own questions first, with the compliance fields layered in afterward.

If you're still keeping batch records the way you inherited them and it isn't working, that's a normal place to be in year two. The fix usually isn't more software. It's an afternoon with a blank spreadsheet and someone who's already made the mistakes you're about to make.
