The federal government's mid-June dairy market report landed with the kind of news members have been waiting two years to hear: nothing alarming. Farm-level milk production is starting to contract in the eastern half of the country as the spring flush winds down and early-summer heat leans on both pastures and herds. Nonfat dry milk and cheese prices eased slightly week over week, but the broader 2026 production forecast was nudged up again, not down.

If that reads like a shrug, it should. After two years of watching feed costs and closures dominate every market update, a report where the biggest story is "the flush is ending on schedule" is itself the story. The federal forecast now projects milk output around 236 billion pounds for the year, with all-milk prices still expected to land higher than 2025's average even after this month's small downward revision to the cheese price line.

## What eases and what doesn't

For members buying milk on a component basis, a tightening supply heading into midsummer is generally good news for your price per hundredweight, even with cheese prices flat to slightly softer. For members further down the chain — the counter and wholesale side of the membership — a calmer commodity market means your case pricing has a better chance of holding steady through July and August instead of chasing a spike.

The heat is the wrinkle worth watching, not celebrating. Several members in our warmer regions have already told us they are seeing the same pattern the federal report describes: cows eating less, milk components shifting, and herds needing more active management through the hottest weeks. If you buy milk rather than produce it, ask your supplier now whether they expect a component dip in July, not after it shows up in your invoice.

## What we're recommending

Treat this report as a planning window rather than a verdict. If your fall case planning has been on hold waiting for a clearer signal, this is close to one: a market that isn't swinging hard in either direction gives you room to lock in supplier agreements or set autumn pricing without guessing at a moving target. The Membership & Outreach Committee has heard from several small producers this spring asking for a plain-language walkthrough of how to read these federal reports without a commodities background — if that's you, reach out through the member portal and we'll point you to the right committee contact.

We'll keep tracking the summer numbers as they come in. A quiet report in June is worth taking at face value, but it is also the kind of month that can turn on a single heat wave, so keep an eye on your own supplier's updates alongside ours.

If you don't normally follow the twice-weekly cash trading reports, this is a good month to start. Early June saw barrel and block cheese prices hold roughly steady from one session to the next, which sounds unremarkable until you compare it with the swings members were describing in committee calls a year ago. A market that isn't moving is, in its own quiet way, a market that's stopped punishing you for guessing wrong on timing.

That stability matters most for members who set prices on a schedule rather than reacting week to week. If your shop or counter reprices seasonally, a flat cash market gives you a rare chance to set a number in June and actually hold it through August without a mid-summer correction. Members who buy in bulk for aging or affinage should ask their suppliers for a written price hold rather than a verbal one — a calm month is exactly when a supplier is most willing to put that in writing, and exactly when it's easiest to forget to ask.

None of this means the underlying cost pressure of the last two years has fully unwound. Feed and energy costs are easing, not gone, and several of the farmstead creameries the federation has supported through consolidation and succession conversations this year are still working off debt taken on during the harder stretch. A calm market report is good news for this month's decisions. It is not, on its own, a signal that every member's underlying finances have caught up.
