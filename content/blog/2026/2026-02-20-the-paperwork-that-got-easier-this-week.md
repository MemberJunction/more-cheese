If you make, pack, or hold cheese and you've been half-dreading a wave of new recordkeeping rules, this week brought some relief instead of more homework. The federal food-safety regulator issued new guidance on its additional traceability recordkeeping requirements, and at the same time finalized an exemption for a specific product line that had been swept into the rule more broadly than made sense: Grade A cottage cheese that already appears on the interstate milk shippers list. Effective this week, that product is out from under the extra paperwork, on the reasoning that it's already covered by processing requirements under the standing milk ordinance system.

For most of our members that exemption alone won't matter much — cottage cheese processors on that specific list are a narrow slice of the membership. What matters more is the shape of the decision: it's a sign the regulator is willing to draw finer lines around which cheese products genuinely need the tightest tracking, rather than applying one blanket standard to every SKU on the traceability list. Several aged and specialty cheeses remain squarely on that list, and nothing in this week's action changes that.

## What's still coming, and when

The broader traceability recordkeeping requirement — the one asking covered producers to be able to trace a lot of cheese back through its supply chain and produce those records within a set window of a request — has had its enforcement start pushed out before, and that extended timeline still stands. We know several of you have been holding off on overhauling your batch-tracking systems because the deadline kept moving, and we understand the instinct. Our advice hasn't changed: build the system now, on your own timeline, rather than in a scramble once enforcement actually starts. A pushed deadline is not a canceled one.

The guidance released this week also answered a handful of the stakeholder questions that had been sitting unresolved — mostly technical points about how records for a mixed-ingredient product should be organized when only some of those ingredients are on the traceability list. If your cheese includes an add-in or inclusion sourced from a separate supplier, that's worth a read before you finalize your own recordkeeping template.

## Where this leaves you

Two practical takeaways. First, check whether your specific product line is still on the traceability list — this week's exemption was narrow, and assuming you're covered by it without checking is a mistake in the wrong direction. Second, if you haven't started building batch-level traceability records that link intake to output, this is a good week to start, given that the rules keep getting refined rather than repealed. The Food Safety Committee is maintaining a plain-language summary of what's changed and what hasn't, and we'll flag it again the next time the guidance moves.

If your operation is still running traceability off separate paper logs in different rooms, our Cave Management and Food Safety & HACCP coursework both touch on building a single batch-record system — not because the rule requires it of everyone yet, but because untangling that system under a real deadline is a much worse way to learn it than doing so now.

We'd also point out, gently: a cave that can already tell you which rack a wheel came from is a cave that's going to have a much easier time with any of this than one that's relying on memory and a whiteboard.
