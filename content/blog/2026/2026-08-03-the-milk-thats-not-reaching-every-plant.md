Two members can be running plants forty miles apart this month and describing two different summers. One tells us milk is arriving on schedule, tanker after tanker, because it comes through a cooperative that owns the relationship end to end. The other is watching the outside-supplier column on the intake sheet get thinner by the week. That split has been building since spring, and August is the month it started to matter for what's actually on the wholesale case.

Federal milk price reporting has Class IV down again this month, continuing a pullback from the summer highs, while Class I moved lower on the back of ample raw production paired with softer demand in some cheese categories. Read quickly, that sounds like a supply glut. It isn't, evenly. It's a supply story with a seam running through the middle of it, and which side of the seam your plant sits on depends more on who owns your milk contract than on how much milk exists in the region.

## Why the split matters more than the average

If you buy your milk on the spot or short-term market rather than through a cooperative affiliation, you have felt this year's price swings twice as hard — once on the way up, once now on the way down, with less of a cushion either direction. Members telling us their supply is "tight" this month generally aren't describing a shortage so much as a supplier who's rationing attention: cooperative-affiliated plants get first call, and everyone else gets what's left after that.

The practical effect shows up as declining cheese production feeding tighter finished-goods inventories over the coming weeks, even while raw milk itself isn't scarce. If you're a smaller producer or a buyer building a fall case, that's worth planning around now rather than in October. A plant running short on the milk side has less room to say yes to a rush order later, and a buyer who assumes cheese availability tracks headline milk prices one-to-one is going to be surprised by lead times that don't match.

## What this means for pricing conversations

For members setting contracts or renewing supply agreements this quarter, the useful question isn't "what's the milk price doing" — it's "what kind of milk contract is my supplier working from." A producer sourcing through a cooperative has more predictable input costs and, generally, steadier throughput; a producer buying on the open market has more upside when prices fall and more exposure when they don't. Neither is wrong, but they call for different conversations with your own buyers about lead time and price stability.

The specialty side of the business is still growing underneath all of this — global demand for aged, authenticated, craft-produced cheese keeps climbing, and that demand doesn't care which milk contract fed the vat. But growing demand sitting on top of an uneven supply chain is exactly the condition that produces spot shortages of specific styles even in a month when the aggregate numbers look fine. If your fall case planning assumes this August's milk price is the whole story, it isn't.

If you're not sure which side of that seam your own supply sits on, that's a conversation worth having with your milk buyer before the flush eases further into fall.
