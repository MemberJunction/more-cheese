The federal food-safety regulator put out a new training curriculum on additional traceability recordkeeping this week, and a handful of members have already written in asking whether it applies to them and, if so, what it actually asks a small cheese operation to do. Both are fair questions, and the honest answer is that the rule affects more of you than the compliance date might suggest.

The underlying requirement is not new. It asks certain foods on a federal traceability list — soft-ripened and semi-soft cheeses among them — to keep specific records at each point they change hands: who sent it, who received it, the lot, the quantity, and the date. Cottage cheese made under the standard pasteurized milk ordinance rules picked up an exemption earlier this year, which has led a few members to assume the whole rule got softer. It didn't; one product category got carved out, and the rest of the list stands. The compliance deadline itself has been pushed to July 2028, which sounds distant but is closer than a three-year runway feels once you account for how long it actually takes to change a recordkeeping habit on a working creamery floor.

## What the training curriculum adds

What is new this week is not the rule but the teaching material behind it — a structured curriculum meant to walk a food business through what a compliant record actually looks like, rather than leaving operators to interpret the regulation cold. That is useful, because the rule is written in fairly abstract language about "critical tracking events," and most of the confusion we hear from members is really a translation problem: what does a critical tracking event look like on a Tuesday when you're wrapping wheels and a driver is waiting at the dock.

The regulator is also weighing whether the lot-level tracking requirement itself needs adjusting for smaller operations, and has floated a discussion paper this week on where it might allow more flexibility before the compliance date arrives. Nothing in that paper is final, and members should not restructure their recordkeeping around a proposal that could still change. But it is a sign the rule is not finished settling, and the committee's advice is to build a system that meets the plain-language requirement as written today rather than betting on a future carve-out.

Our Food Safety Committee has been fielding a steady stream of these questions since the original rule was finalized, and the committee's read is that members who already keep tight batch and lot records for their own quality control are most of the way there already. The gap tends to show up at the handoff points — what gets written down when a wheel leaves your cave for a distributor, and what the distributor is supposed to hand back to you in return. If you have never mapped those handoffs on paper, that is the exercise worth doing this month, before the new curriculum's language becomes the standard everyone is measured against.

## Where to start

The federation's *Listeria Control in Small Creameries* course already touches lot tracking as part of its environmental monitoring unit, and it is a reasonable place to start if traceability paperwork has always felt like someone else's job. It won't file the recordkeeping for you, but it will get your handoffs onto paper before an auditor asks to see them.

If your creamery ships across state lines regularly, this is also a good week to loop in whoever handles your interstate shipment paperwork, since the traceability records and the shipment records tend to ask for overlapping information. Getting the two systems talking to each other now is considerably easier than reconciling them after the fact.
