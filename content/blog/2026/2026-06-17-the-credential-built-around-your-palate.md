Every year around this time we get the same question from members thinking about the Awards & Competition Committee's judging track: do I need to be a "good taster" before I sign up for the Sensory Evaluation Certificate? The honest answer is no, and the credential is designed around that answer. A trained palate isn't a gift some people have and others don't — it's a vocabulary problem, and the course exists to give you the vocabulary.

Sensory training starts with a shared lexicon, not a shared opinion. Before a panel ever scores a wheel, everyone in the room needs to agree on what "pungent" means versus "sour," what separates a "chalky" paste from a "gritty" one, and where the line sits between a forgivable rind defect and a disqualifying one. Without that shared language, two judges can taste the same cheese, both be right about what they experienced, and still land on scores that are impossible to reconcile. The certificate spends real time on this before it ever gets to actual scoring practice.

## What the course actually asks of you

The Sensory Evaluation Certificate runs candidates through structured tasting flights — usually six to eight samples per session, which lines up with the point most trained tasters hit palate fatigue if the flight runs longer than that. You'll taste reference cheeses alongside deliberately defective ones, learn to isolate a single flavor note without letting the others crowd it out, and practice scoring against a rubric rather than a gut reaction. By the end, you're not just tasting cheese — you're producing a defensible written justification for a score, the kind you'd need to stand behind if a competition entrant asked why their wheel placed where it did.

One member finishing the certificate this spring described the shift plainly: "I used to just know when something was off. Now I can tell you which off it is, and that's the part that actually matters when you're one of five judges and you all have to agree on a ribbon." That's the practical payoff — the credential doesn't just make your own tasting sharper, it makes you useful on a panel where consensus is the whole point.

The certificate also feeds directly into Competition Judge Accreditation, which the Awards & Competition Committee requires for anyone scoring at this year's flagship competition. If you're weighing whether to put your name forward for judging in Madison, the sensory certificate is the natural first step, and cohorts scheduled between now and late summer will comfortably clear the accreditation deadline. Reach out to the Awards & Competition Committee if you want the exact cutoff for this year's roster.

It's worth saying plainly that this credential doesn't expire the day the certificate arrives. Judging courses elsewhere in the industry keep their reference materials live for months after enrollment closes precisely because sensory memory fades if you don't keep exercising it, and our own certificate is built the same way — periodic refresher flights, not a one-and-done exam. A judge who trained two years ago and hasn't tasted competitively since is a different judge than one who's kept the habit up, and panels notice the difference in how confidently someone defends a score under questioning.

Whatever your entry point — competition judging, quality control on your own line, or just wanting to talk about your product with more precision — the certificate rewards the same habit: slow down, name what you're tasting, and write it down before you decide what it means.
