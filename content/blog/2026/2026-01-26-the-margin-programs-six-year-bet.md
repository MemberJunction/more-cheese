Milk checks opened the new year lower than most farmstead members wanted to see. Class I base pricing dropped sharply from December, and the broader all-milk average is running at its softest January reading in half a decade, largely because the national herd kept expanding through a year when a lot of members expected it to shrink. If your books look tighter than they did in the fall, you are not imagining it, and you are not alone.

That timing is what makes this year's federal margin coverage enrollment worth a longer look than usual. The window is open through the end of February, and for the first time producers can lock in a coverage level for a full six-year term instead of re-enrolling annually, at a meaningfully discounted premium. For a farmstead operation that has spent the last two seasons watching feed costs whipsaw and then finally ease, a six-year commitment is a different kind of decision than the one-year version members are used to making.

## What the six-year option actually trades away

The appeal is obvious: lock in a coverage level once, save on premium every year after, and stop re-litigating the decision every January. The trade-off is just as real. If your margin outlook improves faster than expected — say costs keep falling the way this stabilization year has been trending — you're still paying into a coverage level you picked when things looked worse, without the annual chance to dial it down. Several members who called our membership desk this month asked essentially the same question: is this a hedge against 2025 happening again, or a bet that 2025 already happened and won't repeat?

We don't have a house answer to that, and we wouldn't give members financial advice even if we did. What we can tell you is what the program actually does at each coverage tier, how the premium discount is structured, and where the enrollment deadline sits relative to your other spring planning. A farmstead operation weighing this against equipment financing or a hire decision should be doing that math with current numbers, not a memory of what margins looked like at the worst of last year's feed spike.

## Where the case for caution comes from

The rebalancing story so far in 2026 has two sides that don't move in lockstep. Herd expansion has kept downward pressure on the milk price side of the margin, which is bad news if you sell raw milk into a co-op pool. But several of the same cost pressures that squeezed farmstead operations through last year's creamery closures are easing, not worsening — which is the other half of a margin calculation, and the half that gets less attention because it's harder to headline. A member doing this math needs both sides on the same page, not last spring's feed bill next to this week's milk price.

If you're an ICF member with your own milking herd or a supply contract tied to a component-priced pool, this is worth twenty minutes with whoever does your books before the enrollment window closes. If you buy milk rather than produce it, the same price softness is worth watching for a different reason: it may show up as room in your supplier's next quote, or it may not, depending on what they locked in last fall. Either way, talk to your supplier this month rather than assuming.

The federation doesn't administer this program and can't file on a member's behalf, but the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a running list of extension offices and cooperative advisors who field these questions every enrollment season. If your accountant hasn't run the six-year numbers against your own production history yet, that's the place to start before the deadline closes the option for another six years.
