Late-August market weekends still look busy from the road: full lots, long lines at the produce stands, the same regulars filling their bags before the heat sets in. But members who have worked a booth for more than a season or two say the shape of the day has already started to change. Selection tightens as other vendors' harvests wind down, end-of-day surplus becomes more common at neighboring stands, and shoppers who spent June and July stocking up for the week start buying smaller and later in the afternoon.

None of that is bad news for a cheese table specifically. Small-batch cheese tends to hold its selling pattern later into the season than produce does, since it isn't tied to a single harvest window. But it does mean the last six to eight weeks of market season reward a different kind of attention than the peak did, and it's worth planning for on purpose rather than just showing up out of habit.

## What changes in the last stretch

The clearest shift is in when people buy. Members running a table report more transactions happening later in the market day as the season turns, with shoppers timing their visit around what is still fresh rather than what's newest. If your booth has been running on a June layout — best-sellers up front, everything else behind it — late August is a reasonable point to walk your own table like a customer would and see what's actually easy to find. A wheel that's been sitting in the same spot since spring may just need to move six inches to sell again.

The other shift is digital. More vendors are taking payment through phone-based apps than did a year or two ago, which matters most in these final weeks when a customer decides on impulse rather than arriving with cash set aside. A member who sells at three markets a week told us the switch paid for itself the first weekend a rind-washed wheel sold to someone who "was just going to look." It's a small operational change, but one that compounds over a season's worth of Saturdays.

This is also happening against a backdrop of real demand for the category members are selling into. Analysts covering the broader specialty cheese market have pointed to sustained consumer interest in authenticated, craft-produced cheese as a growth driver going into next year — which is a fancier way of saying the same thing a lot of our members already know from their own tables: people are still willing to pay for something a farmstead made, if you make it easy for them to find and buy it.

## Worth doing before the season ends

The federation's *Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets* course covers booth layout by time of day, payment friction, and how to plan a table's last weeks so they don't just trail off into October. It also walks through the handoff many members make this time of year: which products move from the market table to a wholesale account for the colder months, and which stay market-only because they don't hold up well to a delivery route.

If you haven't already, this is a good week to decide which of your products are worth carrying into fall pop-ups or holiday markets, and which were really a summer item. A cheese that sold well against fresh produce in July doesn't automatically sell well against baked goods and gift boxes in November. Members who've done this a few cycles say the decision is easier made now, with a full season of sales behind you, than in October when the weather makes the call for you.

If you're weighing whether to add a second weekly market for the fall, or drop one, the course is a reasonable place to start; it's built around exactly that kind of seasonal math rather than general retail advice. Registration is open through the federation's education page.
