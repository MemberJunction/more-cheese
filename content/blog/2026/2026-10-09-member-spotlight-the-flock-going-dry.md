Most members make cheese all year. A sheep-dairy member in the upper Midwest does not, and she told us that October is her favorite month for exactly that reason. "It's the one month when the milk stops and the cheese starts talking," she said.

We spoke with her this week, between the last few milkings and a morning spent turning wheels. She asked us to leave her name off, so she is a farmstead maker with a small flock, a one-room cave, and a hard end date.

## A season with a finish line

Sheep breed in response to shortening days, so their milk is seasonal. A typical small flock lambs in spring, is milked through the summer, and is dried off in the autumn before the rams go in for the next cycle. Goats follow a similar rhythm with a longer window. The economics are not easy anywhere: a major sheep-milk cheese region in Spain expects its 2026 milk output to be about a quarter below the level of two years ago, with farmers saying current prices do not cover their costs. Cow dairies stagger breeding and milk year-round, which is a convenience sheep dairies do not get.

For her, that means roughly five months of milk and seven months of selling what she made in them. "People hear 'seasonal' and think it is a slogan," she said. "For me it's a calendar. I have about a hundred and fifty days to make everything I will sell until next June."

Late-lactation milk is different from spring milk, she says. Output drops, and the fat and protein come back up as the season ends. The curd behaves differently, and she adjusts her make for it. "The last month of milk is the richest I get," she said. "I save it for the cheeses I want to age longest."

**What the work looks like now.** Her October list is short and specific:

- **Count the wheels.** She tallies what is in the cave by style and age, and compares it to what she expects to sell through the holidays.
- **Plan the last makes.** A final firm cheese in the last week of milk goes into the cave for spring.
- **Clean down and rest.** Equipment gets a proper deep clean while there is no milk to process. She rebuilds her swab log for the dry months, too.
- **Call the counters.** She tells her retail customers what is coming, what is sold out, and when fresh cheese returns.

That last point matters. A regional market report from last week described manufacturers in parts of the West hunting for spot milk and finding little, so a retailer who has secured a small maker's wheels early is better off than one scrambling in December. Her fresh cheeses are gone by the end of the month, and a retailer who knows that in September is a retailer who will not be surprised in December.

**What she took from the federation.** She took our **Goat & Sheep Milk Cheesemaking** course early in her membership, and she says the lactation-stage module is the one she returns to. "It gave me words for what I was already seeing in the vat," she said. She also sat in on a workshop on cave planning, and now keeps a one-page "dry season" checklist taped inside the cave door.

She is candid about the hard parts. A seasonal make leaves no room to recover from a bad batch in August. Cash is tight in November, before the holiday sales arrive. And a flock that has gone dry is a flock that still has to be fed. "I plan the winter in September," she said. "If I have not, I find out at the feed store."

Her advice to a new sheep or goat maker was simple. Build your pricing around the months you do not milk. Keep two or three styles that age well and one that sells fast. And, as she put it, "Do not apologize for a calendar. Customers like knowing that something is made when it is made."

**If your make is seasonal too.** If your milk has a calendar, the weeks around the end of lactation are a good time to review your stock and your price list together. **Cave Management** and **Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese** are worth a look, and the Membership & Outreach Committee can introduce you to other seasonal makers in your region.

We will check in with her in spring, when the first fresh cheese of the new season goes out.
