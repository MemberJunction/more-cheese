Not every closure that got talked about last year actually happened. This week's spotlight is on a member — a farmstead cheesemaker's daughter in her early thirties who asked us not to use her name, since the conversation with her father is still recent — whose family creamery came within a season of shutting down and instead changed hands inside the family.

Her father started the operation more than twenty years ago, built it into a small but steady wholesale account with a regional distributor, and by late 2025 was ready to be done. Feed costs had eaten two years of margin, he was tired, and closing felt simpler than finding a buyer. "He'd basically made peace with it," she told us. "He wasn't sad about it in the way I expected. He was just done, and I think he assumed I didn't want it either."

## The conversation nobody scheduled

She did want it, or came to, over a few months of conversations that started as help with the books and turned into something more like due diligence. She pulled two years of cost records, sat with the federation's margin coverage materials to understand what her father had and hadn't been using, and worked out what the operation would need to look like under her instead of him — smaller batch sizes at first, a renegotiated distributor contract, and a much closer eye on the vat price than her father had ever kept.

What made the difference, she says, wasn't a single number. It was realizing the creamery's problem in 2025 was mostly her father's exhaustion plus a bad cost year overlapping at the same time, not a business that fundamentally didn't work. "Take away either one of those and he probably keeps going," she said. "It just happened that both landed on him in the same eighteen months."

That distinction is one we've been hearing more from members generally this winter. National figures show cheese production actually hit a record last year even as individual farmstead operations struggled, which sounds contradictory until you remember an industry total can grow while smaller producers inside it are the ones absorbing the cost pressure. Her father's creamery wasn't losing to the market. It was losing to a specific, survivable combination of bad timing and a person who was ready to stop.

## What she'd tell another family

She took over the operation formally at the start of this year and is now the one signing the vat records. Her advice to other members having a similar conversation is blunt: start the cost-record review before you're deciding whether to close, not after. "By the time it's a crisis conversation, you're making a decision with bad information. We had the numbers because I'd been asking for them for months before it got urgent."

She's also had to get comfortable with a kind of scrutiny her father never applied to himself — checking the federal price reports each month against her own contract terms, something she says he "did by feel, and honestly did fine, but I don't trust myself to do it by feel yet." Retail whole milk averaged a record price per gallon nationally last year, which she reads less as good news for her own creamery and more as a sign of how much cost got passed down the chain in 2025 before it ever reached a shelf — money that, in her case, mostly didn't make it back to the farm.

She's since enrolled in the federation's Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course, partly to formalize what she learned informally and partly, she said, so she'd stop having to reconstruct her father's reasoning from memory. If your own family creamery is somewhere in the middle of a conversation like this one, the Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded enough of these calls over the past year to be a useful first stop — not for the emotional part, but for the paperwork and program questions that tend to come with it.
