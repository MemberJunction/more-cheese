This week's spotlight isn't one member — it's a shape we keep seeing across a lot of member accounts, so we're telling it as a composite instead of naming any one creamery.

She started as an apprentice six years ago, the kind of hire who was supposed to stay a season and ended up staying because nobody else showed up who wanted the 4 a.m. shift as much as she did. For most of those six years, she was exactly what her title said: someone learning the make, filling in on the bottling line, taking the courses the owner told her to take. Nothing about her employment file looked like a succession plan.

## The shift nobody scheduled

What's changed is quieter than a formal handoff. Over the past year, she's started making the calls when the owner is out — to the distributor about a late pallet, to the inspector about a scheduling conflict, to a buyer asking about next month's wholesale case. None of that was written into any job description. It happened because someone needed to do it, she was the one there, and she turned out to be good at it.

That's the pattern we're hearing about from a growing number of members this winter: succession that doesn't look like a transition at all until you add up eighteen months of small decisions and realize the apprentice is functionally running half the business. It's a much less dramatic story than a formal buyout, and it's also, by a lot of accounts, a much more common one.

## What she'd tell you if you asked

If you talked to her — or to any of the several members whose situation resembles hers — she'd probably tell you the hardest part isn't the skills. It's that nobody sat her down and said "this is happening," so she's had to guess at her own standing more than once. Is she being trained to take over, or just trusted with more because she's reliable? Those are different situations, and the gap between them causes real friction when it isn't named out loud, on either side.

She'd also tell you the credential pathway helped more than she expected. She's most of the way through her Certified Cheese Professional coursework now, not because the owner asked her to get it, but because she wanted something on paper that was hers regardless of how the ownership question resolves. That's a smart hedge, and one we'd recommend to anyone in a similar spot: build your own credentials alongside whatever informal trust is being extended to you, so your standing doesn't depend entirely on one person's plans staying the same.

If your creamery has someone in this position — an employee who's become load-bearing without a title change to match — this is a good season to have the conversation directly rather than let the ambiguity sit. And if you're the one in her position, know that you're not alone in it; enough members have described something close to this story that we're going to keep an eye on it as a recurring thread through the year.

Members thinking through either side of this can reach the Membership & Outreach Committee, which keeps a growing list of members willing to talk through what an informal handoff looked like from where they sat.
