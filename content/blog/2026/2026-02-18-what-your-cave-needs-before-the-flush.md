February doesn't feel like a cave-planning month. The wheels on your racks right now were mostly made in the fall, the cold outside is doing a lot of the humidity work for you, and spring flush — the surge of milk that comes when cows and goats move onto new pasture — is still six to ten weeks away depending on your region. That gap is exactly why this is the month to do the maintenance you can't do once the cave is full.

The federal government's latest monthly livestock and dairy outlook, released this month, put milk production running a few points above last year's pace with output per animal continuing to climb even as herd size holds roughly flat. Read plainly, that points to a flush that's real but not the kind of overwhelming surge some of you dealt with in past years. That's useful context for rack-space planning, but it doesn't change the underlying cave work. A cave that's tight on humidity control or airflow in April will be tight whether the flush is a trickle or a flood.

## The three things worth checking now

Start with your humidity setpoint against what it's actually reading. Caves drift over a winter — a door seal degrades, a humidifier reservoir scales up, a sensor reads five points off from where it did in September. If you haven't calibrated against a second, independent hygrometer since fall, do it before you're relying on that reading to manage a room full of fresh, high-moisture wheels that can't tolerate being wrong by five percent either direction.

Next, airflow. Rind development depends on air moving evenly across every shelf, not just the ones near the fan. Members who've mapped their cave's dead zones — the corners where a rind consistently develops slower or faster than the rest of the batch — tend to do it in a slow month like this one, moving a thermometer shelf to shelf over a few days rather than guessing. It's tedious. It's also the difference between a rack of wheels that age uniformly and one where you're culling outliers every week in May.

Third: rack capacity math, done honestly. If last spring's flush left you improvising shelf space in a hallway, this is the month to decide whether that was a one-time squeeze or a sign you're structurally short on room. A cave addition or a second unit takes longer to plan and permit than it takes to build, so a February decision is what actually gets you ready by April.

Batch tracking belongs on this list too. Cave season and recordkeeping season are really the same season: if your batch records currently live in a notebook on a shelf near the door, spring flush is the worst possible time to discover that the notebook can't answer a question about a specific wheel fast enough. Building a cleaner intake-to-release tracking habit now, while the cave is quiet, means it's already routine by the time volume picks up.
