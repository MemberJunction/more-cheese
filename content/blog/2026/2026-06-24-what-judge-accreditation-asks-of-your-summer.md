Every year around this time, we get a wave of questions about Competition Judge Accreditation, and every year the answer surprises people a little: it is not something you pick up in the weeks before a competition. Members who sit at the judging table this year mostly started the credential last winter or earlier, and the ones scoring wheels at our flagship competition next month have been building toward it for longer than that.

The credential exists because judging a cheese competition well is a different skill from making cheese well, or even from selling it. A maker can have a superb palate for their own product and still struggle to score consistently across forty entries in an unfamiliar category, using a rubric that has to hold up whether the judge is evaluating a bloomy-rind triple crème or a two-year cheddar back to back. Accreditation is built to close that gap.

## What's actually in it

The coursework covers three things most people underestimate going in. First, calibration: learning to apply a scoresheet the same way a room full of other judges would, which is harder than it sounds and takes repeated practice against known reference wheels. Second, defect vocabulary — being able to name what is wrong with a paste or a rind precisely enough that a maker can act on the feedback, rather than a vague "something's off here." Third, and least glamorous, the paperwork and chain-of-custody side: how entries move through a competition, how ties get broken, and what a judge is and is not allowed to discuss with an entrant afterward.

That last piece matters more than new candidates expect. A judge who blind-tastes a wheel and later realizes who made it has to disclose that and, depending on the category, may need to be recused. The credential spends real time on this because it protects the integrity of every competition result the federation puts its name behind — including the categories that matter most to smaller producers competing against much larger operations for the same ribbon.

## Timing it against Madison

Accreditation review sessions for members hoping to judge at the Madison conference typically run through July. It's a pattern that shows up across the competition calendar generally this year: several of the larger international cheese competitions that opened entries this June report high entry volumes across most categories, which tracks with what our own Awards & Competition Committee is seeing for this year's slate. More entries means more judges are needed at the table, and more pressure on the calibration process that keeps scores consistent when the room is bigger than usual. If you started the credential earlier this year and are close to finishing, now is the window to confirm your standing with the Awards & Competition Committee before the judging roster locks. If you have not started and were hoping to judge this cycle, it is realistic to aim instead for next year's competition rather than compressing months of calibration work into a few weeks — the rubric consistency the credential builds does not compress well, and a rushed judge helps no one, including themselves.

For members who are further out, the credential is worth starting even if judging this year or next isn't the goal. Several members who hold it tell us the calibration training changed how they evaluate their own wheels before they ever enter one in competition — which is its own kind of return on the time. Details and the current cohort schedule are on the certifications page of the member portal.
