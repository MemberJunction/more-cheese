Next Tuesday's Listeria Control webinar is one we'd flag even in a quiet year, and this isn't a quiet year to be thinking about environmental monitoring. Small-format creameries are heading into a production ramp as spring milk volumes build, caves are getting opened and reorganized after a slow winter stretch, and every one of those changes is exactly the kind of disruption that gives Listeria monocytogenes a chance to move somewhere it hadn't been before. The webinar is timed on purpose to land before that ramp starts, not after.

If you weren't around for the last version of this session, the format is straightforward: ninety minutes, live Q&A at the end, and a walkthrough built for small teams who don't have a dedicated food safety hire running their environmental monitoring program full time. That's most of our membership. The content leans practical — swab site selection, how to read a positive result without either panicking or dismissing it, and what a corrective action actually needs to include before you're comfortable putting product back on the line.

## Why this keeps coming up

This week a European food-safety authority published new formal guidance on environmental monitoring for Listeria monocytogenes in ready-to-eat food operations — the kind of document regulators write when they want businesses to move from an informal swab habit to a documented program with a real schedule and a real response plan for a positive result. It's not a rule that reaches our members directly, but the shape of it is worth paying attention to: a written swabbing schedule, a defined response when something comes back positive, and a way to trend your results over time instead of treating each swab as its own isolated event. That's exactly the structure the webinar walks through.

That distinction matters more than the headline number of swabs. A facility that collects samples inconsistently, or only right before an audit, tends to learn less from a negative result than a facility running the same panel every week regardless of who's watching. Consistency is what turns environmental monitoring from a compliance exercise into an actual early-warning system, and it's the piece the webinar spends the most time on.

The Food Safety Committee built this session specifically for members running raw-milk or mixed-milk operations, where the aging environment itself is doing more of the safety work than pasteurization can. If your monitoring program is more informal than you'd want it to look on paper, this is a good week to fix that before, not after, an inspector or a buyer asks to see it.

## What to bring

Come with your current swab map if you have one — even a rough one sketched on paper counts. The presenters will walk through common blind spots (drains, wheeled equipment, anything with a gasket) using real layouts as examples, and it's easier to spot the gaps in your own space if you're looking at it while they talk. Registration is open now through the events page, and it's free for current members.

If your schedule doesn't allow a live Tuesday session, a recording goes up on the members' portal within a few days, though you'll miss the live Q&A where a lot of the specific, operation-by-operation questions tend to get answered. Either way, block the time this week rather than letting it slide into spring, when the ramp-up itself becomes the excuse not to get to it.
