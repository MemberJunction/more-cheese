For most of last year, the calls we got from members were about staying open. This year, a different kind of call has started showing up: what happens to the creamery after I'm not the one running it.

That shift is worth sitting with. Feed and energy costs, which squeezed margins hard through 2025, have settled into something closer to normal this winter. The 2025 all-milk price came in lower than 2024's, and the year closed with more culling than usual on some operations, but the emergency has passed. Members who spent the last twelve months in survival mode are finding a little room to think past the next invoice. For a lot of them, that room is going straight into a question they'd been avoiding: who takes this over.

## A generational bottleneck, not a new problem

This isn't a 2026 story so much as a story that finally has time to be told out loud. The founders who built the American artisan cheese boom of the 1980s and '90s are reaching retirement age now, and the handoff is rarely simple. A next generation that grew up in the business doesn't always have the capital to buy out a parent's equity. One that didn't grow up in it doesn't always want the life. Farmstead cheese production has been declining for years, and part of that decline is less about demand and more about nobody being positioned to keep the vat running when the person who started it steps back.

What's changed this winter is that members are actually naming this out loud on membership calls and at chapter meetups, instead of treating it as a private family matter. That's useful for us, because it's exactly the kind of transition the Membership & Outreach Committee can help members plan around — not with legal or financial advice, which isn't something we're in a position to give, but with connections to other members who've been through a handoff and a directory of who in the network has done it more than once.

## What we're actually seeing

A few patterns keep recurring in what members are telling us. Some creameries are splitting the handoff into pieces — the license and the recipes go to a former apprentice, while the land stays with the family. Others are bringing in a business partner who isn't a family member at all, just someone who wants to make cheese and has the capital the founder doesn't need anymore. A few are choosing, deliberately, to wind down rather than force a transfer that nobody in the family actually wants — and that's a legitimate outcome too, even if it's a harder one to talk about at a conference booth.

If your creamery is somewhere in this conversation, the most useful thing you can do this quarter is get your recordkeeping in order before you need it for a buyer, a bank, or a family member weighing in. Clean production records, a documented make procedure, and an up-to-date food safety plan turn a vague "the business is worth something" into something a successor — or a lender — can actually evaluate.

We don't have a course built specifically around succession planning yet, and we're not going to pretend one exists. What we do have is each other. If you're navigating a handoff, or you've already been through one and are willing to talk about it, reach out to the Membership & Outreach Committee. We're building a list of members willing to have that conversation with someone else who's just starting it.
