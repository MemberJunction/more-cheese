A cut-and-wrapped, imported cheese was recalled in several states this month because an egg-derived ingredient wasn't declared on its label. People with an egg allergy could have reacted to it. Nobody had made a bad cheese. The product simply carried something the label never mentioned, and the shop that wrapped it found out the hard way.

Members have been asking us since the news broke whether the same thing could happen to them. The honest answer is that it can happen to anyone who hasn't read their own ingredient list line by line lately. Here is how we'd approach it.

## Start with what goes into the vat

Most cheesemakers can recite the milk, culture, rennet and salt. The trouble tends to hide one step further out. A preservative added to a washed rind brine, a lysozyme used to control gas in a particular style, a flavoring in a rind wash, a coating, or a cultured seasoning in a flavored cheese may each come from an allergen source. Your supplier's spec sheet should say. If it doesn't, ask, and keep the reply in your file.

The federal framework treats nine foods as major allergens: milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. A declaration can appear in the ingredient list with the source in parentheses, or in a separate "Contains" statement. Tree nuts need the specific nut named. State dairy label guides repeat those basics and add their own requirements for packaged dairy products, so check the one that applies to your plant.

A small, wry aside: cheese is already a milk allergen product, and nobody forgets to say so. The ones that get missed are the quiet extras.

## Where labels slip

In our experience, and from what members have told us, label problems tend to come from four places.

- **A changed supplier.** The new culture or coating has a different carrier ingredient, and the label is never updated.
- **Cut-and-wrap labeling.** Store scale labels built from a product database can drift away from the actual pack, especially when a cheese arrives from overseas with a label in another language.
- **Flavored and washed styles.** A new rind wash or a seasonal herb coating adds an ingredient to one batch and not the next.
- **Shared equipment.** Allergen cross-contact is a separate question from an ingredient being present, and your plan needs to address both.

None of these is exotic. They're ordinary slips, which is why a short, regular check beats a big annual audit.

A twenty-minute check you can do this week: pull every current label you sell and put it beside the ingredient specs for that product. Cross off each ingredient on the spec and confirm it appears on the label, along with any allergen source. Do the same for anything in a brine, wash or coating. Then ask one question of every product: has anything changed since the label was last printed?

If you sell through a retailer who wraps and re-labels your wheels, confirm their label matches yours. It's a friendly phone call, and it's much cheaper than a recall.
