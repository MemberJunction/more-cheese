Every succession story we hear starts with a timeline that got shorter than planned. This one
belongs to a farmstead cheesemaker in her late twenties who runs a small goat operation her mother
started, and who expected to take over sometime in her thirties — not the spring her mother's back
finally gave out and the handoff happened in a matter of weeks instead of years.

"We'd talked about it in the abstract for a long time," she told us. "What we hadn't done was
write anything down. So when it actually happened, I was learning the vendor list and the food
safety plan and the payroll schedule all at once, while also trying to make sure the make schedule
didn't slip." Two years into stabilization-era conditions that finally stopped punishing
farmstead operations for existing, she describes the timing as lucky in one narrow sense: input
costs had already started to ease before the weight of running the place landed fully on her.

## What the paperwork didn't cover

The membership side, she says, turned out to be more useful than she expected going in. Her
mother had kept the federation membership current for over a decade, and the directory listing,
the course history, and the food safety documentation all transferred with the account rather than
needing to be rebuilt from scratch. What didn't transfer automatically was the informal knowledge —
which distributor rep actually calls back, which wholesale account pays late every single month,
which corner of the aging cave runs a degree warmer in July than the thermometer near the door
suggests.

She spent her first few months making a point of writing that knowledge down as she learned it,
specifically so it wouldn't live only in her head the way it had lived only in her mother's. "I
don't want the next person who has to do this in a hurry to be as unprepared as I was," she said.
That's become something close to a project for her: a working document, still growing, that
functions as the operational memory her mother never had time to put on paper.

Asked what she'd tell another member facing an accelerated handoff, she didn't hesitate: get the
food safety plan and the vendor relationships documented well before you think you'll need them,
not after. She's also become an advocate, in committee conversations and in casual ones, for
members treating succession as an ongoing plan rather than a single event — something closer to
what a well-run handoff actually looks like, but which is easy to defer when the person currently
running things is still healthy and the timeline still feels distant.

## Smaller now, on purpose

Her creamery is smaller now than it was under her mother — she made the deliberate choice to trim
two wholesale accounts in her first year rather than try to hold everything at once while still
learning the job. She considers that a success, not a retreat. "I'd rather run a smaller operation
well than run my mother's operation badly," she said. "The case gets to grow back once I actually
know what I'm doing."

If your own succession plan currently lives entirely in conversation rather than in writing, her
experience is a reasonable prompt to change that this year, while the timeline is still yours to
set.
