If you've ever thought about applying to judge at the ICF Annual Conference, this is the month
to start, not July. The conference itself lands in late summer, but the Competition Judge
Accreditation isn't something you pick up in a weekend right before entries close, and the
Judging & Competition Preparation course that most successful applicants take runs on a
schedule built around exactly this kind of lead time.

## What the credential actually checks

The Competition Judge Accreditation is not a test of whether you can tell a good wheel from a
mediocre one by taste alone. Most members who come to it already have that instinct from years
behind a counter or a cave. What it checks is whether you can turn that instinct into scoring
that another judge, working from the same rubric, would land close to. That means structured
practice with the categories the federation actually uses at competition, written justification
for a score rather than a gut reaction, and calibration exercises where your scores get compared
against a panel's before you're anywhere near a real flight of entries.

That calibration piece is the part people underestimate. Two experienced tasters can disagree
sharply on a single wheel and both be defensible, right up until a scoring rubric forces them to
name what they're actually weighing — rind character, paste texture, balance, a defect versus a
deliberate style choice. The accreditation exists because a competition with inconsistent judging
isn't much of a competition, and members who've put years into an entry deserve scores that mean
something.

## Why the course, and why now

The Judging & Competition Preparation course is where most candidates build that muscle before
they sit for accreditation. It walks through the same categories and rubrics the Awards &
Competition Committee uses, with practice flights and feedback from someone who has judged
before. Starting the course in March instead of June matters for a plain scheduling reason: the
accreditation review itself takes several weeks, and the committee wants judges confirmed well
before entries for this year's competition start arriving, not scrambling to fill a panel the
week of the conference.

It also matters for a less obvious reason. Judging is a skill that degrades if you don't practice
it regularly, the same way a make sheet gets sloppy if you stop checking it against your notes.
Members who've held the accreditation for a few years still tell us the course refreshers before
a competition year sharpen their scoring, not just their memory of the rubric.

You don't need to be chasing a stage career in competition judging to make this worth doing. Some
members pursue the accreditation specifically to bring a sharper, more structured eye back to
their own cave or their own entries the following year. Others do it because a shop or creamery
benefits from having someone on staff who can speak the same language as a judging panel when a
customer asks why one wheel scored better than another.

If you're weighing whether this is the year to start, the Awards & Competition Committee can tell
you what the current judge roster looks like by category and where the gaps are. Course
enrollment for the spring cohort of Judging & Competition Preparation is open now, well ahead of
this summer's conference.
