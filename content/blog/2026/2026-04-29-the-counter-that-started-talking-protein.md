For most of the fifteen years she's worked a specialty cheese counter, this member built her pitch around origin and process — where the milk came from, how long the wheel aged, what made it different from the block cheddar three aisles over. This spring she noticed the pitch wasn't landing the way it used to, at least not with the customers showing up most often. "A younger customer will pick up a wedge and ask me how much protein is in it before they ask me anything about the cave it came out of," she said. "I had to catch up fast or lose that conversation entirely."

She's not imagining the shift. A trend report published this spring found that Gen Z has the lowest cheese purchase rate of any generation, and that when younger shoppers do buy, they want something different from what's always sold well with older customers — protein content and convenient formats leading the list, ahead of the origin story that's carried her counter for years. Dairy analysts tracking the broader protein trend heading into this year noted it had gone from a niche interest among athletes to something that's "saturated pretty much every type of consumer, every age group," which matches what she's seeing at her own case even among shoppers who aren't especially young.

## Same case, different opening line

She didn't change what she stocks. What changed is the first thing she says. A firm, aged sheep's milk cheese that used to get introduced by region now gets introduced by texture and protein density first, with the origin story following once the customer's already interested. "I'm not hiding the story," she said. "I'm just not leading with it for everyone anymore. Some customers want the history first. A lot of my younger regulars want to know what it's going to do for them, and then they'll ask where it's from."

She's also started keeping a short mental list of which wheels in her case hold up best to that pitch — generally the firmer, aged styles with less moisture and a denser bite — and steering new customers there first rather than starting them on a soft-ripened cheese that doesn't fit the conversation they came in for. It's a small adjustment, but she says it's changed how many first-time visitors turn into repeat customers, and it hasn't cost her anything with the regulars who still want the full story.

## What she'd tell another counter

Her advice to other members running a retail case is blunt: don't assume the customer walking up already speaks your language. "I used to think explaining terroir was the whole job. Now I think the whole job is figuring out what someone actually wants to know, and terroir is just one of the answers I have ready." She still loves talking provenance with the customers who want it — that hasn't gone anywhere. She's just stopped assuming everyone wants to start there, and she's noticed her newer staff pick up the habit faster when she frames it that way instead of as a script to memorize.
