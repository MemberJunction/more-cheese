Every winter we get a run of questions about Certified Cheesemonger, usually from members whose counters are staffing up for spring and want to know what the credential is actually worth to a shop. It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that it tests something narrower and more useful than people assume.

Certified Cheesemonger is not a certificate that says you know cheese in general — that's closer to what the Cheese Foundations Certificate covers. It's built around the specific job of standing at a case and helping someone choose what to buy: identifying a wheel by rind and paste when the label is gone, describing flavor and texture in language a customer will actually understand, pairing and portioning correctly, and handling cheese in a way that doesn't shorten its life between delivery and sale. If your job is selling cheese rather than making or aging it, this is the credential built for you.

## What the exam actually checks

The written portion covers cheese families, production methods at a level a monger needs — not a cheesemaker's depth, but enough to answer a customer's question honestly — and safe handling and storage. The practical portion is where most candidates say the real test happens: identifying unlabeled samples, cutting and wrapping to spec, and walking a mock customer interaction from question to sale. Members who've been through it describe the practical exam as harder than they expected, mostly because it rewards speed and confidence as much as knowledge. Knowing the answer and being able to say it clearly while your hands are still working the wheel are different skills.

This is also the credential we hear the most about from specialty retail buyers, who've told committee volunteers that a Certified Cheesemonger on staff changes how they trust a shop's recommendations. With specialty cheese sales continuing to outpace the broader dairy case, according to the trade reporting we follow, that trust at the counter matters more, not less, this year. Federal forecasters have also been raising their production and price outlooks for the year, which if it holds means shops will likely be moving more cheese, not less, over the coming months — a monger who can talk a customer through a wider case with confidence is worth more to a shop in that kind of year, not less.

None of that is graded directly on the exam, but it's the reason the credential exists. A counter staffed by people who can only ring up a sale isn't building the kind of repeat customer a specialty shop depends on. A counter staffed by people who can explain why a price moved, or why one wheel from a familiar producer tastes different from last month's batch, usually is.

## Who should — and shouldn't — sign up now

If you've worked a cheese counter for at least a season and want something that formalizes what you already do, this is a reasonable spring goal; the practical portion rewards counter time you've already logged. If you're newer to the trade, or your work is mostly production rather than sales, start with the Cheese Foundations Certificate instead — Cheesemonger assumes a baseline of product knowledge it doesn't teach from scratch.

Course materials and the practical exam schedule are available through the member portal. If you're not sure which credential fits your role, the Education Committee fields exactly this question year-round and would rather point you to the right one now than have you retake the wrong one later.
