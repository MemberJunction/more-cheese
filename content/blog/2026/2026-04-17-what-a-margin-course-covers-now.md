Members keep telling us the same thing this spring: the milk market finally looks less brutal
than it did last year, and cheese barrel and block prices on the wholesale exchange have been
firming rather than falling. It's tempting to treat that as the whole story. It isn't. A calmer
input market changes one line in your cost structure. It doesn't touch packaging, labor, the price
of the propane running your cave, or the wholesale account that's been paying net-60 since 2023.
That gap between "inputs eased" and "margins improved" is exactly what our Pricing & Margin for
Specialty Cheese course spends most of its time on.

## Why cost of goods isn't one number

The course starts by pulling apart cost of goods sold into pieces members often lump together —
raw milk and cultures on one side, everything from mold-ripening supplies to whey disposal on the
other, and labor and overhead treated separately from both. Aged cheeses and fresh cheeses carry
genuinely different cost structures: an aged wheel ties up cave space and time that a fresh
mozzarella never does, which means the two products need different pricing logic even when
they're sitting in the same case. Members who've only ever priced by "what similar cheeses cost at
the shop down the road" often discover in this course that their aged product has been
underpriced for years relative to what it actually costs to make.

That distinction matters more this year than it did during the worst of the feed and energy
squeeze, when most members were simply trying to survive input costs rather than optimize around
them. With some breathing room back and wholesale prices firming, it's a reasonable moment to
actually rebuild a pricing model instead of running the same numbers you set two years ago under
duress. The course also covers how to talk to a distributor or retail buyer about a price increase
without it reading as opportunism — framing matters here, and the course includes example
language members have used successfully, tied to specific cost drivers rather than "prices are
going up."

## Who this is for

This course tends to be most useful for members who've been pricing by instinct or by matching a
competitor, rather than from their own cost structure. If you've never actually calculated your
cost per pound including labor and overhead, not just raw ingredients, that's the gap this course
is built to close. It also pairs well for anyone newly running pricing decisions for the first
time, including several members who took on that responsibility earlier than planned after a
recent ownership transition.

National forecasts for cheese and milk prices were revised upward again this spring, which is a
reasonable tailwind but not a guarantee — a forecast is an average across the whole country, not a
promise about your specific wholesale account or your specific cave. The course is built around
that distinction: members leave with their own numbers, not a national average dressed up as
personal advice.

The course runs as part of the federation's regular course catalog; check the current schedule for
the next cohort, and reach out to the Education Committee if you have questions about whether it's
the right fit before you enroll.
