Today's in-person workshop is a small one by design. Where our flagship conference draws hundreds of members into a convention hall, this session caps registration at a couple dozen so that everyone gets real time with a full flight of samples and enough one-on-one feedback to actually change how they taste. If you signed up, you already know the format: less lecture, more nose-to-glass practice, with an instructor circulating the room rather than standing at the front of it.

The workshop grew out of a pattern our Education Committee kept hearing from members who'd already taken the Advanced Sensory Evaluation course: the classroom version teaches the vocabulary and the scoring framework, but nothing substitutes for tasting the same defect in six different wheels back to back until your palate actually locks onto it. This session is built entirely around that repetition — a set of samples chosen specifically to surface the differences between a rind that's developing correctly and one that's a week away from a problem.

## Why this matters beyond the workshop room

Sensory judgment isn't just a competition skill, even though it's often taught alongside competition prep. It's also the first line of quality control in a lot of small operations, where the person tasting the wheel before it ships is the same person who made it. A cheesemaker who can name what they're smelling — ammonia developing too early, a bitterness that signals an underripe curd, the specific mustiness of a cave that's running too humid — catches problems before a customer does, and catches them earlier than a lab test would.

That's also why sensory training keeps showing up on hands-on educational schedules across the industry this cycle, alongside grading and evaluation sessions aimed at working cheesemakers rather than judges alone. The demand makes sense: as buyers keep chasing more distinctive, more international flavor profiles, the margin for a rind that's slightly off gets thinner, not wider. A cheesemaker who can name the difference between "developing correctly" and "a week from a problem" is the one whose flagship wheel still tastes like itself in a market that keeps moving.

None of this replaces documented food-safety practice, and it isn't meant to. A wheel that smells and tastes correct can still carry a risk that only testing catches, and a wheel with a genuine defect is not something any amount of confidence should talk you into shipping. If your operation works with raw milk, our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate and the Listeria Control in Small Creameries course are the places to go for the recordkeeping and testing side; this workshop builds the palate that tells you when something's worth testing in the first place.

## If you couldn't make it this time

We run sensory-focused sessions like this a few times a year, and the Education Committee plans the next cohort's schedule based partly on how full this one runs. If today's session is booked and you're reading this from the waitlist, the Advanced Sensory Evaluation course itself is available on its regular cohort schedule and covers the same core framework, just with fewer hands-on repetitions per person.

Either way, if you're aging anything longer than a few weeks, put a standing fifteen minutes on your own calendar to taste through your current lineup with the vocabulary this workshop teaches. It's a habit, not a one-time skill, and the members who keep it up are consistently the ones whose competition entries improve year over year.
