The federal milk production forecast moved again this month, and it moved the direction most members expected after the fall we all watched: up. A larger national herd and steady per-cow output pushed the government's production estimate higher for the second straight update, and the price outlook for cheese and butter came down slightly to match. Nonfat dry milk and whey held firmer, which tells you where the demand is actually pulling right now — protein, not fat.

None of that is news to anyone who has been checking a milk check since October. Base pricing opened January below where it closed December, continuing a run that has now stretched across three straight order announcements. For members who spent 2025 managing feed and energy costs that wouldn't quit, a softer milk price sounds like one more thing landing on the wrong side of the ledger. It isn't quite that simple, and it's worth walking through why.

## Supply growing faster than the case

A bigger production number is not, on its own, bad news for a cheesemaker. It is bad news for a milk seller with nowhere else to put the gallon. If you're buying milk to make cheese, more of it on the market generally means steadier input costs and a buyer's market for the raw material — assuming your own supply contract lets you take advantage of it. If you're a farmstead producer milking your own herd, the calculus flips: your milk check softens at the same time your case still has to carry the difference.

That split is exactly why we keep telling members not to read one headline number as their own number. A regional co-op's blend price, a local sale barn's cull-cow numbers, a specific plant's component premiums — all of those move independently of the national average, sometimes by a wide margin. The federation's economics briefing, distributed to committee volunteers each quarter, breaks the production and pricing data down by region for exactly this reason. If you haven't seen the January edition, ask your state committee contact.

## Where the relief shows up, and where it doesn't

The softer cheese and butter forecast is a modest tailwind if you're buying commodity block for a blended product line. It's closer to irrelevant if your case is built on aged, artisan, or named-region product, where your price is set by your own cost structure and reputation rather than a national settlement. Several members who make that point to us every winter are right to keep making it — but it's also worth checking your make sheet against current input costs at least once a quarter regardless of which case you run, because feed and packaging prices don't always move in step with the milk number that gets the headline. Cold storage holdings gave a second, quieter signal worth watching too: total natural cheese in refrigerated warehouses is running close to flat on the year, with at least one major category down month over month — a market working through inventory rather than piling it up, generally a healthier setup heading into spring than the alternative.

If your milk is purchased, this is a reasonable window to lock in a spring contract rather than ride the spot market, and it's worth a conversation with your buyer this month rather than in March when everyone else has the same idea. If your milk is your own, treat the softer check as a prompt to revisit your case pricing now, not in the middle of a summer rush when you have less time to think it through. Either way, the federation's Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through building a cost model that holds up regardless of which way the national number moves next quarter — a useful hour if you haven't run your numbers since the fall.
