For most of last year, the story members told us was about scarcity: not enough milk, not
enough margin, a cave that had to make do with a smaller vat than usual. This spring the story
has quietly shifted. There's more milk moving through the system than there was a year ago, and a
larger share of it is heading somewhere new — into cheese vats and high-protein products, rather
than the powder dryers that used to soak up the surplus.

Nonfat dry milk touched its highest spot price in more than a decade this spring, which sounds
like it should mean tight supply everywhere. It's actually the opposite kind of tight: dryer
capacity is sitting underused because processors are pulling skim solids toward the products
consumers want more of right now, particularly high-protein items. That pull is part of why
national forecasts for both cheese and nonfat dry milk prices were revised upward again this
spring, on top of an already-larger milk production forecast for the year. For a federation built
around cheesemakers rather than powder plants, that's milk moving toward you, not away from you.

## What more milk in the vat actually changes

More milk available to cheese production doesn't automatically mean easier margins for a small or
mid-sized operation. If anything, members tell us it changes the competitive picture more than
the cost picture. A quality manager at a mid-sized creamery in the upper Midwest put it plainly on
a recent committee call: "We're not short on milk. We're short on shelf space that isn't already
somebody else's private label." When larger processors have more milk to work with, they tend to
run more volume through established retail lines, which can squeeze the smaller wholesale accounts
independent creameries depend on.

The flip side is that cheese demand itself is real and growing, not just a redirection of supply.
Members selling direct, through a farmstand or a specialty counter, are generally better
positioned to benefit from strong underlying demand than members competing purely on wholesale
volume against bigger operations. If your case has been leaning heavily on one or two wholesale
accounts, this is a reasonable spring to ask whether a farmers market slot or a direct-to-retailer
relationship would spread that risk out.

## Firmer forecasts aren't the same as firmer margins

Upward revisions to national cheese and milk price forecasts are real relief on the input side for
members who buy milk rather than produce it themselves, and they matter for farmstead operations
managing their own herds too. But firmer prices at the milk level don't automatically translate
into firmer margins at the cheese counter, since packaging, transport, and labor costs haven't
retreated at the same pace they climbed over the last two years.

Members working through their own numbers this spring, rather than assuming last year's cost
pressure has simply reversed itself, are the ones best positioned to take advantage of an easing
input market instead of just riding it. If margin math isn't something your operation tracks
closely today, our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly this kind of
recalculation — worth a look before the summer selling season, when this year's numbers will
matter more than last year's habits.

The market is giving cheesemakers more room this spring than it has in a while. Whether that room
turns into better margins depends less on the milk supply and more on what each operation does
with the next few months.
