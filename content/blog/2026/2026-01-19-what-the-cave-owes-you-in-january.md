Every affineur has a January story, and most of them start the same way: the thermometer outside said the cold snap was over, and the hygrometer inside said otherwise. Cave management doesn't take a break just because the worst of the cold has passed. If anything, the stretch from mid-January through early February is when a lot of small caves lose ground they don't get back until spring.

The mechanics are simple enough to explain and stubborn enough to keep catching people. Outdoor temperature swings change how hard your climate system has to work, and every degree of extra effort tends to pull moisture out of the air along with it. A cave that sat comfortably at 90 percent relative humidity in December can drift into the low 80s in January without a single setting changed on the control panel, because the system is compensating for outdoor conditions that shifted underneath it. Rinds that were developing evenly start to dry unevenly. Natural rind wheels crack at the equator before anyone notices the readout has moved.

Feed and energy costs have eased some from where they sat through most of last year, and national milk production is running ahead of last January's pace, which has taken some of the financial pressure off small producers heading into this season. That's real relief, and worth sitting with for a second. But easier margins don't fix a cave that's fighting its own equipment. If your humidity logs from the past two winters show a January dip, this is the year to get ahead of it rather than chase it in March when the damage is already in the wheel.

## What actually helps

Start with your logs, not your instincts. A cave that "feels dry" three weeks after the fact tells you less than a daily hygrometer reading would have. Members who've gotten this right generally do three things: they check calibration on their humidity sensors at the start of the season rather than the end, they separate their airflow adjustments from their humidity adjustments instead of treating one control as a proxy for the other, and they build in a buffer — running slightly higher than target in early January — so a cold snap doesn't push them under the floor.

If your setup runs on passive humidification, January is also when a lot of members discover their brine pans or wet floors need refilling more often than the rest of the year, simply because dry outside air pulls moisture through the room faster. It's not glamorous work, but it's the difference between a wheel that ages evenly and one that needs to be reworked into a blend.
