A farmstead cheesemaker in the Appalachian foothills spent the last week of August doing something she describes as embarrassing in hindsight: rewriting her entire environmental swab log from scratch. She'd inherited the original version from the person who ran the cave before her, updated it here and there over the years, and never actually sat down to ask whether it still matched how her space was laid out. Then she sat in on this week's Listeria Control webinar and realized her schedule tested three spots that hadn't existed in her current facility for two years, and missed a condensate line that had.

"I'd been testing the ghost of my old cave," she told us. "The log looked thorough. It just wasn't testing the room I actually have." She counts herself lucky nothing came back positive in the meantime — she has no evidence anything was ever wrong — but she also isn't willing to call that luck a food safety program.

## Building it back one spot at a time

She spent three evenings walking her own space with a notepad, tracing where moisture actually collects rather than where she assumed it would. Drains, obviously. But also a shelf edge where a small aging-room dehumidifier drips during humid stretches, and a floor seam near the wash sink that never quite dries between shifts. None of it was dramatic. All of it was the kind of thing that's easy to miss when you're testing from memory instead of testing from a current walk-through.

"The webinar's point was that the room changes and your log doesn't always change with it," she said. "My cave's been through two renovations since I wrote that original schedule. Of course it needed a rewrite." She's since built a habit of walking the space with the log in hand every quarter, not just when something prompts her to, and she's started keeping photos of each swab point so a future hire — or a future version of her, five years from now — doesn't have to guess what "under the second rack" meant.

This is the kind of quiet correction that rarely makes it into a food safety inspection story, because nothing went wrong. That's sort of the point. Most of the members we hear from after a close call are talking about the thing that already happened. She's an example of the less dramatic and more useful habit: catching the gap before it costs you anything, because a webinar made you actually look.

If your own testing schedule was written more than a couple of years ago, or written by someone who isn't the one walking your space today, it might be worth the same three evenings she spent. The Food Safety Committee can point you toward the Listeria Control in Small Creameries course if you want a structured way to work through it rather than starting from a blank notepad.

She's also started something smaller that other members might steal outright: a one-page map of her cave and wash-up area with every swab point numbered, pinned inside the door where anyone on staff can see it. It sounds almost too simple to mention, but she says it changed how her one part-time employee thinks about the space — testing stopped being "the owner's clipboard thing" and started being something the whole crew could see and understand. When she walked a new hire through the map last week, the hire asked why a shelf edge two feet from the wash sink was on the list at all. That question, she said, was exactly the kind of thing she wanted someone other than herself to start noticing.

Her plan now is to bring the map to the next regional gathering of nearby producers, informally, to see whether anyone else's cave has a blind spot they've stopped seeing. "You get used to your own room," she told us. "Sometimes you need someone else's eyes on it before you notice what you walked past for two years." That's not a formal ICF program — just one member's idea, offered to whoever wants to borrow it.
