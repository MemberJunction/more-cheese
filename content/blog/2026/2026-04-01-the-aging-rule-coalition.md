Every January, the federal food-safety regulator publishes its priority list for the year, and most years our members skim it and move on. This year's list includes a line item that matters directly to a meaningful share of the roster: an examination of cheese aging practices, sitting alongside dairy safety training and food traceability work as one of the program's stated deliverables for 2026. It is not a proposed rule. It is a stated intention to look, which is an earlier and quieter stage than most members are used to tracking.

Our Food Safety Committee flagged the line item in February and scheduled a coalition meeting to talk through what an examination like this could mean before any actual rule text exists. We expected a small room. We got a full one.

## What "examination" does and doesn't mean

The current standard is familiar to anyone who's built a cheese business around it: a minimum 60-day aging period for cheese made from unpasteurized milk, unchanged for decades and applied the same way regardless of moisture content, salt level, or the specific pathogen risk a given style actually carries. Members who make hard, long-aged raw-milk cheeses have argued for years that a single flat number treats a nine-month wheel the same as one that just cleared the minimum, when the underlying chemistry isn't the same at all. Members who don't touch raw milk at all mostly haven't had a reason to follow the debate closely.

"Examination" in a priority-deliverables document means the regulator intends to gather information and possibly revisit its criteria. It does not mean a new rule is coming this year, it does not mean the 60-day floor is going away, and it does not mean the standard is getting stricter either. Both directions have been floated in past federal reviews of this same question, sometimes in the same document. The honest answer, which is the one our committee gave the room, is that nobody outside the agency knows yet which way this goes, or whether it goes anywhere at all this cycle.

## Why the room filled up anyway

What drew members to the meeting wasn't certainty about the outcome, it was the chance to get organized before there's a public comment period to respond to, rather than scrambling to write one in sixty days like the last cycle. A member who's been through a prior comment period put it plainly at the meeting: the federation's collective voice only carries weight if it's assembled ahead of the deadline, not after the draft language has already been decided.

If you make or age raw-milk cheese and want to be part of whatever comment the federation eventually submits, the Food Safety Committee is keeping a list of members who want direct notice the moment an actual proposal appears, rather than a general newsletter mention. If you're newer to this particular fight, our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate covers the reasoning behind the current standard in enough depth that the debate will make more sense the next time it surfaces, which, based on this January's list, may be sooner than usual.
