Several members reached out this month asking a version of the same question: why did the surveillance testing that used to happen at their farm move to their plant instead? The short answer is that regulators in a handful of states have shifted their approach to monitoring avian influenza in dairy supplies, moving from testing individual herds to sampling milk in bulk at the silo, where it's already being pooled from multiple farms before processing. If your milk goes through a Grade A receiving plant, that plant may now be collecting samples on a rolling basis rather than your farm being visited directly.

For a raw-milk cheesemaker, this distinction is not just bureaucratic. Silo-level sampling catches a positive result faster across a wider footprint, but it also means a single flagged load can implicate milk from several unrelated farms at once while the source gets traced back. We've heard from one member whose creamery had two days of production held pending clearance, even though her own herd tested clean, simply because her milk had been pooled with a supplier under investigation. That is a real business cost, and it's one the federation has raised with the Food Safety Committee as something worth flagging to state agriculture offices: pooled testing needs a faster trace-back process, not just a faster test.

## What this changes for you

If you're a farmstead operation that bottles or processes only your own milk, this shift likely changes very little — you were probably already being monitored directly, and that continues. If you buy milk from other producers, or if your milk is co-mingled at any point before it reaches your vat, it's worth asking your supplier or distributor directly whether their receiving plant has moved to this pooled sampling approach, and what their notification process looks like if a sample comes back positive. Don't assume; ask.

Some regions previously under full quarantine restrictions have also begun releasing affected operations back into normal commerce, provided they agree to ongoing monthly testing routed through their processing plant. That's a meaningful step toward normal, but "released from quarantine" is not the same as "off the watch list" — those creameries remain on a surveillance roster, and members buying from a released region should expect that paper trail to be part of the transaction going forward, not a one-time footnote.

## Where we've weighed in

None of this changes the aging and pasteurization requirements our members already work under, and we want to be plain about that: this is a monitoring and recordkeeping question, not a change to how cheese is made or labeled. Our position, filed as public comment with the relevant food-safety docket, has been that any pooled-sampling program needs a published trace-back timeline members can plan around — not an open-ended hold. If you've had a hold experience like the one described above, the Food Safety Committee wants to hear about it; specific timelines are what make that kind of comment persuasive rather than anecdotal.

If you handle raw milk in any part of your operation, the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate covers how to build a monitoring plan that anticipates exactly this kind of supply-chain question before it becomes a production disruption.
