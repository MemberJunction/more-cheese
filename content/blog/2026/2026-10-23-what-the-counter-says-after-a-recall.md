The recall notice goes to the owner. The first question goes to whoever is standing at the counter.

That became clear again last week, when a raw-milk cheddar made at a small farmstead in the Northeast was recalled after a lab found E. coli in a retail sample. It was sold at grocers in two states. As of the notice, no illnesses had been reported, and customers were told not to eat the cheese and to either discard it or return it for a refund. The package was identified by a lot code and a best-by date.

We are not going to comment on the producer or speculate about what happened. That belongs to the regulators. What we want to talk about is the other end of the chain: the person with a cheese iron and a line of customers.

## The ninety seconds that matter

A shopper holding last week's receipt has a simple question. "Is the cheese I bought one of those?" Your answer needs to be quick, accurate and calm, and it needs to avoid three traps.

**Do not guess.** If you cannot read a lot code off a package, say so and check the notice. Do not tell anyone a cheese is fine because it looks fine. Cheese that carries a pathogen does not look any different.

**Do not give medical advice.** If a customer says they ate the product and feel unwell, the notice already tells them what to do: seek medical care and mention the possible exposure. Repeat that and stop.

**Do not editorialize about the maker.** You do not know the facts, and the customer on the other side of the counter may be a cousin of the maker.

One of our members, a shop owner who has run a counter for more than a decade, told us what her team does now. "We keep a laminated card at the register," she said. "Product name, lot code, best-by, what the notice says to do. Whoever is on shift can read it out in thirty seconds. Before we had the card, every recall turned into a scramble."

## Where the credential comes in

The Certified Cheesemonger credential is, among other things, a statement that its holder can run a counter responsibly. That includes knowing your product and your sources, handling and storing it properly, and speaking about it accurately. A recall tests all three at once.

We would be overselling it if we said the credential is a recall script. It is not. But the habits it builds are the ones that keep a counter calm: tracing a wheel back to its supplier, keeping lot information next to the product rather than in a binder in the back, and knowing the difference between what you know and what you only assume.

If your counter handles raw-milk or other higher-risk cheeses, the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate goes deeper on hazards and controls, and our course *Listeria Control in Small Creameries* covers the production side that your suppliers deal with every day. Ask your suppliers whether they take it.

Here is a drill for this week. Run it at your next team huddle. It takes ten minutes.

1. Pick one cheese from your case. Pull the package and read the lot code and date out loud.
2. Ask: if this were recalled today, which of our products came from the same lot? Where would you look?
3. Ask: who removes it from the case, who tells the other location, and who answers the phone?
4. Write the answer to a customer who asks "Is mine affected?" in one sentence. Practice it aloud, with someone pretending to be impatient.
5. Check that your receipts or your orders can link a customer back to a purchase date, if you want to offer that at all.

This is also the week to find out what your suppliers send you when a recall touches them. Some send a clear notice with lot codes. Some send a note. You want to know which before you need it.

One more thing. A draft petition circulating this month would ask the federal food regulator to require a warning on raw-milk cheese, and its authors want feedback from counters as well as makers, including on whether a placard would work. We have no position on it yet, but your view of how a placard would hold up at a busy counter is exactly the kind of experience they are asking for.

If you are weighing the Certified Cheesemonger credential, the Education Committee can point you to the right pathway and the next cohort. We hope you will use it before the holiday rush brings the lines back.
