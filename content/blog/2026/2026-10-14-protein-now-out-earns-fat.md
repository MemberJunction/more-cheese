For about two decades, American dairy herds were bred, fed and paid to make more butterfat. It worked. A recent trade analysis puts U.S. butterfat up roughly 13 percent over ten years, while the European Union and New Zealand barely moved. Protein, the component a cheese vat actually cares about, did not keep pace.

This week the question for our members is a quiet one: who is paid for what, and does it match what you need in your vat?

## The numbers, with caution

The analysis we first read in January, and went back to this week, estimates that the protein premium in a typical federal milk check overtook the butterfat premium for the first time this year. Its table shows butterfat earning roughly $3.42 per hundredweight in 2022 and an estimated $2.10 this year, while protein rose from $1.86 to about $2.42 over the same stretch. The total milk check in that scenario falls.

Treat those as one analyst's scenario, not as a price list. We have not checked them against a federal price announcement, and your own check will differ by region and by how your milk is bought.

The direction is still worth noting. The national protein-to-fat ratio has slid from about 0.82 or higher in the 2000s to roughly 0.77. Processors who want cheese, and whey too, find that a low ratio costs them. Adding protein or removing fat from the milk is routine, but it is not free, and the cost eventually moves back to the farm.

**What a cheesemaker sees.** Yield in a cheese vat is sensitive to the balance between protein and fat. The same analysis cited industry guidance that milk with a little over 3 percent true protein, butterfat in the upper threes and a ratio near 0.80 is the easiest to process. It also described Upper Midwest herds with slightly less fat and more protein that out-earned high-fat, low-protein herds on yield and component value.

A farmstead maker in the upper Midwest said to us: "I always bragged about my rich milk. Then I saw my yield per vat sit still and finally looked at the protein number."

That is the member lesson. A rich cream line makes a lovely story and does not always make more cheese.

**The cash market, briefly.** Cash cheese trading has been choppy. A third-party summary of Friday, October 9 put 40-pound blocks near $1.39 per pound and barrels near $1.45, with blocks up about three cents on the day. We mention it only to say the cheese price is not doing the work for you. If block and barrel prices stay this unsettled, the part of your milk check you can influence is the component mix.

## What to do

- **Ask your supplier or your processor how components are paid.** If protein is rewarded, find out whether the schedule is changing.
- **Look at your make sheets.** Log fat, protein and yield for the same ten batches and see whether yield tracks protein more closely than fat.
- **If you milk your own herd,** talk with your herd nutritionist about protein before you change a ration. Any feed change deserves a conversation with someone who knows your animals.
- **Remember the margin program.** Enrollment for next year's federal margin coverage is open through December 18, and component income is one input to the margin you would be protecting. Monday's post walks through the choice.
- **Do not chase a ratio blindly.** A ratio that is too high carries its own problems, and the analysis flagged those too.

The **Cheese Chemistry** course covers how protein, fat and moisture set yield and texture, and **Milk Quality & Herd Health** covers the farm side of the same question. Both are good fall reading if your cave is full and your vat schedule is light.

If your numbers surprise you, tell the Standards Committee. We would like to hear from makers whose yield moved in the other direction, because a trade analysis is only as good as the herds behind it.
