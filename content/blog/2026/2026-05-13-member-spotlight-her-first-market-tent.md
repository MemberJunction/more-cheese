For her first two seasons in business, a member of ours who runs a small cow's-milk operation sold entirely wholesale — a handful of shop accounts, one distributor, no direct contact with the person who actually ate the cheese. This month she's setting up a tent at her regional farmers market for the first time, and she told us the preparation has looked almost nothing like she expected.

"I thought the hard part would be the cheese," she said. "The cheese was the easy part. I already knew how to make what I'm bringing. What I didn't know was how to run a table."

## The parts nobody warned her about

Pricing turned out to be the first surprise. Wholesale pricing is a negotiation with one buyer who already knows the category; market pricing is a decision she has to defend, politely, dozens of times a day to people who might be comparing her wheel to a supermarket block. She's settled on offering small tasting portions cut ahead of time, both because cutting to order slows the line and because a taste does more to justify her price than any sign could — general market-vendor advice we've seen this year makes the same point, and it held up for her.

The second surprise was logistics that have nothing to do with cheese at all — a cooler that holds temperature for a full market day, a card reader that works without reliable signal, a folding table sturdy enough not to wobble every time she sets a wheel down. None of that showed up in her original business plan, which was built almost entirely around production capacity and wholesale accounts. She's since learned, the way most first-time vendors do, that a market day is as much an events-logistics problem as a food one.

## What she's bringing instead of a full case

She's not bringing her whole catalog. On her supplier's advice, she's starting with three cheeses — one approachable soft style for first-time tasters, one aged style for repeat customers who want something to talk about, and one seasonal special she'll rotate through the summer to give regulars a reason to keep coming back. A focused table that a shopper can understand in ten seconds outperforms a sprawling one that takes explaining, and she'd rather build that reputation slowly than overwhelm her first customers with choices.

She's also treating the first few weeks as research rather than revenue. "I'm watching what people actually pick up versus what they say they like," she said. "Those aren't always the same cheese." She plans to use those weeks to figure out which style earns a permanent spot at the table before she commits more of her limited production to any one of them, and she's keeping a simple notebook at the table so the observations don't just live in her memory.

Talking to her, we kept coming back to one theme: how much of running a table is just being present. "You have to make eye contact, you have to be willing to explain the same thing for the fortieth time like it's the first," she said. "I didn't think that part would be tiring, but by hour five it is." She's planning her schedule this summer around that reality, building in a break rather than staffing the tent alone for a full market day.

If you're weighing a similar move from wholesale-only into direct sales, the federation's Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course covers the table-logistics side most new vendors underestimate — the cooler, the card reader, the signage — so your first market day is about meeting customers, not troubleshooting equipment.
