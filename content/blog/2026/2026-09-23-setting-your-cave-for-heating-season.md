The autumnal equinox falls this week, and anyone who runs an aging room knows what comes with it. Summer's problem was heat. Winter's problem is dry air and a heater that cycles on and off. In between there is a stretch of a few weeks where your room can do both in a single day, and the rinds notice.

This is a practical checklist for that stretch. It is not a substitute for your own logs, which are the most useful document you own. It is a prompt to go read them.

**Start with what your room actually does.** Dry air slows rind flora and the aging itself, and humidity follows temperature and dew point, so a heater that changes one changes the other. A survey of small aging-space builders found that underground rooms often swing by as much as 15 degrees Fahrenheit over a year, and that many owners ended up adding conventional refrigeration because soil temperature alone could not hold the room steady. A cave is not a constant. If yours is a converted cooler, a basement or a shipping container, expect it to drift when the weather does.

"I thought my room was stable because the number on the wall hadn't moved in July," a washed-rind maker in the Hudson Valley told us. "Then I put a second logger on a top shelf and found it ran six degrees warmer than the wall sensor. I'd been trusting the wrong spot for three years." She now keeps one sensor at cheese height on a middle shelf and one near the door, and compares them weekly.

That story is worth stealing. Before the heating season starts, move a logger to where the wheels are, not where the wall is.

## Four checks before the first cold week

1. **Calibrate your sensors.** Compare two against each other, or against a reference you trust. A humidity reading that is eight points off will quietly steer every decision after it.
2. **Walk the airflow.** Gentle movement keeps surfaces from going stale, but a fan pointed straight at a bloomy wheel dries the rind. Stand in the room with a strip of tissue and watch where it moves. Look for dead corners as well as drafts.
3. **Decide your humidity source.** Misters around the perimeter, a humidifier on a controller and a pan of water on a shelf are different tools with different risks. Pick the one you can keep clean, since standing water in a cheese room is a food-safety question as well as a humidity one.
4. **Plan your drains and condensate.** Cooler nights mean more condensation on cold surfaces. Know where it drips, and add the spot to your swab map if it is not already there.

**Treat the transition as a sensory exercise.** Rinds tell you things before an instrument does. A wheel that smells slightly ammoniated, or a surface that has turned tacky, is data. A defect-based approach, learning what a good wheel is like first and then what the departures from it smell and feel like, is a standard way of training a team to catch problems early. Panel guidance from one university dairy center suggests five to eight staff per session if you are doing this formally, and professional panels agree on a shared vocabulary so that one taster's "soapy" is another's. For a two-person cave, a shared notebook and a weekly walk will do.

"My apprentice caught a soapy note I'd stopped noticing," a farmstead maker in the upper Midwest said. "It was the humidifier. We'd skipped a cleaning because it was a busy week." One quick fix and one conversation later, the batch was fine. It is much easier to talk about a note than a loss.

The Humidity & Airflow Control course takes these four checks in more detail, and Cave Management covers the broader routine for rooms of different construction. Members who want a second pair of eyes can ask the Education Committee about peer visits; several members have offered to swap walk-throughs this fall.

A last piece of advice, with the caveat that it is yours to weigh against your own room: write down the settings you change and the date you change them. In February, you will want to know why a rind behaved the way it did, and "I think I turned the humidifier up around Halloween" is a poor record.
