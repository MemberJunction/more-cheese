A committee mark-up on a new farm bill draft opened this week in Washington, which for most of our members means exactly nothing changes on the make floor today and possibly quite a bit changes over the next year. For one member spotlight this week, we wanted to talk to the kind of person who actually reads these things line by line: a longtime farmstead cheesemaker who has spent the better part of a decade as the federation's unofficial legislative tripwire, flagging dairy title language to the Membership & Outreach Committee long before it shows up in a headline.

She asked not to be named in this piece — she's quick to say the advocacy work isn't about her, it's about the fifty or so smaller producers in her region who don't have the time to track a bill through committee themselves. "Somebody has to read the appendix," she told us. "It's usually not glamorous. It's a paragraph about handler reporting requirements buried on page 340." That paragraph, she says, is often exactly where a small creamery's actual exposure lives.

## What she watches for

Her method is unglamorous by design: she reads the dairy title of every major agriculture bill draft the day it's released, flags anything that touches margin coverage, interstate shipment, or reporting thresholds, and sends a plain-language summary to the committee within 48 hours. This week's draft is no exception — it's early, and mark-up sessions like the one that opened this week routinely reshape dairy provisions before a bill ever reaches a floor vote, so she's cautious about drawing conclusions yet. "The version that gets marked up almost never looks like the version that gets voted on," she said. "I've learned to wait for the second draft before I get members worked up about anything."

That patience is its own kind of advocacy. A federation position filed too early, based on language that changes in committee anyway, spends credibility the organization needs later in the process. She's watched that mistake happen at other trade groups and has made a point of never letting it happen here.

## Why this matters more in a stabilization year

After a year of closures and cost pressure, 2026 has given members a little more room to think past the current quarter, and that's exactly when legislative tracking pays off most — not during a crisis, when everyone's attention is already elsewhere, but in a quieter year when a dairy title provision can move through committee with almost nobody outside Washington noticing. She's watching this draft specifically for anything touching the margin coverage program smaller farmstead operations lean on, given how central that support was during last year's squeeze.

She's also careful to separate what she's watching in the bill from what's happening in the market right now. This week's federal dairy outlook briefing showed the all-milk price recovering some ground after January's low, with national production still running close to 3% ahead of last year — numbers she reads alongside the legislative text, because a margin coverage formula only matters in the context of where prices actually sit. "You can't advocate for a safety net without knowing whether anyone currently needs it," she said. Right now, by her read, fewer members need it urgently than did a year ago, which is exactly why she thinks this is the moment to get the formula right rather than the emergency fix members were asking for during the worst of 2025.

If you want to help without becoming the next person reading page 340 yourself, the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a distribution list for her plain-language bill summaries — sign up once, and you'll hear about a change in reporting requirements from a fellow member before you hear about it from anyone else.
