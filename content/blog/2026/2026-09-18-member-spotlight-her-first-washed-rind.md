When we asked Marisol Vandenberg how her fall was going, she answered with a humidity number. "Ninety-one," she said. "It was eighty-six on Tuesday, and I've been apologizing to the cave ever since." She is in her second year as a cheesemaker at Larkhollow Creamery, a family farmstead that has been in the federation's directory for a few years, and she has just taken over a washed-rind cheese the farm's founder made for a decade.

She is exactly the kind of member we've been paying attention to this year: a next-generation maker inheriting a recipe, a cave and a set of habits someone else built. We spent an afternoon with her this week, on the phone and over a shared spreadsheet.

## Inheriting a cheese, not just a recipe

The recipe was easy, she told us. The founder's make sheet was clear, and she had run it on her own twice this summer without trouble. The hard part came after the vat, in the cave, where the cheese either becomes what the label says or doesn't.

"A washed rind isn't something you make," she said. "It's something you keep up with. You wash it, you turn it, you look at it, and you do that three times a week for two months. The recipe doesn't tell you what the rind should smell like on day nineteen."

The founder had carried that knowledge in his head and his hands. Marisol had to turn it into numbers she could repeat. For the first batch she wrote down room temperature, humidity, brine strength and wash schedule every day, and took a photograph of every shelf. By the fourth week she had a pattern: the rind turned a good, even orange when humidity held in the high eighties to low nineties, and went patchy and dry in the corners when it sagged.

That tracks with what cave keepers generally report. Soft washed styles tend to do best at high humidity, because a moist rind supports the bacteria that give these cheeses their color and aroma. Humidity swings matter in fall, too: as the weather cools, a cave that sat steady in August can start drifting, and a rind notices before you do.

Her "orange week" came in early September, when a few wheels developed a patchy, faded look on one side. She was convinced she had done something wrong in the make. Instead she traced it to a shelf near the door, where the temperature swung a few degrees every time the loading door opened. She moved those wheels, added a damp cloth near the door for a day or two, and tightened her wash schedule on that rack.

"I would have blamed the milk," she said. "I'm glad I had the log, because the log blamed the door."

She credits two things for catching it: the daily log she started in July, and the Rind Development & Washing course she finished through the Education Committee over the summer. "The course gave me words for what I was seeing," she said. "Before that I just thought it looked wrong."

## What she'd tell the next person to inherit a cheese

We asked what advice she'd give another maker taking over a cave. She didn't hesitate.

- Write down everything the previous maker does without thinking, even the things that seem too obvious to record.
- Log the room, not just the cheese. Humidity and temperature at the shelf tell you more than a single reading at the door.
- Photograph the rind on the same day each week. You'll see change you'd miss in person.
- Ask for help early. Her farm's founder still comes to the cave on Thursdays, and she has stopped pretending she doesn't need him.

Marisol says the outlook at the farm is calmer than it was last year, and that matters. Feed and energy costs have eased, and the family is no longer deciding whether to sell the herd. Trade analysts say the big dairy exporters are leaning further into cheese, which is encouraging to hear, but she says her plans don't depend on a forecast: they depend on a cave that holds ninety-one.

If you're a maker inheriting a recipe, a cave or a whole creamery, talk to us. Our Education Committee can point you to the right course, and the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with other members who have been through a handoff. And if you have a rind story of your own, we'd love to hear it.
