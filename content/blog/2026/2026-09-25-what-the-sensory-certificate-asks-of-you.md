Several members have asked us recently which credential to pursue next, and the answer depends on what you taste for a living. If a good part of your week is spent deciding whether a wheel is ready, whether a batch is drifting or whether a supplier's cheese deserves a spot in your case, the Sensory Evaluation Certificate is worth a hard look.

The certificate is one of the seven ICF credentials. It tests whether you can describe cheese precisely, and whether you can describe it the same way twice. It is not a test of whether you like a cheese. A palate is a tool, and the certificate asks whether yours is calibrated.

Two courses sit underneath it: Sensory Foundations, which builds vocabulary and technique, and Advanced Sensory Evaluation, which pushes into defects, texture and blind comparison. You do not need to take both before sitting, but most members who pass say the second one made the difference.

## Learn the good wheel first

A tip that appears in university dairy-center guidance, and one our instructors repeat: start with a gold-standard sample and a written list of its attributes. Then taste a defective sample next to it. The contrast teaches faster than any lecture. From there, build a list of likely defects, set an acceptable range for each attribute, and train yourself to spot both.

Professional panels anchor terms with reference vials labeled "buttery" or "rancid," and follow a fixed routine for when to chew and when to breathe through the nose. Common off-notes are worth learning by name: butyric or rancid, ammoniated, soapy, barny. A flavor wheel helps you organize them, though no wheel replaces tasting.

"I thought I had a good palate until I sat down with six wheels of the same style and couldn't rank them the same way on Tuesday and Thursday," a buyer for a regional chain of grocers told us. "That was the humbling part. The course made me write everything down, and my notes got more consistent before my nose got any better." She sat the certificate in the spring and says it changed how she briefs her counter staff.

**How to prepare.** Five habits help:

- **Taste on a schedule.** Same time of day, same palate cleanser, same room. Variability in your setup is variability in your results.
- **Use a ballot.** Even a simple one-page form with fixed attributes makes you taste in order and compare like with like.
- **Find a panel.** Guidance from one university dairy center suggests five to eight people for a formal session. Three is workable for practice. If you are a one-person shop, ask a neighboring member to swap blind samples with you.
- **Check agreement.** If two tasters score the same wheel differently, that is a conversation to have, not a mistake to hide. Panels that agree are more useful than panels that are individually brilliant.
- **Keep a running log.** Dates, lot numbers, notes. Review it quarterly.

It is easy to treat sensory work as a buyer's skill. Makers use it too, perhaps more. A cave that has drifted a few degrees may show up in a rind note before it shows up on a logger, a point we made in Wednesday's cave post. A washed-rind maker who can name an off-note can trace it to a cause: a humidifier, a brine, a wash schedule. One who cannot may just discard the batch.

"My head affineur says the certificate gave us a shared language," a creamery manager in the Northeast told us. "Before, it was 'something's off.' Now it's 'a bit soapy, second shelf, left side.' That's something I can act on."
