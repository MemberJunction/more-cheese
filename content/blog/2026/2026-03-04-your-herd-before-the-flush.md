Every spring, the same question shows up in our inbox around this time: milk volume is climbing, and something in the vat behaves differently than it did in January. Fat and protein ratios shift as cows move onto fresh forage, somatic cell counts can swing with the transition, and a make schedule tuned to winter milk sometimes stops giving you the yield or the texture you expect. None of that is a mystery once you know to look for it, but a lot of members tell us they only start looking after the flush has already thrown off a batch.

This year's flush is shaping up to be a big one. National herd numbers have expanded meaningfully over the past year, and per-cow output keeps climbing on top of that, which analysts have been describing as one of the larger spring milk surges in recent memory. A larger flush doesn't just mean more milk moving through your vat faster — it usually means a sharper, more compressed transition period for the herd behind that milk, with less time to adjust before volumes peak.

## Where the course actually starts

Our Milk Quality & Herd Health course is the one we point members to for exactly this stretch of the calendar, and it's built around the transition itself rather than any single season. The herd-health half covers what typically changes physiologically as cows move to spring pasture or fresh-cut forage — the same shifts that show up a few days later as changes in your milk's composition. Extension research on transition-cow management makes the same point from the other direction: farms that monitor fresh cows closely during this window, rather than relying on end-of-lactation numbers, catch problems while they're still cheap to fix. The milk-quality half of the course works backward from that: how to read a rising somatic cell count as an early signal rather than a surprise, and how small adjustments to your make — a slightly longer set time, a different cutting schedule — can hold your yield steady through a period when the raw material itself is moving.

## Where members actually get caught out

The mistake we hear about most isn't ignorance of the flush — every experienced member knows it's coming. It's timing: waiting until yields already dropped or a batch already came out soft before adjusting anything. The course frames spring milk quality as a monitoring problem you start in late winter, not a firefighting problem you solve in April. If you're a smaller operation without a dedicated quality manager, that shift in mindset alone is often worth more than any single technique in the material.

The course also spends real time on documentation, which matters for a reason beyond good practice: if your milk supply or your own herd has any health event during this transition, having a clean record of what you were tracking and when makes any follow-up conversation with a buyer or an inspector considerably shorter. Members who've been through a rough flush season before tend to be the ones who show up for this course early rather than after.

If you're weighing whether to fit it in before your own volume starts climbing, that's the right instinct — it's designed to be useful before the flush, not as a postmortem once it's over. Check the course calendar for the next cohort and talk to your quality lead about whether this is the year to send someone through it.
