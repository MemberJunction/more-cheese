For years, the federal guestworker visa program most farms rely on for temporary labor has come with an asterisk for dairy: because the program is built around seasonal work and a dairy herd needs milking every day of the year, most dairy operations simply could not use it, regardless of how badly they needed the labor. New federal guidance issued this month clarifies that a dairy operation's temporary or seasonal labor need — a hiring gap during calving, a short-term expansion, a stretch covering staff turnover — can now qualify, even though the underlying dairy work itself runs year-round.

This matters more to our members than it might first appear, because the labor question sits upstream of almost everything else in a small operation's calendar. A creamery that cannot find reliable seasonal help during its busiest production months either turns down orders, leans harder on family labor that is already stretched, or delays the kind of expansion that would let it capture a stronger wholesale season. The guidance does not create new visa slots or waive the program's existing wage and housing requirements — those stay exactly as demanding as before — but it removes a categorical exclusion that had made the program effectively unavailable to most dairy operations no matter how well they could meet those requirements.

## What it actually changes

The clarification is narrower than the framing around it sometimes suggests. It addresses eligibility, not capacity: a dairy operation with a genuine temporary or seasonal need can now apply, but the program's paperwork, timelines, and cost structure are unchanged, and none of that is quick. Operations that have never worked with an immigration attorney or a farm labor contractor before should expect the application process itself to take real staff time to learn, even before a single worker arrives. This is not a same-season fix for anyone short-staffed right now.

It is also not a fit for every labor gap. A farmstead operation with a genuinely year-round, ongoing staffing need — not a seasonal spike but a permanent second hand — is not who this guidance is built for, and members in that position should keep looking at the visa categories built for permanent agricultural labor instead. Where it is most likely to help is the operation with a predictable seasonal crunch: extra cheesemaking capacity during the spring flush, additional hands for a summer market season, or coverage during a known stretch of staff turnover.

## Talking to the people who actually run these programs

We are not going to pretend the federation can walk a member through an individual visa petition — that is a conversation for an immigration attorney or an accredited farm labor contractor, and it is not a place to guess. What we can do is flag the change and point members toward it before a seasonal hiring plan gets built around the old assumption that this program simply was not an option. If your operation has a predictable seasonal labor gap and you have never looked into a guestworker program because you assumed dairy did not qualify, this month's guidance is worth a real conversation with someone who specializes in agricultural labor law, not a decision made off a headline.

The Membership & Outreach Committee is collecting member questions on workforce topics for a future session, and workforce guidance like this is exactly the kind of change members have told us they want explained plainly rather than buried in a policy memo. If this affects your hiring plans for the rest of the year, let the committee know what you are running into.
