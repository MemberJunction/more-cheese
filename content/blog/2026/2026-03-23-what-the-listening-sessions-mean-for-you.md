Earlier this month, the federal food-safety regulator opened the first in a round of public listening sessions on how lot-level tracking should actually work once the traceability rule comes fully into force. A member-only session ran the first week of March; the broader public session is scheduled for later this quarter. If you make, age, or distribute a product on the rule's high-risk foods list, this is the moment to have opinions, not the moment to wait and see.

We've had a steady trickle of calls since the sessions were announced, and most of them boil down to the same worry: does this mean re-labeling every wheel that leaves the cave? Not exactly, and that's the part worth explaining.

## What's actually being asked

The listening sessions aren't about whether traceability records are required — that was settled when the rule was finalized. They're about the mechanics: what counts as a "lot" for an aged wheel that gets cut, wrapped, and re-sold in a dozen different portion sizes; how a small creamery with one shared cooler is supposed to keep lot codes distinct from a co-packer's; and whether a hand-written batch log, kept consistently, satisfies the recordkeeping requirement or whether it has to live in a specific software format.

That last question matters more than it sounds. A federation survey we ran last fall found that most of our farmstead members under fifteen employees are still tracking batches on paper or in a spreadsheet they built themselves. The rule doesn't require you to buy new software. It requires that whatever system you use produces the right fields, on request, within twenty-four hours. That's a much lower bar, and it's worth saying out loud so members stop assuming the worst.

There's also a running conversation about where cheese sits on the "high-risk" list at all, and whether products aged past a certain point should be treated differently than fresh, unaged ones. Nothing is decided. If your cave runs long aging cycles, this is exactly the kind of comment that's worth filing while the record is open, rather than after a rule is set.

The federal meeting notice frames this as a genuine two-way conversation rather than a formality, which is a change in tone from how these processes have sometimes gone in the past. Whether that holds through to a final published guidance is a fair thing to stay skeptical about, but for now, the door is open wider than usual.

## What to do this week

Two concrete things. First, if you haven't already, pull your current batch-and-lot recordkeeping and check it against the rule's required fields — the traceability lot code, the date, the quantity, and the location where the transformation happened. Our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate coursework walks through this field by field, and it's a faster read than the Federal Register notice.

Second, if you have an opinion about how lot-level tracking should apply to aged product, send it to the Food Safety Committee. They're compiling member input to submit before the comment window closes, and a paragraph from someone who actually runs a cave carries more weight in that record than most testimony from people who don't.

None of this is legal advice, and we'd rather you confirm anything rule-specific with your own compliance contact before you change how you keep records. But the sessions are a genuine chance to shape how a fairly blunt federal rule gets applied to a business that looks nothing like a large processing plant. Worth an hour of your week.
