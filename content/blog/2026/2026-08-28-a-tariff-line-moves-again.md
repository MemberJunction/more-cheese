Back in February, we wrote about a tariff line that had finally held still after months of back-and-forth. That calm didn't last the summer. This week, a major US trading partner announced a new round of retaliatory tariffs on US dairy imports, cheese included, set to take effect September 8. For members who export, that's roughly a two-week runway to check what's actually in their contracts.

The federation isn't in a position to predict how the broader trade dispute resolves, and members should get their own customs guidance for anything with a shipment date near or after September 8. What we can do is walk through what tends to matter most for a specialty cheese exporter when a tariff line moves on short notice, based on what members have told us worked and didn't work the last time a rate changed underneath them.

## What to check before the line changes

The first thing to look at is your contract's tariff-allocation language, if you have any export contracts at all. Some agreements specify that the buyer absorbs new duties; others split the difference; a fair number say nothing, which usually means an uncomfortable phone call after the fact. If you don't know which kind of contract you're working under, this is the week to find out, not the week after the new rate takes effect and the numbers on an invoice no longer match what either side expected.

Second, check your shipment timing against the effective date. A pallet that clears customs on September 7 is a different financial proposition than one that clears on September 9, and freight delays are common enough that "we'll ship early next week" isn't a reliable hedge on its own. If a shipment is close to the line, talk to your freight forwarder now about realistic transit windows, not best-case ones, and ask specifically what happens to a shipment that's already in transit when a new rate takes effect.

Third, this is a good moment to review your export documentation generally — HS codes, country-of-origin certification, and any existing certificates of free sale — since errors in that paperwork tend to surface exactly when a customs office is already paying closer attention to a product category. The federation's *Export Documentation Essentials* course covers this in more depth for members shipping for the first time or the fifth time, and it's worth a refresher even for exporters who feel confident in their process.

## The bigger picture

This is happening against a backdrop of otherwise decent export demand for US cheese this year — plenty of coverage this month has pointed to cheese as one of the stronger performers in US dairy exports overall, even as milk availability has tightened somewhat for cheese plants that don't have their own supply. A single tariff line moving doesn't undo that trend on its own, but it does mean the members most exposed to one trading partner should be looking harder at diversifying their export mix, not just riding out this particular dispute and hoping the next one lands somewhere else.

If your export volume to any single country is large enough that a new tariff would meaningfully change your margin, it's worth a conversation with the Membership & Outreach Committee about what other members in similar positions have done. We don't have a template answer, but we do have members who've navigated a tariff shift before, and most of them are glad to compare notes over email or at the next regional meetup.
