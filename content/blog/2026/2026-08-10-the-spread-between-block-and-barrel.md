The federal milk price checks members compare every month moved the wrong direction again this week: the blended price across the federal marketing orders came in about a dollar lower per hundredweight than July, and the Class IV number used to peg a lot of vat cheese contracts slid with it. If you make cheddar for wholesale, you already felt this before you read a single report — it showed up as a slightly smaller check and a buyer asking, again, whether your price can hold.

Here's the number we think deserves more attention than the headline milk check: the spread between block and barrel cheddar. Forty-pound blocks jumped a nickel on the spot market at the start of this week while barrels sat still, widening a gap that had already been open most of August. That kind of move usually means barrel-cheese processors — the ones supplying a lot of the shred and process-cheese market — are short on milk relative to block makers. For a small or mid-size cheddar producer selling into wholesale, that gap is a genuine signal about which buyers have leverage this month and which don't.

It isn't happening against a backdrop of shrinking supply, either. Industry reporting on first-half 2026 production shows cheese output nationally running a couple of percentage points ahead of last year. Plenty of cheese is being made; it's the mix and the timing of milk flowing to different plants that's producing this spread, not an overall shortage.

## What the spread tells your sales calls

If you're negotiating a wholesale contract renewal in the next few weeks, know which side of that spread your buyer sits on before you sit down. A distributor moving barrel-format product for foodservice is working from a tighter supply picture right now than a retail block buyer is, and that shows up in how hard they'll push back on price. It's a small thing, but it's the difference between "we can't move on that number" and "we might, if you can guarantee volume."

None of this changes what the milk actually costs to produce. Feed and labor haven't moved much since spring, so a lower milk check without a matching drop in input costs is a margin squeeze, plain and simple. If your cost accounting hasn't been touched since the price run-up earlier this year, this is the week to open it back up. Members using Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese have told us the course's break-even worksheet is built exactly for a month like this one — plug in this week's milk price and last week's feed invoice and see where you actually stand before you're on the phone with a buyer.

## Small comfort, but a real one

The good news, such as it is: nobody we've talked to this month is describing conditions like last year's squeeze. Cheese-grade milk is tight in places, not scarce everywhere, and demand for specialty and artisan cheese has stayed firm through the summer according to the market reporting we track. A tighter spread and a softer milk check are the kind of numbers a stabilizing market throws at you — normal turbulence, not a repeat of 2025.
