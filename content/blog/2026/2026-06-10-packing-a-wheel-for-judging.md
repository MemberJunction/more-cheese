Entries for the Annual Conference's flagship competition close in a few weeks, and the Awards & Competition Committee tells us the calls they field this time of year are rarely about the cheese itself. They're about the box. Every year, a handful of otherwise strong entries get marked down or disqualified for reasons that have nothing to do with flavor: wrong portion size, plugged wheels, packaging that doesn't match what's actually on your retail shelf.

The rule that catches people most often is simple to state and easy to forget under deadline pressure: enter the cheese in the form you sell it. If your wholesale unit is a ten-pound wheel, send a wheel, not a wedge cut to look tidy for shipping. If your product line is five-pound bricks, a brick is the entry. Judges are evaluating a commercial product, and a resized or reshaped entry reads as exactly that to a trained eye, even before anyone tastes it.

## Portion size, without the guesswork

For larger formats, you don't need to ship the whole thing. An entry of twenty-five pounds or more can typically be represented by a five-to-ten-pound cut; anything under that but over a pound usually calls for either a similar cut or a small handful of full units. Check your category's entry packet for the exact numbers before you box anything — categories vary, and this is the single easiest thing to get right if you read the form twice before you pack.

Judging itself runs in pairs: one judge scoring technical qualities (deducting from a perfect base score for defects in flavor, body, texture, salt, color, and finish) and one scoring aesthetic qualities (building a score up from the cheese's best characteristics). Neither judge is grading your presentation table. They are grading the wheel or brick you shipped, cut open under work lights, nothing more.

## Before you ship

A few habits our longtime competitors swear by: pull your entry from the cave a day earlier than you think you need to, so it travels at a stable temperature instead of finishing its cooldown in transit. Double-check that anything with a federal standard of identity — a cheddar, a Swiss-style, a mozzarella — actually meets that standard's specifications before you enter it in that category; misclassified entries get pulled rather than judged. And if this is your first year entering, the Judging & Competition Preparation course walks through exactly this packing and labeling checklist with real examples from past cycles, not just the rulebook language.

If you're on the fence about entering this year, the deadline is closer than the summer feels. A conversation with the Awards & Competition Committee costs you ten minutes and might save you a wasted shipment.

It helps to remember the scale on the other side of the table. A national competition can call in several dozen judges to work through thousands of entries across a handful of days before a single Best of Show gets named. Nobody on that side of the table is lingering over your box art or your cover letter. They're cutting into a wheel, checking it against the standard for its category, and moving to the next one. Every minute a judge spends puzzling over whether your entry matches its stated category, or fighting with packaging that wasn't built for a working table, is a minute they aren't spending noticing what your cheese does well.

That's really the whole case for getting the boring logistics right. The categories your product could plausibly fit are usually narrower than makers assume; read the category descriptions closely rather than picking the one that sounds most flattering, since a genuinely excellent cheese entered in the wrong category is competing against products it was never meant to be compared with. If you're unsure which category fits, ask the committee before you enter rather than after you've already shipped.

For first-time entrants specifically, our advice is to run a dry pack a week before the real one. Box the portion size you plan to send, weigh it, check that any required consumer packaging is on it with no visible brand labeling where the rules call for a blind entry, and time how long it actually takes to get from your cave to a shipping counter. Nothing catches an avoidable mistake faster than doing the whole process once under no time pressure. If your dry run turns something up, you've still got time before the real deadline; if it doesn't, you'll ship your entry knowing the cheese is the only thing left for the judges to evaluate.
