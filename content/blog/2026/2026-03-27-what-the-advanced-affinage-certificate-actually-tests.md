Of all seven credentials the federation offers, the Advanced Affinage Certificate gets the most questions and the fewest applications, which tells us the description isn't doing its job. It's not a beginner's credential, and it's not only for people who own a cave. Here's what it's actually testing.

## Who it assumes you already are

The certificate assumes you've already got the basics down — humidity, temperature, rind development, the general vocabulary of an aging program. It doesn't re-teach any of that; the Cheese Foundations Certificate and Sensory Evaluation Certificate cover that ground, and we'd point you there first if you're newer to the craft. Advanced Affinage is built for someone who's already running an aging program, successfully, and wants a credential that says so to a buyer or an inspector who's never set foot in your cave.

That distinction matters this year in particular. With production up and margins finally easing after last year's cost squeeze, a lot of members are thinking about scaling their aging programs rather than just surviving them — a second cave, a longer-aged line, a wholesale account that wants documentation. Recent industry reporting on the specialty cheese category points the same direction: buyers are increasingly asking producers to document how a product was made and aged, not just what it tastes like on the counter. The credential is aimed at exactly that transition, and it's meant to be a portable answer to that kind of question, one you can hand to a buyer instead of re-explaining your process from scratch every time someone asks.

## What's actually on the exam

Three sections. The first is a written component on microbial succession — what's supposed to be happening on a rind at week two versus week twelve, and how to tell the difference between a normal bloom and a problem before it costs you a batch. The second is a case-study section: you're given a cave's humidity and temperature logs over several weeks and asked to diagnose what went wrong, or confirm that nothing did. The third is a practical evaluation, in person, where you walk an assessor through an active aging room and explain your own program out loud — why this shelf, why this turning schedule, why you moved a batch when you did.

That practical section is the one people underestimate. It's less about having the right answer and more about being able to explain your reasoning to someone who's going to push back on it. If you've never had to defend a cave decision to an outside evaluator, it's worth practicing on a colleague first, out loud, before the assessor ever walks through your door.

Most candidates fail the same way: not on the written section, but by treating the practical walkthrough like a memorized script instead of a real conversation about a real cave. An assessor who's spent years evaluating aging programs can tell the difference within the first two minutes, and it's better to find that out from a colleague's blunt feedback than from a failed exam.

The credential doesn't require you to take a specific course first, but most members who pass it have gone through the Affinage Fundamentals coursework at some point, even if it was years ago, and current cohorts are open for anyone who wants a refresher before sitting the exam. If you're already running an aging program and you've been putting off the paperwork, this is a reasonable spring to finally do it — the exam windows open again next month, and your Education Committee contact can walk you through scheduling.
