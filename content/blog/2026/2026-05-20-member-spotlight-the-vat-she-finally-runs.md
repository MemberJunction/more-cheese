For three years, a farmstead assistant on a mid-sized sheep-and-cow operation in the upper Midwest signed off on someone else's make sheet. She adjusted the set times, watched the whey break, called the cuts — but the numbers on the page belonged to the head cheesemaker who'd built the program. This spring, that cheesemaker retired, and the vat became hers.

"I thought I was ready because I'd done every step of it a hundred times," she told us. "What I wasn't ready for was making the call when two things disagreed with each other — when the pH says one thing and the clock says another, and there's no one two feet away to ask." She said the first month she second-guessed herself constantly, then started keeping her own log of every judgment call and why she made it, partly so she'd have a record and partly so she'd stop asking the question twice.

## What actually changed

The recipe didn't change. The herd didn't change. What changed, she said, was the weight of small decisions that used to be someone else's to carry. A slightly warmer curd on a humid week, a batch that needed an extra ten minutes on the table — those used to be judgment calls she watched someone else make and quietly agreed or disagreed with. Now they're hers, and there's no appeal.

She's also become the one who has to explain the make to a new hire, which she said taught her more about her own process than three years of doing it herself. "You don't realize how much you know until you have to say it out loud in an order that makes sense to somebody else." She's started writing down the reasoning behind steps that used to just live in her hands, partly for the next person and partly, she admits, so she has something to check her own memory against on a bad day.

## What she'd tell the next assistant stepping up

Her advice for anyone facing a similar handoff, planned or not: keep a log before you need one. "I wish I'd started writing down my reasoning in year one instead of year three, when I finally had to." She also said the hardest part wasn't technical — it was trusting a decision enough to stop revisiting it once the wheels were pressed. Membership webinars and courses can teach the chemistry; the confidence to let a decision stand is something she says she's still building, one batch at a time.

If you're the assistant who's quietly running more of the operation than your title says, or the owner weighing when to hand that vat over, her one concrete suggestion was to start the overlap earlier than feels necessary — a full season, if the operation can afford it, not a few weeks. That runway matters more than it looks like from the outside: industry surveys this spring have been blunt about how many small dairy operations are heading toward a handoff with no plan and no obvious successor at all, which makes even an imperfect overlap a genuine advantage.

The federation doesn't have a course that replaces that kind of runway, but the Milk Quality & Herd Health course and a season of shared decisions can get a new operator most of the way there. She said the credential itself mattered less than having a reason to sit down with the outgoing cheesemaker and walk through the make sheet line by line, out loud, before the questions got expensive. If your own operation is a year or two out from a similar handoff, that conversation is worth starting now rather than waiting for the retirement date to force it.
