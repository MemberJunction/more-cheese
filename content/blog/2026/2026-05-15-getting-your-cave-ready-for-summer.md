Most aging rooms hold their conditions reasonably well through the cooler months without much intervention. Summer is a different test. As outside temperatures climb, a room that was quietly stable in March can start drifting a degree or two warmer by mid-afternoon, and humidity that held steady all winter can swing with every door opening. If your cave has never been stress-tested by real heat before, this is the month to find its limits on purpose, not by accident in July.

## The two numbers that actually matter

Hard cheeses like cheddar and alpine styles generally want the room around 80–85% relative humidity; bloomy and washed-rind styles want it closer to 90–95%. Both ranges want a temperature in the 50–55°F band. The mistake we hear about most often isn't picking the wrong target — it's not noticing when the room has drifted away from it, because nobody checked the hygrometer for a week. A cheap digital hygrometer, checked daily during a heat stretch, catches a slow drift long before it shows up as a cracked rind or a slick one.

Airflow matters as much as either number on its own. A room can read correctly on both dials and still develop a warm pocket near a wall that gets afternoon sun, or a damp corner where air barely moves. If you've never mapped your own room with a thermometer in five or six spots at once, summer is the season that will teach you where the pockets are — better to find them with a thermometer than with a batch of cheese.

A drifting cave isn't only a quality problem, either. This year's food-safety research has kept environmental control in the conversation for a reason: researchers have been highlighting options for reducing risk in raw-milk cheese specifically through better management of the production and aging environment, not just the milk itself. A cave that runs warmer and wetter than intended isn't just a cosmetic risk to the rind — it's a shift in the conditions your monitoring program was built around, which is exactly the kind of change your environmental sampling plan should be catching before a customer ever notices.

## Low-cost fixes before you spend on equipment

Not every fix requires new refrigeration. An insulated door, or even a heavy curtain hung just inside an existing one, cuts down on the warm air that rushes in every time someone opens the room. Positioning anything heat-sensitive away from a wall that catches direct sun helps more than most members expect. And a simple saturated salt solution — plain table salt in distilled water, left in an open container — holds humidity in a small room close to a predictable, steady level without running a humidifier around the clock.

None of this replaces mechanical cooling if your room genuinely can't hold temperature once outside heat sets in, but it buys real time, and it's worth doing before you decide new equipment is the only answer. The federation's Humidity & Airflow Control course walks through building a simple heat map of your own room, reading a hygrometer correctly, and deciding when a low-cost fix is enough versus when it's time to budget for mechanical help. Whichever category your cave falls into, better to know now than to find out mid-summer with a rack of wheels riding on the answer.
