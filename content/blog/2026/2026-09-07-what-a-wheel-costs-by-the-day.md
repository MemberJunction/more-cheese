Most of us know exactly what a wheel cost on make day. Milk, culture, rennet, a few hours of labor. Ask the same maker what that wheel cost on day 120 and you tend to get a long pause, and then a guess.

The pause is understandable. Cave costs don't arrive as an invoice with a wheel's name on it. They show up as a power bill, a share of the rent, a pile of cloth and sanitizer, and an hour of someone's afternoon spent flipping. Spread across a full room, each wheel's share is tiny. Left unmeasured, the shares add up to a real number.

## Build a daily carrying rate

A farmstead maker we spoke with last week put it this way: "I knew my make cost. I did not know my *keeping* cost, and the keeping is half the cheese." She spent a Sunday building a simple rate, and the method is one anyone can borrow.

1. **List what the room costs each month.** Cave electricity and dehumidification, a fair share of rent and insurance, depreciation on shelving, and the supplies you burn through on rind care.
2. **Add the labor.** Flipping, brushing and washing take real hours. Put a number on them even if the person doing them is you.
3. **Count wheel-days.** Add up the days each wheel spends in the room that month. Forty wheels for thirty days is twelve hundred wheel-days.
4. **Divide.** Monthly cave cost over wheel-days is your daily carrying rate per wheel.

Now multiply that rate by the days a style ages, and add it to the make-day cost. A cheese that rests for six months has carried a lot more than one that leaves in six weeks. That is usually where the surprise lives, and it is why the long-aged wheel on your price list may be earning less than the young one next to it.

## Shrink belongs in the math

Aging also costs you weight. A wheel loses moisture the whole time it sits, so the pound you sell is lighter than the pound you pressed. Weigh a few wheels of each style at hoop-out and again at sale, and keep that percentage next to your carrying rate. If the loss for a given style starts creeping up against your own history, look at the room before you look at the recipe. Humidity or temperature drift is a common reason.

Keep your logs too. Temperature and humidity readings are records you may be glad to have when someone asks about your room, and a clipboard that was last filled in on Tuesday is a poor witness. A simple digital logger with a dated history is easier to defend.

Costs have eased from the squeeze many of you felt in 2025, and several members have told us the relief is real. That is a good moment to measure rather than to relax. The national margin program that dairy farms use compares the all-milk price against a standard feed-cost formula, and its published forecast for 2026 expected the average margin to sit lower than in 2025, with the payments concentrated early in the year. If you milk your own herd, that tells you the milk side of your ledger is moving. The cave side is the part you control, and it only takes an afternoon to measure.

We aren't going to tell you what to charge. Every creamery has its own cost structure, and your accountant is the right person for questions about how aging costs are treated in your books. What we can offer is a way to see the numbers.
