Most of us read the market from the front of the vat: curd, blocks, wheels. This month the more interesting movement is at the back of it. In the federal wholesale report for the week ending September 5, salted butter and 40-pound cheddar blocks both slipped, while dry whey edged up to roughly 67 cents a pound. A week later blocks eased again and whey gave back less than half a cent. It is a small move. It also continues a long run.

## What the numbers say

For the week ending September 5, butter averaged near $1.47 a pound, down a few cents, and blocks were close to flat. Nonfat dry milk rose by about five cents. Dry whey gained just over a penny. In the following week's report, blocks fell by a little over three cents to about $1.58, butter recovered slightly and whey eased to about 66 cents. Meanwhile the federal fluid-milk base price for September was announced at $17.04 per hundredweight, lower than August, because butter and cheese benchmarks eased more than powder firmed.

None of that is dramatic on its own. The pattern behind it is the part worth reading. Trade analysts reported in July that dry whey has held above 60 cents a pound since last September, a level that would have looked fanciful a few years ago. Under the federal pricing formula, every five-cent move in dry whey shifts the cheese-class milk price by about 30 cents per hundredweight. A by-product is quietly propping up the milk check.

The reason, by most accounts, is protein. Processors are chasing high-protein whey ingredients for sports nutrition and fortified foods, and that demand has tightened supply of the lower-protein products too. There are also soft spots: some high-protein exports have slowed this year and prices for the most concentrated products have eased from their June highs. Analysts still expect whey to stay elevated through year-end, but hedge accordingly.

## What it means at a creamery your size

A large plant with a drying line sees this directly. A farmstead making a few thousand pounds of cheese a week mostly does not, because your whey probably goes to pigs, a neighbor's field or a hauler. But it is not irrelevant to you.

"I used to count whey as a disposal cost and nothing else," a sheep-milk maker in the northern Plains told us this week. "Then my hauler changed his rate and I started asking what it was actually worth. Turns out the answer depends a lot on who's buying and how far they're driving." She has not changed her make. She has started pricing her whey the way she prices everything else, which is a habit we would encourage.

Three practical points:

- **Check your milk contract.** If you buy milk on a cheese-class formula, whey is already inside your cost. Read how the formula treats it before you assume a falling block price is good news.
- **Ask what your whey could earn.** A short list of nearby buyers, hauling costs and any cooperative arrangement will tell you whether the by-product is a cost, a wash or a small credit.
- **Do not build a plan on a spike.** Protein-driven prices can ease as fast as they rose. Treat a high whey price as a bonus on your sheet, not as a line you rely on.

Butter and cheese softening while whey climbs is the kind of split that makes a budget feel like a cave in October: the temperature is fine, the humidity is off and you are not sure which dial to touch. Resist the urge to touch all of them. Our early-September milk-check coverage looked at the gap between the cheese and butter classes; this is the same market seen from the other end of the vat.

Our read for the rest of September: butter has seasonal inventory pressure, block cheese is soft, and whey is carrying more of the milk check than it used to. We will keep watching the weekly numbers and say so when something changes.
