Wholesale cheese prices moved up again in the cash market last week, and the more interesting number wasn't the price itself but the gap between the two grades traded there. Barrels and blocks — the commodity cheddar that sets the tone for a lot of contract pricing further down the chain — have been trading only fractions of a cent apart for months now, instead of the wider spread that used to be normal. If you sell anything priced off that benchmark, even loosely, this is worth five minutes of your attention.

A narrow spread usually means the market can't find much reason to prefer one form over the other, which tends to happen when overall supply and demand are close to balanced rather than lopsided. That's broadly consistent with what federal forecasters have been saying about 2026: milk production is running higher than last year, largely on gains in milk per cow, and the most recent revision actually raised most dairy product price forecasts rather than cutting them. A separate reading of the international auction market in early March showed the broader price index up over five percent, with cheddar and several other categories moving higher together. None of that is a coincidence — it points to demand holding up against a genuinely large milk supply, which is a different story than the oversupply years some of our longer-tenured members remember.

For members further from the commodity end of things — your aged wheels, your flavored lines, your direct accounts — this isn't a number you price against directly. It's a signal about the floor underneath the whole market, and it's a friendlier floor than we've had in some recent springs.

## Why this matters going into spring

Milk production climbs seasonally as we move toward the spring flush, and that additional volume usually puts downward pressure on wholesale prices unless demand keeps pace. A tight, stable barrel-block spread right now suggests the market is entering that seasonal increase from a steadier starting point than it has in a couple of years — worth remembering if you're a small producer wondering whether to lock in a supply contract or wait. It's also a reasonable moment to revisit your own cost basis before the flush arrives, since a stable commodity market doesn't mean your feed, labor, or energy costs are stable too.

If you buy milk or cultures on contract, or if your distributor pricing references a commodity index, ask your rep now what benchmark they're using and how often it resets. Members have told us in past years that surprises here tend to show up in the contract renewal, not the invoice — by the time you notice, you've usually already agreed to the number. It's a five-minute question that's cheaper to ask in March than to untangle in June.

The federation doesn't publish price forecasts and won't start now; that's not our lane, and figures a season out are guesses dressed up as data. What we can tell you is which questions members are asking their own suppliers and buyers this time of year, and encourage you to ask them too before your next quarterly contract conversation. If your pricing committee or your own bookkeeper wants a plain-language rundown of what "barrel-block spread" actually means before that conversation, our membership office can point you to the primer we send new members every winter.
