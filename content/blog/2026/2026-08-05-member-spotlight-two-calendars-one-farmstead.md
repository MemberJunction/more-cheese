We ask a lot of members what August looks like for them, and this year one answer stuck with us. A farmstead cheesemaker running a small herd and an even smaller cave told us she keeps two calendars taped to the same wall — one for hay, one for cheese — and the trick of her whole summer is making sure neither one wins.

She's in her third season making cheese full time, after several years helping on the family dairy side before deciding the milk should stop at the farm gate instead of leaving on a truck. That decision means every August choice about hay — when to cut, when the forecast forces a second cutting window, how many bales the barn actually has room for — happens in the same week as choices about which wheels come out of the cave for a retail order and which ones need another month. Neither calendar defers to the other, and this is the month that shows it.

## When curd and cutting collide

"The herd doesn't know it's cheese season," she told us, "and the cheese doesn't know it's hay season. I do, and that's the whole job." Her cave sits at the back of the barn, close enough that heat from a long cutting day radiates through the wall by evening — not enough to spike the humidity readings dangerously, but enough that she's learned to check them twice a day in August instead of once, and to plan turning the youngest wheels for the cooler morning hours rather than late afternoon.

This is also the month her wholesale account asks for its steadiest volume of the year, which she says used to feel like bad timing until she stopped fighting it. Now the retail case gets whatever's ready and good; the wholesale order gets planned three weeks out, built around wheels she already knows will be at peak by delivery day, not wheels she hopes will get there. It's a small shift — from reacting to a calendar to writing one — but she says it's the difference between an August that exhausts her and one that just keeps her busy.

## What she'd tell a member starting this same season

Her advice for anyone running livestock and a creamery off the same small operation: write both calendars down on paper, in the same room, before the season starts, not partway through it. "The year I tried to keep it all in my head was the year I lost track of a wheel that needed turning and didn't catch it for four days," she said. "Nothing spoiled. But I stopped trusting my own memory that August, and I haven't gone back."

She's also become an evangelist, in a low-key way, for keeping a simple written log of cave conditions during any month where the barn's other work generates heat — not a formal food-safety record, just a habit that catches drift before it becomes a problem. It's the kind of practice a lot of small farmstead operations build out of necessity and never think to mention until someone asks.

If you're balancing field work and cave work on the same small operation this month, you're not the only one improvising a system for it. The Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a running list of members willing to compare notes on exactly this kind of scheduling — reach out if you'd like to be added, or if you've got a system of your own worth passing along.
