A new round of tariffs on one of the country's largest cross-border dairy trading relationships takes effect this week, and it landed on members' desks the way these things usually do: as a customs broker's email forwarded three times before anyone read it closely. The new duties apply to a specific list of dairy products moving in one direction, with a retaliatory list from the other side expected to follow within weeks. If your cheese, or the whey and cultures that go into it, crosses that particular border in either direction, this is not background noise. It changes your landed cost on Tuesday in a way it didn't on Monday.

We've spent the week fielding a version of the same question from members who export finished wheels, and from members who buy cultures, coagulants, or packaging from suppliers on the other side of that line: does this apply to me, and how do I find out for certain? The honest answer is that tariff schedules are organized by harmonized system code, not by product name, and a lot of cheese-adjacent inputs — casein, milk protein concentrate, certain wheys — sit on lists that read nothing like "cheese" until you know to look for them. We're hearing from a few members that a shipment they assumed was unaffected got flagged at the border anyway, because the product code didn't match what they expected.

## What we're telling members to check this week

First, pull your last twelve months of harmonized system codes for anything you import or export across that border — not just finished cheese, but ingredients and packaging too — and check each one against the new tariff list, not against a summary of it. Summaries miss codes. Second, if you have product in transit or scheduled to ship in the next two weeks, call your customs broker before you call anyone else; timing at the border right now determines which side of the new rate a shipment lands on. Third, if you sell wholesale into that market through a distributor, ask them directly whether they're absorbing the new duty, passing it through, or renegotiating terms — because right now, plenty of distributors don't have an answer yet either.

This is landing at an odd moment for the broader market. Milk production has been running ahead of last year, and cheese prices have been softer through the summer than most members would like, which means there's less room in anyone's margin to absorb a sudden new cost on the export or import side. A tariff that would have been an annoyance in a tighter market is a real problem in a loose one.

We don't have a policy position on the tariffs themselves — trade policy of this scale is well outside what our federation weighs in on. What we can do is keep members current on what's actually in the new rules as clarifications come out, because the early guidance from any new tariff schedule tends to get revised more than once in the first month. If you find a discrepancy between what your broker tells you and what the published schedule says, tell us — we're tracking member reports so we can flag patterns worth raising with the committee that watches trade issues, even if we're not the ones negotiating the fix.

For members not directly exposed to this particular border, don't assume you're clear. A supplier two steps up your chain may be, and their new cost is about to become your new invoice.
