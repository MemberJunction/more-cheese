Spring flush has a reputation, and it is not a flattering one for anyone paid by the hundredweight. As the herd's milk output climbs through April and May, plants that can only handle so much raw milk usually end up with more than they need, and prices soften accordingly. That is the pattern most of our members grew up planning around. This year the pattern is bending, because a wave of new cheese and butter plant capacity built over the last two years has come online faster than the milk supply feeding it has grown, even with the national herd near its largest size in three decades.

The practical result is a spring flush that isn't burdensome the way it usually is. Some regions are seeing plants compete for spot milk rather than turn it away, an arrangement that would have sounded backwards a few years ago and now shows up as real premiums on a spot load. It is not universal, and it is not a permanent feature of the market, but it is a genuinely different spring than the one most members are used to.

## Why this matters even if you don't sell raw milk

If you are a cheesemaker who buys milk rather than milks your own herd, a tighter-than-usual spring flush changes your leverage in a way worth noticing now rather than in June. A plant fighting for supply is a plant less likely to push back on a modest price adjustment, and more likely to want a longer-term supply commitment from a buyer it can count on. That is a different negotiating table than the one members have sat at for most of the past year, when milk was plentiful and buyers had the leverage.

If you milk your own herd and sell through a cooperative or plant relationship, this is a reasonable season to ask whether your own contract terms reflect a market that has genuinely shifted, rather than assuming last year's terms are still the right ones. The federation is not in a position to tell any member what to ask for, but a market moving in your direction is worth a conversation you might otherwise have put off.

## What to watch through the rest of spring

New capacity does not stay ahead of milk supply forever. Herds are still growing, and most of the regional build-out members have told us about is designed with a few more years of expansion in mind, not a permanent structural shortage. The spot premiums showing up this spring are a signal of a market catching up to itself, not a new normal to plan a whole business around.

For members trying to make sense of how a supply story like this one interacts with their own milk contracts, the Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding questions all spring and can point you toward other members in your region working through the same math. A market this unusual is exactly the kind of thing worth comparing notes on rather than puzzling through alone.
