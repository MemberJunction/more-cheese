When a draft petition started circulating this month, most of us read it for what it might mean. One of our members read it for what it might cost.

She runs a small farmstead in the Northeast, makes a raw-milk cheddar from one herd, and sells most of it through a handful of grocers and a Saturday stand. She asked us not to use her name, and so we haven't. What she did let us do is walk through her kitchen-table arithmetic, because it is a useful model for anyone else in the same position.

## The petition, in plain terms

First, what is actually on the table. A food-safety law firm published a draft citizen petition that would ask the federal food regulator to require a warning on all raw-milk cheese, domestic or imported. It would also ask the regulator to finish a review of the 60-day aging exception that it said in 2013 it would reconsider, and to publish its raw-milk cheese sampling data. The draft does not ask for a ban or for limits on sales.

The petition is not ours, and the federation has not endorsed it. It is also still a draft: the authors asked for feedback before filing, and asked makers specifically whether a single prescribed sentence, with a transition placard, would be workable and what label changes would cost. That last question is the one our member took seriously.

## Her arithmetic

"I'm not arguing about whether the sentence is fair," she told us. "I'm asking what happens to my Tuesday."

She started with her packaging. She has two label sizes and a vacuum-sealed wedge, and the existing label stock was ordered months ago. She priced a reprint, a design change, the leftover stock she would have to write off, and the hours it takes her to re-label a case by hand if a retailer asks for the new wording early.

Then she went to the counter side, because the draft also asks whether counter placards and online product pages should carry the warning. "My grocers have ten different suppliers," she said. "I do not want to be the one whose cheese needs a special sign."

She will not share her final figure, and we will not invent one. But she did give us the shape of it: the one-time cost is manageable, the transition is the hard part, and the answer depends almost entirely on how long a maker has to run down old stock.

If you have not done this exercise for your own make, our course *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* is built for it. It treats a packaging change as a line in your margin sheet, not a surprise.

Her bigger worries were not about money. The draft cites a national pediatricians' group, which said earlier this month that cheese aged under the 60-day exception still carries an infection risk. It also points to a recent outbreak that has sickened 13 people in nine states. She read both and did not flinch.

"I make this cheese for people I could name," she said. "If a sentence on the label would have kept one child out of a hospital, I want to know that before I argue about the font."

She is also realistic that the record is thin on one point. The authors themselves note that the regulator's review has had no proposal in more than a decade. "I would rather have a decision than another decade of waiting," she said. "I cannot plan a business around a rule that might move."

She plans to send comments, and has a short list that we think is a good template for any member:

- Ask how much time makers will have to run down existing label stock.
- Ask whether the wording can sit on a placard or a product page instead of every wedge.
- Ask what the regulator's own sampling data says, and when it will be published.
- Ask what a maker should do while the 60-day review is still open.

The window for comments on the draft has been reported as closing at the end of this month, though we have seen more than one date, so please confirm it at the source. If you want help drafting a comment, the Food Safety Committee is happy to read it before you send it. We cannot give legal advice, and a comment is not a substitute for it.

Whatever you decide, write it down while it is fresh: your costs, your timeline and your questions. Your make sheet is evidence.
