Early February is when a hard winter finally shows up in the bulk tank. Somatic cell counts creep, components drift, and a make that behaved perfectly in November suddenly needs a different hand. Before you start second-guessing your culture or your set time, it's worth looking at the herd first.

Cold stress is easy to underestimate because cattle handle it better than people assume, right up until they don't. A dry, well-coated animal can shrug off temperatures well below freezing without much trouble. The problem is what happens around that threshold — wet bedding, drafts, or a hair coat that didn't dry out between milkings all chip away at the margin an animal has before she starts spending energy on staying warm instead of on making milk.

## Where to look first

If your quality numbers are drifting and you can't explain it from the make side, walk the barn before you touch the vat. A few things worth checking:

**Bedding and draft.** Deep, dry bedding does more for winter milk quality than almost anything else on the farm side. Damp bedding against a cold hide is a fast way to lose the insulating value of a winter coat, and it's one of the more common quiet causes of a somatic cell count that won't come down.

**Teat condition at the parlor.** Cows leaving the parlor with wet teats in cold air are at real risk of chapping and frostbite, both of which are an open door for mastitis-causing organisms. A cold-weather teat dip, and a few extra seconds making sure teats are dry before the animal goes back outside, pays for itself in fewer treated cows.

**Water intake.** It's counterintuitive, but cold weather often means animals drink less, and lower water intake drags feed intake down with it. Check that water sources aren't icing over, especially on the coldest overnight stretches — a tank that's frozen for even a few hours can knock production for the rest of the day.

**Energy in the ration.** Cattle need meaningfully more energy in cold weather just to maintain body temperature, before any of that energy goes toward milk. If your nutritionist hasn't adjusted the ration for this stretch of cold, that's worth a call now rather than after the next quality report comes back.

## Why this belongs in your make room, not just your barn

For members running their own herd, milk quality problems in February usually trace back to one of the four things above, not to anything wrong with the cheese itself. But even members buying milk from an outside supplier benefit from understanding this — a supplier's herd having a rough cold snap is a useful thing to ask about before you assume your culture went bad.
