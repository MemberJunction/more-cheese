We hear the same assumption often enough that it's worth addressing directly: members outside retail tend to assume the Certified Cheese Professional (CCP) credential is a counter-facing badge, something for a cheesemonger who talks customers through a case rather than anyone who actually makes the product. The exam itself does not agree. It is built in four sections — production, affinage and handling, sensory evaluation, and business and regulatory knowledge — and a maker with strong production instincts but thin sensory vocabulary will find the exam just as unforgiving as a retail candidate who has never run a vat.

That structure is deliberate. The Awards & Competition and Education Committees built the CCP a few years back to sit above the entry-level Cheese Foundations Certificate, and the goal was a single credential that certifies someone understands the whole life of a cheese, not just the part of it they personally work in. A production manager who can walk a customer through why a washed rind smells the way it does, or a retail buyer who understands why a maker's humidity log matters, is more useful to a business than either half alone.

## The section makers underestimate

The business and regulatory section is the one production-side candidates most often walk in unprepared for, and it is worth naming directly. It covers labeling requirements, the basics of interstate shipment rules for aged raw-milk product, and enough recordkeeping fundamentals to know when a question belongs to your food safety plan rather than your marketing copy. None of it requires the depth of the federation's Food Safety & HACCP Certificate, but candidates who have never sat through the labeling module of that program tend to lose points here that have nothing to do with their cheese knowledge.

The sensory section trips up the opposite group. Retail and floor staff who talk about flavor all day sometimes struggle with the exam's more structured vocabulary — naming a specific off-flavor rather than describing it, or placing a texture defect in the right category rather than just noticing it is there. The federation's Building a Tasting Vocabulary course exists largely because of this gap, and members preparing for the CCP tell us it is the single most useful two-week investment before sitting the exam.

Timing matters here too. Application windows for a credential like this tend to open months ahead of the sitting itself and close well before it, with the exam day arriving quietly on the calendar for anyone who was not tracking the deadlines. Members who wait until the month of the exam to start studying are usually the ones who tell us afterward that the business and regulatory section caught them off guard.

## Getting ready without overbuilding your prep

You do not need every federation credential to sit the CCP, and we would rather members not collect certificates for their own sake. What helps is targeted: if your background is production, spend your prep time on the business and regulatory section and the sensory vocabulary that does not come naturally from running a vat. If your background is retail or floor sales, spend it on the production and affinage sections, even at a level well short of running your own cave. The exam rewards breadth over depth in any one area, which is exactly the point of a credential meant to certify someone understands the whole business, not just their corner of it.

The next CCP exam window is posted on the certifications page, and the Education Committee runs a short prep session in the weeks before each sitting that walks through the section breakdown in more detail than we have room for here. If you have taken the exam already and have a section you wish someone had warned you about, we would like to hear it — member feedback has shaped every revision of this credential so far.
