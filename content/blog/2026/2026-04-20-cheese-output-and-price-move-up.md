Two numbers moved in the same direction this month, and members keep asking us if that's a mistake. It isn't. This month's federal forecast raised the Class III price alongside the milk production estimate, and it raised the cheese price forecast alongside both. Usually more milk means a softer price. This spring it hasn't, and the reason has more to do with what's happening to the cheese than what's happening to the cow.

Cheese production is up from a year ago, and the composition of that increase matters. Mozzarella output is running well ahead of last year, and Italian-style cheeses more broadly are carrying the growth. Cheddar, by contrast, is down. When the volume gain concentrates in styles that move fast through food service and export channels rather than sitting in a cold-storage inventory number, the market reads it as demand catching up to supply rather than supply outrunning demand. That's a fine distinction on a spreadsheet and a real one in your milk check.

## What this means at the vat

If you're buying milk on a Class III-linked contract, the forecast bump is real money, but it's a forecast, not a settled price, and the last two years have taught most of our members to treat any single month's number as provisional. The federation's economics working group has been telling us the same thing it told us in January: build your spring plan around a range, not a point estimate, and revisit it after the next global dairy auction report rather than after every headline. Those auction reads have been choppy lately, up one cycle and down the next, and a single reading in either direction isn't a trend on its own.

For members selling finished cheese rather than raw milk, the more useful read is the production mix. If mozzarella and other Italian-style volumes are absorbing this spring's flush, that's relative headroom for cheddar-adjacent and aged styles that aren't competing with that surge for cave space, distributor attention, or case placement. A few members who make both told us this week they're shifting flush-season output toward their slower-turning aged lines precisely because the fast-moving styles are already well supplied elsewhere.

## The caution in the room

Nobody on our economics calls this week wanted to call it settled. The production estimate went up because dairy cow inventory is larger than expected, not because output per cow accelerated, and a bigger herd is a slower thing to unwind if demand cools later in the year. Members who lived through the 2025 feed-cost squeeze are, understandably, the least willing to call this a turning point after one good month. The federation isn't issuing guidance beyond what the data supports, and right now the data supports cautious optimism rather than a victory lap.

There's also a calendar reason to stay measured. Spring risk-management planning, locking in forward contracts, revisiting hedges, checking margin coverage elections, tends to happen in April regardless of what the numbers say that particular month, and a few of our members flagged that the timing of this favorable report is doing some of the persuading here. A good number that arrives right when you're already sitting down to plan feels more decisive than it should.

If you want the fuller numbers behind this month's report, including the international trade context that's been propping up cheese prices even as global dairy auction pricing softened slightly at the most recent reading, a future post will walk through the export side in more depth. In the meantime, if your contract renews before summer, this is a good week to have that conversation with your buyer while the forecast is still favorable.
