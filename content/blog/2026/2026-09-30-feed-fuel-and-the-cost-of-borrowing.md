For most of this year our message to members has been that the 2025 squeeze was easing. Feed was cheaper, energy had calmed down, and the conversations we were having were about succession and consolidation, not survival. This week's numbers are a reminder that easing is not the same as over.

## Milk is growing, and so is the bill

Milk production climbed 1.7 percent in July compared with a year earlier, and the milking herd was about 1.9 percent larger. That is plenty of milk, and it is one reason cheese prices have been under pressure while consumer confidence wavers.

The cost side is moving the other way. Feed costs are expected to keep rising through year-end, diesel is pushing up hauling charges, and borrowing costs are likely to climb. That is the farm on one end and the route that brings milk to your vat on the other.

None of this is a crisis. It is a margin story, and margin stories are the ones that decide who makes a profit in a flat year.

**Why a cheesemaker should care about a dairy farm's balance sheet.** Most of our members do not milk cows, but most of them buy milk from someone who does. When that supplier's feed bill climbs, you hear about it in one of three ways: a price conversation, a delivery change, or a quiet decision to cull deeper into the herd. A farmstead member who milks her own animals gets all three at once.

Financing is the quieter piece. Federal farm loan rates ticked up for October, with direct operating loans moving to 5.375 percent and direct ownership loans to 6.25 percent. Higher borrowing costs matter most to the member who is financing a cave expansion, a new vat, or the purchase of a retiring neighbor's creamery. Those are exactly the transactions our 2026 members are discussing.

"My loan officer used the phrase 'rate volatility' three times in one meeting," a small-creamery owner in the upper Midwest told us. "I stopped hearing the number and started hearing the word." She has decided to finish a cave retrofit before year-end rather than wait, not because the rate is low but because she does not trust next quarter's.

**Heat left a mark on the milk itself.** There is another input worth knowing about. A recent university study of millions of cow records found that heat stress cuts milk fat and protein as well as volume. A 10-point rise in the temperature-humidity index was associated with a 1.2 percent drop in yield but a 2.8 percent drop in annual revenue, because farms are paid on components. For you, that means a hot summer can show up in September as lower solids in the vat, even if the volume looks fine.

If your yields have felt off since July, check your supplier's component tests before you check your make sheet. The Milk Quality & Herd Health course covers how to read those reports and what to ask when they move.

**What we would do this week.**

- **Re-run your cost per pound.** Update milk, hauling and any financing costs in your wheel math. Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese walks through a model that takes a range of inputs.
- **Ask your milk supplier what their fall feed plan is.** A candid conversation now beats a surprise surcharge in December.
- **Look at your borrowing calendar.** If you expect to take on debt in the next year, talk to your lender about fixed versus variable terms before you need the money.

Our Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a running list of members willing to compare notes on all three. If you want to be on it, reply to this week's newsletter.

Costs ease and tighten in cycles, and this one is more tightening than crisis. A thoughtful plan will do more for your margin than any single price move.
