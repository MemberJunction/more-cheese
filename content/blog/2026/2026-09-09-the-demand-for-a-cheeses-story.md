Two pieces of market news crossed our desks this week, and they seem to point in different directions. Read together, they say something useful.

The first is the wholesale picture. In the week ending September 5, the federal price report showed cheddar blocks and butter edging lower while nonfat dry milk and dry whey climbed. That is the commodity side of the business, and a lot of you watch it closely.

The second is a market forecast published in mid-August. It sees the global cheese market growing from about $209 billion this year to roughly $329 billion by 2034. Forecasts are estimates, and different research houses land on different totals, so treat the numbers as a direction and not a promise. The reasoning behind them is what caught our attention.

## What the forecast says people want

The report names demand for fresh, homemade and specialty products as one of the things supporting artisan producers who use natural processing techniques. It also points to shoppers who favor cheeses with few or no preservatives, and to natural cheeses such as cheddar that people like for their taste, shelf life and cooking versatility.

The same forecast says much of the growth comes from everyday eating: snacking, pizza, sandwiches and home cooking. In other words, demand is widening, not narrowing. The artisan segment is one of several things pulling the market up, and it benefits from the same interest in natural, minimally processed food that sells the rest of the case.

## What we think it means for you

Commodity prices and specialty demand move on different clocks. A softer block price this week doesn't mean the shopper stopped caring about the wheel in your case. A counter manager at a member shop told us, "When the block price drops, my customers still ask where the cheese came from. Nobody has ever asked me about the Class III number at the register."

That is a single member's view, not a survey, and we would be careful not to overstate it. But it matches what the forecast describes: the story, the place and the method carry weight with buyers.

Three practical thoughts:

- **Know your story in two sentences.** Where the milk comes from, how you make it, and what a taster should notice. If you can't say it quickly, your counter staff can't either.
- **Put the story on paper.** Cards, shelf talkers and listings should say the same thing your voice does. Members who sell through a wholesale buyer often find the buyer repeats whatever is written down for them.
- **Mind your claims.** "Farmstead," "raw milk" and "aged" are all words a label can't use loosely. If you aren't sure how a term applies to your product, check the labeling requirements rather than guessing.

Provenance sells only when it is true and specific. A vague "handcrafted" tells a customer very little, while "milked at six, in the vat by eight" tells them a great deal.

If a portion of your revenue is tied to commodity-linked pricing, the softer block price is worth a look in your own numbers. We covered the split between cheese-linked and powder-linked prices in last week's industry note, and that split hasn't gone away. Specialty demand is a tailwind, not a cushion, and it won't help a creamery that hasn't priced its aged inventory correctly. Our Monday post on daily cave carrying costs is a good place to start.
