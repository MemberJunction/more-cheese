Late June is peak farmers' market weather in most of the country, and if you sell direct to consumers you are probably three or four weekends into the season by now. That is a good time to stop and check the parts of your setup that get set once in May and never looked at again until something goes wrong.

Start with the cooler. A tabletop refrigerated case or a well-packed cooler with gel packs both work, but only if you are actually checking the temperature during the market, not just when you pack up in the morning. A thermometer that lives in the case, not in a drawer at the shop, catches the slow drift that happens over a four-hour market in July heat. If you are cutting to order at the table, the same rule applies to your cutting surface and knife — a cold case does not help if the wheel spends fifteen minutes warming up on the counter between cuts.

## What the sign should say

Members selling at markets tell us the biggest recurring headache is not spoilage, it is labeling. A farmers' market table is still a point of sale, and most states expect the same basics you would put on a shop label: product name, ingredients, net weight, and your business name and address. Raw-milk products carry additional disclosure requirements that vary by state, and federal enforcement posture around interstate raw-milk sales has tightened this year, even where the underlying state statutes haven't changed. If you sell a raw-milk cheese at a market outside your home state, or a customer asks whether you ship, that is exactly the situation this year's enforcement attention is aimed at — worth a call to your state department of agriculture before the season gets any busier, not after an inspector asks.

Pricing signage matters too, and not just for compliance. A card that names the milk source, the age, and one plain-language flavor note ("nutty, firm, good for melting") sells more wheels than a card with just a price. Members who do this consistently tell us it cuts down on the same three questions being asked at every single customer interaction, which matters when there is a line and you are also trying to keep the register straight.

## A course built for exactly this

The federation's Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course starts a new cohort on June 28, and it runs through the heart of the summer market calendar rather than around it — the timing is deliberate. It covers the label and disclosure basics above in more state-specific detail, plus the parts that are harder to learn from a checklist: how to price for a market crowd versus a wholesale account, how to handle a slow market day without discounting your way into a bad habit, and what to do with unsold inventory that has been sitting in a cooler since 9 a.m.

If you are new to market selling this year, or you inherited a stand from someone else and have been running it on inherited habits, this is a good moment to enroll — the cohort structure means you will be comparing notes with other members mid-season, not in a classroom disconnected from the actual Saturday you just had. Registration is open through the member portal, and the Membership & Outreach Committee can point you to members in your region who have run a stand for a few seasons if you want a faster answer than the course schedule allows.
