A member in our raw-milk producer segment spent this spring doing something most of her peers put off for years: building a second aging room from scratch, sized specifically around record-keeping rather than just square footage. She asked us not to use her creamery's name, since the room isn't finished being fine-tuned, but she was happy to walk us through why she did it now.

"Everyone's been talking about the aging rule for two years like it's a single yes-or-no vote," she told us. "It isn't. It's closer to a slow rewrite, and the maker who's ready for either outcome is the one who wins, not the one who guessed right."

## Building around the paperwork, not just the wheels

Her old aging space was one room, one thermometer, one logbook that lived on a clipboard. The new one splits temperature zones by cheese type and, more importantly, ties every shelf to a batch number that traces straight back to a specific day's milk pickup and pH reading at the point the curd was set. That last detail matters more than it used to: recent peer-reviewed research has shown that aging alone, even well past the standard sixty days, isn't a guaranteed safeguard against every pathogen of current concern, and that batches made at a lower pH clear that bar more reliably. A maker who can show, batch by batch, what her pH was at set and how long each wheel actually aged is in a much stronger position than one who can only say "we follow the sixty-day rule" in general terms.

That's not a small lift for a small creamery. She spent more on sensors and a proper batch-tracking notebook than she originally budgeted, and she said the hardest part wasn't the construction, it was retraining herself to log a pH reading every single time instead of "the days it seemed worth writing down."

## What she'd tell a member starting later

Her advice to anyone thinking about doing something similar: start with the records system before the room. "You can build a beautiful cave and still not be able to answer the one question an inspector or a buyer actually asks, which is 'show me this specific wheel's history.' Fix that first, and the room follows."

She also pushed back gently on the idea that this kind of build-out is only for larger operations. Her new room is smaller than her old one; she gave up floor space to gain zoning and shelving she could label clearly. "People hear 'traceability' and picture a warehouse system. Mine is a whiteboard-sized spreadsheet and a set of shelf tags I made myself. The size of the room was never the hard part."

The Food Safety Committee has said the federal review of raw-milk aging criteria remains open, with no finalized change expected soon, but the direction of travel favors makers who can document their process in detail rather than lean on the age of the cheese alone. That's a useful thing to hear regardless of which way any eventual rule change lands: better batch records help you catch your own process drift long before an inspector or a regulator ever asks to see them, and they make a recall, if you're ever unlucky enough to need one, a matter of hours instead of days.
