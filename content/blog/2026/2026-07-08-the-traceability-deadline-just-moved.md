Members who have been quietly dreading the federal food traceability rule got some breathing room this year. The compliance date, originally set for January of this year, was extended by roughly two and a half years, and an exemption for hard cheeses made from pasteurized milk is now formally in place. Neither change eliminates the requirement for most of our membership's product lines. Both change how much runway a small creamery has to build the recordkeeping it needs.

Our Food Safety Committee has been tracking this rule since it first went final, mostly because the original design assumed a scale of operation that describes maybe a third of our membership. A twelve-employee farmstead creamery that ships direct to six regional retailers does not keep records the same way a national distributor does, and the comment letters our members filed over the past two years leaned hard on that gap — not asking for an exemption from safety, just for a timeline and a recordkeeping format that fits a smaller operation.

It is worth being precise about who the exemption actually covers, because we have already fielded a few hopeful questions that got the scope wrong. Hard cheese made from pasteurized milk is out of scope. Blue cheese, washed-rind cheese, fresh mozzarella, and any raw-milk cheese — pasteurized or not, in that last category — all remain fully subject to the rule once the new date arrives. If your line runs mostly soft-ripened or washed-rind styles, the extension is the part that helps you; there is no exemption coming for those categories.

## What the extension actually buys you

If your product falls on the traceability list — and most soft-ripened and fresh categories still do — you now have until the middle of 2028 rather than the start of this year to have lot-level tracking in place for receiving, transformation, and shipping events. That is real time to build the habit in pieces: start with receiving records this year, add transformation logging next year, and you are not scrambling in the last quarter before a hard date.

For anyone making a hard cheese that now qualifies for the exemption, do not throw out your recordkeeping in the meantime either. A distributor or retailer buying from you may still ask for lot traceability even where the federal rule no longer requires it, simply because their own systems are built around it. And if your creamery makes more than one style — a cheddar that is exempt alongside a washed-rind that is not — a single recordkeeping system across both product lines is going to be less error-prone than trying to run two different standards side by side. The traceability training our Education Committee rolled out earlier this year still applies regardless of which side of the exemption your product lands on — a clean chain of custody is worth having on its own.

If you want to weigh in before the exemption is finalized, the Membership & Outreach Committee is collecting member examples of how a shorter or longer compliance runway would actually affect your operation — concrete numbers help more than general objections. Reach out through the advocacy page if you have a specific case to share, especially if you ship across state lines.
