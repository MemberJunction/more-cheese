If you were watching your milk check in January and thinking it looked thin, you weren't imagining it. The federal all-milk price for that month came in at its lowest January reading in five years, and after the feed and energy costs that squeezed farmstead members through most of 2025, a soft opening to the new year was not what anyone wanted to see.

February looks different. The all-milk price moved up roughly five percent month over month, on the strength of stronger output per cow rather than a bigger herd. Exports have been part of the demand side of that story too — U.S. dairy exports were still running well ahead of the prior year as of the most recent trade data, with cheese exports up double digits and setting a new monthly volume record. Cheese production for 2025 also came in at a record for the country as a whole, which tells you demand didn't go anywhere even while margins were tight.

## What "turned" actually means for you

A one-month rebound is not a trend, and we're not going to tell members to relax the vigilance that got them through last year. But the shape of this rebound matters. It's coming from productivity — more milk per animal, not more animals — which historically holds up better than a herd-driven bump when feed costs move again. If you spent 2025 tightening your cost sheet, keep it tight. The members who came through last year in the best shape were the ones who didn't unwind their discipline the first time a number looked friendlier.

The record 2025 production number is worth sitting with for a second, because it cuts against the story a lot of members lived through last year. National cheese output grew even as feed and energy costs pushed some farmstead operations to the edge, which isn't a contradiction so much as a reminder that an industry total and an individual member's balance sheet can move in opposite directions at the same time. If your own creamery had a rough 2025 in a year the trade press is calling a record, that's not a sign you did something wrong — it's a sign the pain wasn't spread evenly.

If you sell into wholesale, the number that matters most to your own bottom line usually isn't the national headline figure — it's the specific class price that moves your milk check. That's a separate calculation from the all-milk average we opened with, and if you've never sat down and worked out how the two relate for your own contract, this is as good a week as any to do it with your accountant or a Membership & Outreach Committee volunteer.

## For members still catching up

We know the rebound doesn't erase what 2025 cost some of you. If your creamery deferred equipment maintenance, drew down a line of credit, or renegotiated supply terms to get through last year, a better milk check this spring doesn't automatically fix that. The federation's margin coverage program remains the most direct tool most members have for smoothing a bad stretch like the one behind us — if you haven't looked at your enrollment level since 2025, this is a reasonable week to do it, before the spring flush changes the math again.

Members with questions about how the February numbers interact with their own operation's cost structure should reach out to the Membership & Outreach Committee, which can point you to the right resource rather than trying to answer a farm-specific question in a newsletter. And if you're a member who came through 2025 in reasonably good shape and has thoughts on what worked, we'd like to hear them — those stories are useful to the members who are still catching up.
