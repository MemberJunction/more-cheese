Two pieces of market news landed within a few weeks of each other, and they are worth reading side by side. One is about what a very large overseas market is choosing to import. The other is about the first upward move in the federal base milk price since early summer.

Neither is a headline for most of our members. Both touch the way a small creamery plans the winter.

## What overseas buyers are choosing

Trade data published this summer showed one large import market bringing in more than 121,000 metric tons of cheese in the first half of 2026, up about a quarter on the same stretch of last year. Fresh cheese did most of the lifting. It grew by roughly 26 percent and made up nearly half of the total.

The reasons given are not about the cheeseboard. Demand is coming from bakeries and from tea-and-cream drinks, where a soft, mild, high-moisture cheese is an ingredient rather than a centerpiece. Total dairy imports in that market barely moved over the same period, which tells you that cheese is being chosen deliberately, not just swept up in a general rise.

The suppliers named were mostly large, southern-hemisphere and European exporters. We would not suggest that a farmstead creamery with one vat should chase that trade. The point for you is quieter: fresh and lactic styles have a reliable audience that is growing, and the buyers are paying attention to how those cheeses are made and kept.

If you make fresh cheese, that is a fair reason to look again at your own range. Our course **Fresh & Lactic Cheeses** covers the make-to-shelf timeline that matters most for a short-life product, including how a day of lost cold chain changes your sell-by window.

## The milk price turned the corner, a little

The advanced base price for fluid milk in October came in at $18.88 per hundredweight, up $1.84 from September and 84 cents above the same month last year. It is the first improvement since June. Adding the regional differentials, the October price averages around $23 across the milk marketing orders, with a spread of several dollars between the highest and lowest zones.

Two details matter if you buy or sell milk:

- The higher of two skim-based formulas set the base price again, and it was the one tied to powder and butter products, not cheese. The gap between the two skim factors widened to nearly $3.
- That base price feeds the regional uniform prices that will be announced over the coming weeks, so a producer's actual milk check will not match the headline number.

A regional market report from the end of last week adds that farm-level milk output in the West is steady, with only small gains, and that buyers in parts of the region are finding spot milk scarce.

We said in September that the milk check was moving in two directions at once. This is a small step in the supportive one. It does not erase a hard year, and we would not plan a budget around one month.

Our read, as a communications team and not as market analysts, is cautious. Costs have eased since the squeeze of last year, and a firmer milk price takes a little of the breathing room back. For makers who buy milk, the sensible move is to price your winter cheeses against the next few uniform-price announcements, not the current one.

For makers selling fresh styles, a growing export appetite is a reason to check your co-packing options and your labeling now, not in January.

A short list for the week:

1. Pull last winter's milk invoices and compare them with this month's.
2. Look at which of your cheeses are fresh or short-aged, and what share of revenue they carry.
3. Book a place in **Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese** if your price list has not moved in a year.
