We've heard from a few members this week asking a version of the same question: with cheese moving faster off some plants' lines than others right now, should they be pulling wheels out of the cave earlier than usual to meet demand, or holding the line on their normal aging schedule? It's a fair question for August, and the honest answer is that the market pressure and the affinage decision are two different problems wearing the same clothes.

Tightening finished-goods inventories this month mean a buyer calling about a firm order is more likely than usual to ask "what can you ship this week" instead of "what's your normal turnaround." That's real pressure, and it's tempting to answer it by moving young wheels out the door a little early. But a wheel pulled before it's ready doesn't just taste unfinished — it changes what your buyer expects from you next time, and that's a harder thing to walk back than a delayed shipment.

## What actually changes when demand tightens

The affinage fundamentals underneath this haven't changed just because the market got busier. Rind development, moisture migration, and flavor development still run on the clock the cheese sets, not the clock the order sheet sets. What can change, without compromising the wheel, is which lots you choose to prioritize for a given order. If you're carrying multiple batches at slightly different points in their aging window, the batch closest to peak — not the youngest one available — is the one that should go out first, even if it means telling a buyer their order ships in nine days instead of six.

This is also a good month to double-check your turning and humidity routine rather than your shipping calendar. A cave running a little warmer than usual, whether from summer heat or from more frequent door-opening during a busier stretch, accelerates surface activity in ways that can make a wheel look ready before it is. Members who track humidity and temperature twice a day during high-traffic months catch this drift before it shows up as an inconsistent batch; members checking once a day sometimes don't notice until a buyer does.

## Building the habit before you need it

If your cave inventory system right now is "which wheel looks ready when I walk by," this is the month to build something sturdier — a simple log noting when each batch went in, its target window, and what condition it's in at each check. It doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs to exist before the week you're fielding three rush orders at once and trying to remember which lot has three more weeks left in it.

The Affinage Fundamentals course covers exactly this: how to read a wheel's real readiness against a calendar date, how to build a simple tracking habit that survives a busy month, and how to have the conversation with a buyer about lead time without it sounding like an excuse. A few members who took it specifically because of a demand spike have told us the biggest change wasn't in the cave — it was in how confidently they could say "not yet" to an order.
