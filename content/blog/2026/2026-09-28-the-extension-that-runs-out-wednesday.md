On Wednesday, the one-year extension of the federal farm bill runs out. A new five-year bill cleared the upper chamber's agriculture committee on September 16, on a 12-11 party-line vote, and now waits for floor time. Nobody expects it to land before Wednesday, so for a while the extension's lapse is the practical reality.

Members have been asking us what that means for a creamery. The short answer is: less than the headlines suggest this week, and potentially a great deal more by the end of the year. Here is the plain version.

## What pauses on October 1

Two dairy programs stop when the extension does. The first is the federal dairy indemnity program. The second is the dairy forward-pricing program, which lets a handler sign a long contract at a fixed price without owing the minimum federal order price.

For the forward-pricing program, the dates matter. A contract set on or before September 30 that runs through September 30, 2029 is not affected. A handler establishing a new forward contract on or after October 1 would lose the exemption. If you sell milk or buy it under a fixed-price arrangement, and you were planning to sign or renew this fall, read your contract language now and ask your counterparty how they are treating it.

The margin-protection program for dairy producers is not affected. It was funded through 2031 in a separate law last year, so members who enrolled for coverage can stop worrying about that one.

## The bigger date is December 31

The part that keeps our Advocacy volunteers up at night is the end of the calendar year. If lawmakers pass neither a new bill nor another extension by then, dairy policy would revert to the old permanent-law statutes from the late 1930s and 1940s. Those laws call for price supports built on a parity formula from another century, and trade analysts describe the resulting numbers as far above anything the market has paid in modern times.

Nobody in the dairy trade wants that outcome, because it would scramble milk pricing and, with it, the cost of every vat you fill. Margins are already thin in places: a university study this summer found that heat stress trimmed milk fat and protein enough that revenue fell more than twice as fast as yield, and farms are paid on those components. Pricing noise on top of that is the last thing a small creamery needs. It is a deadline designed to focus attention, and it will probably do that. But "probably" is a thin thing to build a 2027 budget on. We are treating it as a planning risk, not a prediction.

"I'm not worried about next week," a farmstead cheesemaker in the Northeast told us at a recent member call. "I'm worried about the person who buys my milk fixing a price in January and not knowing what the rules will be in February. That uncertainty ends up in my invoice." She is describing a real pattern. When rules are in doubt, counterparties tend to shorten contract terms and pad prices.

**What the bill would do for cheese.** The upper chamber text carries a few provisions our members have asked about for years. It would back exporters' ability to keep using common food names in foreign markets, which matters to anyone whose cheese carries a style name rather than a place name. It would also redirect funds into export-promotion programs and keep a dairy nutrition incentive. None of that is final, and the lower chamber passed a different version in the spring, so the two will have to be reconciled.

Our Advocacy volunteers have been watching our "Dairy pricing reform" and "Interstate cheese shipment" topics most closely, since those are where members have sent the most comments this year.

**What we are asking of you.** Three small things, in order of effort:

1. **Check your contracts.** If a fixed-price milk or cheese contract is up for signature this fall, tell us what your counterparty says. We are collecting anonymous examples.
2. **Tell your own delegation.** A two-paragraph note from a producer who has actually priced a vat tends to beat a form letter. Say how many wheels you make and what a delay would cost you.
3. **Come to the advocacy session.** The Membership & Outreach Committee is hosting a short call this week for members who want help drafting their note. Watch your inbox for the time.

If you are planning around a hold-up, the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through building a margin model with a milk-price range rather than a single number. That is the right tool for a quarter like this one.

We will update this post if anything moves on the upper chamber floor. For now, the date to circle is December 31, not Wednesday.
