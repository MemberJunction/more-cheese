The conversations we're having with members this year sound different from the ones we had in 2025. Last year was about holding on — feed costs, energy bills, whether the creamery would make it through winter. This year, for a meaningful slice of our membership, the question has shifted to something else entirely: who runs this in five years, and what is it actually worth to them?

That question comes up more than you'd expect at farmstead scale. Recent reporting on the broader dairy sector puts the number of operators planning to step back within five years above one in four, and the reasons aren't only financial. A milking schedule that a founder built a life around doesn't always look like a life a successor wants to sign up for. But underneath the personal side of that decision sits a very practical one: a buyer, an heir, or a junior partner needs a real number, not a feeling, and most small cheese businesses have never priced themselves as a going concern.

## The course built for a narrower problem

Our *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* course was built for a narrower problem — helping a maker set a wholesale or counter price that actually covers milk, labor, and aging loss instead of guessing and hoping. It turns out the same worksheet does double duty for succession. If you can show a clean per-wheel margin across your product mix, broken out by channel, you've already built most of what a successor or a lender will ask to see. Members who've taken the course specifically for a handoff conversation tell us the surprising part wasn't the math — it was realizing how much of their pricing had been set by habit rather than by their actual cost structure.

That distinction matters more now than it did during the artisan boom years, when demand covered for a lot of pricing sloppiness. In a market where cheddar has spent this year holding steady rather than climbing, a successor inheriting a vague pricing sheet is inheriting your risk along with your cave. A clean one, by contrast, is something they can actually defend to a bank or a co-op partner, and something a junior partner can use to decide whether the business she's being offered a stake in is actually earning what everyone assumes it is.

The exercise also surfaces problems earlier than a founder might otherwise notice them. One line item that trips people up almost every cohort is aging loss — the wheels that shrink, crack, or simply don't come out right. A founder who has absorbed that loss mentally for twenty years, folding it into a vague sense of "some wheels just don't make it," often has no written number for it at all. Forcing that number onto paper changes the conversation with a successor from a feeling about the business to a fact about it, which tends to be a more comfortable place to negotiate from for both sides.

## A starting point, not a full plan

The course doesn't cover estate planning, business valuation, or the legal structure of a transfer — none of that is ours to teach, and a producer weighing a real handoff should be working with their own accountant and attorney on those pieces. What it gives you is the operating number underneath all of that: a defensible, current picture of what each product actually earns. If you're a member thinking about a transition in the next few years, that's the piece worth building first, while you're still the one holding the pencil.

It's worth starting well before you think you need to. Members who've gone through a handoff tell us the pricing work took longer than expected precisely because old habits are hard to untangle from real costs, and a rushed version built the week before a sale conversation rarely holds up to scrutiny.
