For most of last year, the question we heard most often on export calls wasn't "what's the rate" — it was "what will the rate be by the time this container clears." That question has quieted down. The tariff line most of our exporting members track has now held at the same level for six straight weeks, the longest stretch of stability since the trade turbulence that opened 2025. It is a small thing to notice and, for anyone who ships pallets on contracts signed months in advance, a genuinely large relief.

Steadiness matters more than the number itself. A member who runs exports for a mid-sized creamery told us her buyers overseas had started asking for shorter contract terms last year — three months instead of twelve — because nobody wanted to be locked into a rate that might not exist by delivery. That behavior hasn't fully reversed yet, but she says two of her longest-standing accounts have gone back to asking about annual terms for the first time since early 2025. That's a vote of confidence a spreadsheet won't capture as cleanly as a phone call will.

## What's actually holding, and what isn't

Worth being precise here, because "stable" and "resolved" are not the same word. The rate itself has not moved, but the paperwork underneath it has — a wave of technical corrections and clarifications has been working through the federal register in recent weeks, mostly narrowing which products certain quota categories cover. None of it changes what our members owe today. All of it is worth reading if you export, because a category boundary redrawn in March can matter more to your bottom line than a headline rate that stays put. The Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding questions on this and can point you to the specific notices relevant to your product category.

Milk production is also running close to 3% ahead of last year nationally, which keeps downward pressure on domestic prices even as the all-milk price recovered somewhat from January's low, according to this week's federal dairy outlook briefing. That combination — more supply at home, steadier terms abroad — is part of why 2026 has felt less like firefighting and more like planning, at least for members who weathered the closures of last year. It's also worth noting the northeast storm that buried the region in snow this week is a reminder that trade stability doesn't protect a shipment from a truck stuck on the interstate; a wheel that sits an extra two days in an unconditioned trailer during a blizzard is a different wheel than the one you shipped, so a call to your distributor beats an assumption this week.

## What to do with six quiet weeks

Stability, even temporary, is a planning window. If you've been avoiding a longer-term supply or distribution contract because the tariff picture felt too unsettled to commit to, this is a reasonable stretch to revisit that conversation. It won't necessarily last — nothing in trade policy this year has lasted more than a season — but for now, the ground is holding, and that's worth building on while it does.
