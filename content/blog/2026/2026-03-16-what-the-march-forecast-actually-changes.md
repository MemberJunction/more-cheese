Mid-March brought an update most members won't read directly but will feel through their milk
check anyway. The federal forecast for the 2026 all-milk price moved up, and most of the
dairy product price forecasts that feed into it moved up too. At the same time, the weekly
cheese market report showed block cheddar at its highest print since last November, with
barrels close behind. Two numbers, moving the same direction, on the same week. That is worth
a closer look before anyone recalculates a spring budget off a single headline.

## Two forecasts are not one forecast

The production forecast and the price forecast are doing different jobs, and it is easy to
read them as a single story when they are not. A higher production forecast means the
government expects more milk on the ground this year, mostly from herds in the Central region
that have been offering more milk to cheese plants than they were a few months ago. A higher
price forecast means the government expects that milk, plus the cheese made from it, to sell
for more than it previously projected. Both things can be true without canceling out, and
both are still forecasts, not settled numbers. If your make sheet or your milk contract is
tied to one index rather than the other, know which one, because they will not always move
together for the rest of the year.

For a small creamery, the more useful number this month may be the spread between the two
figures, not either one alone. A rising production forecast with only a modest bump to the
all-milk price is a market with room to soften again if a few more herds decide to ship more.
That is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to keep your cost-per-wheel math current instead
of running on a number from January.

## What we'd actually watch for

If you buy milk rather than milk your own herd, ask your supplier whether the additional
volume behind the production forecast is showing up as more offers, the way it has in parts
of the Central region, or as tighter terms on your existing contract. If you sell finished
cheese, a firmer block and barrel market is good news for your wholesale price, but it can
take weeks to show up at retail, and a retailer holding older inventory may not move on shelf
price as fast as the commodity market did. Neither gap is a mistake. It is just the normal lag
between a commodity print and what shows up in your ledger.

We'd also flag one thing for members thinking this settles the "is 2026 a good year" question:
several trade outlets covering the same forecast this month described the underlying picture as
still tight on margin, even with prices firmer than last fall. A higher price forecast paired
with a higher production forecast is consistent with a market that stabilizes without
necessarily getting easy. That fits what the federation has been hearing directly from members
since January: costs have eased from where they were during the worst of last year's squeeze,
but nobody we've talked to is calling this a rebound.

If you want to sanity-check your own numbers against the federal releases rather than a
summary, the weekly market news reports are public and dated, and they are the same source
