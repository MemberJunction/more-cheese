If your milk check arrived a little heavier this month, you are not imagining it. The federal Class I base price climbed past $20 per hundredweight this spring, and it did that at the same time forecasters were raising their production estimates for the year. Normally more milk means softer prices. This spring it has not worked that way, and members have been asking us why.

The short answer, as best the federation can piece together from public market reporting, is demand. Domestic processors are pulling harder on milkfat and protein than the supply models expected, export outlets have held up, and the most recent global trade auction showed prices moving up rather than down, with butter and mozzarella both posting gains. Put those together and you get a market that is absorbing a bigger milk pool without giving back much ground on price. Association economists we've spoken with, in the loose sense that means the handful of members who track this for a living and share notes with each other, are calling it a rebalancing rather than a shortage. Whether it holds through the fall is a separate question, and one the federation is not in the business of forecasting for you.

## What it means for your case

For members buying milk rather than milking it, a firmer price is a cost-side headache no matter how it got there. If you have not revisited your wholesale sheet since the price moved past $20, this is a reasonable week to do it, especially on any wheel that ages long enough that today's milk cost will still be sitting in inventory when it finally sells. Our *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* course walks through building that lag into a price sheet instead of discovering it at the register.

For members who are also producers, the news is more encouraging, at least for now. A firmer all-milk price is the first real relief a lot of farmstead operations have seen since the cost squeeze of 2025, and several members have told us it is the first spring in three years where the milk side of the ledger and the cheese side moved in the same direction.

## Watch the export line, too

The same trade data showing firmer domestic prices also showed export demand holding rather than slipping, which matters more than it might seem for members who do not export a single wheel. A healthy export channel is one of the things keeping the domestic milk pool from backing up onto the wholesale market and pushing prices the other way. If you want to understand how that channel actually works, our *Export Documentation Essentials* course is built for members who buy or sell domestically but want to understand what happens at the border.

We will keep watching the public reporting through the summer and flag anything that looks like it is turning. In the meantime, price your summer case like the milk under it costs what it costs today, not what it cost in January.
