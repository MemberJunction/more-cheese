If you make raw-milk cheese and you've ever wondered why your 61-day wheel can cross a state line but your 59-day wheel can't, you've already found the rule the federation's Interstate Cheese Shipment petition is aimed at. Federal rules allow raw-milk cheese to move across state lines once it's aged at least 60 days and clearly labeled as unpasteurized. Anything younger stays put, no matter how the individual states involved feel about it. It's one of the few food-safety lines in this business that's a single national number rather than a patchwork, and the petition isn't asking to change that number. It's asking for the labeling and paperwork around it to be consistent from state to state, which right now it isn't.

## What's actually being asked for

A member shipping a 60-plus-day wheel from a Midwestern creamery to a coastal retailer today may need a different label format, a different lot-tracing document, or a different affidavit depending on which state's receiving inspector shows up. None of that touches the 60-day aging line itself. The petition, which our advocacy volunteers have been circulating at events and through the federation's action alerts for several cycles now, asks for a single model label and shipping-document format that any state would accept, so a maker isn't reformatting paperwork for every distributor relationship.

That's a modest ask by design. Federal legislative activity around raw milk has picked up again this year, including a proposed bill that would touch interstate raw milk more broadly, and the federation's Standards Committee has been deliberately keeping this petition narrow so it doesn't get pulled into that larger fight. Committee volunteers have told us the paperwork problem is real and solvable on its own; the aging-line debate is a different, much bigger conversation the federation isn't picking a side in.

The timing is not entirely coincidental. There's been fresh attention this year on whether the 60-day aging window does everything it's assumed to do, after research on a different pathogen raised questions about how reliably aging alone clears certain risks at the margins of the window. The federation has been careful to keep that research question separate from the shipment petition. One is a paperwork-consistency ask that doesn't touch the science. The other is a much larger federal conversation about the standard itself, and members should not read the petition as taking a position on it either way.

Ask five makers who ship interstate about their paperwork, and you'll get five slightly different answers about what a given state's inspector actually wants to see at the dock. Some states accept a simple manufacture-date stamp on the wheel itself. Others want a separate affidavit attesting to the aging period, notarized in some cases, plus a lot number that ties back to a specific vat and make date in the creamery's own records. A maker selling into three or four states ends up keeping three or four versions of what is, functionally, the same piece of information, and redoing that paperwork for every new distributor relationship is exactly the kind of friction that discourages a small creamery from expanding past its home state in the first place.

## Where it stands

The petition has been open for signatures for a while, and it's picked up renewed attention this spring as more small and mid-sized aged-cheese makers start shipping wholesale for the first time. If you've filled out three different versions of essentially the same shipping affidavit for three different state inspectors, this is the effort meant to fix that, and it's a place where a specific example from your own paperwork is more useful to the Standards Committee than a general complaint.

If you want to add your name or just want to see the current draft of the model label format, the Standards Committee is the contact point, and the petition itself is still open. It won't move the 60-day line. It might save you an afternoon of paperwork on your next interstate pallet.
