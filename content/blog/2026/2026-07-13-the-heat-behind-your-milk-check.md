If your milk supplier called this month with a smaller-than-usual load or a slightly later delivery, you are not imagining a pattern. A run of unusually hot weeks has pushed several dairy regions into heat stress territory, and the federation has been fielding more calls than usual from members trying to figure out whether it is a blip or a trend.

Heat stress is straightforward biology with an unforgiving effect on the milk check. Cows eat less when it is hot, because digestion generates its own heat and an animal already working to cool itself has no appetite for adding to the load. Less feed intake means less milk, and it also tends to mean lower butterfat and protein in what does come through the line — the two components that matter most if you are paying on a component basis. Market reporting for the week of our conference lead-up showed spot milk tightening in multiple regions, with manufacturers who needed extra volume paying a premium to get it, and cream multiples running higher than normal for this time of year as component levels dipped along with the heat. Cheddar block prices held mostly steady week to week even as butter firmed, which is its own small signal: a tighter, more volatile milk supply doesn't move every dairy product the same direction or at the same speed, and it is worth watching cheese and butter separately rather than assuming one tells you about the other.

## What tighter supply means for your sheet

For a small creamery buying milk rather than milking your own herd, the practical effect is a cost line that moves without much warning. A few things worth checking before you finalize July's pricing:

- **Confirm your supplier's component testing cadence.** If butterfat and protein are down even slightly, your yield per hundredweight of milk changes, and that is worth knowing before you commit to a wholesale order at last month's math.
- **Ask about substitution language in your contract.** Some supply agreements let a distributor fill a shortfall from a different farm on short notice. That is usually fine for a commodity cheese, but if your recipe depends on a specific herd's forage or a particular somatic cell profile, it is worth flagging now rather than after a batch comes out wrong.
- **Watch cream separately from fluid milk.** Members making anything cream-enriched — a triple-crème, a bloomy rind that gets a cream wash — are seeing that line move faster than the base milk price. Several members have told us they are holding cream-heavy recipes to smaller batches until the multiple settles.

None of this is unique to any one region this year. Reporting through mid-July showed the same pattern in dairy areas across Europe, where extended heat and dry conditions cut weekly collection numbers in places that don't usually see this kind of volatility in midsummer. It is a reminder that a make room in Wisconsin and an affineur's cave in the Jura are, in a real sense, downstream of the same weather.

## The part that usually gets missed

The instinct when a milk check tightens is to look at what you're paying for milk. It is worth also looking at what you're charging for cheese. A retail buyer who locked in a summer price based on spring milk costs may not have room in their margin for a mid-season adjustment, and a hard conversation now is cheaper than an unprofitable case later. The federation's Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly this kind of mid-cycle repricing, including how to have that conversation with a buyer without losing the account.

Heat waves pass. Component tests come back up once temperatures drop and appetite returns, usually within a few weeks of the weather breaking. But the volatility is a useful annual reminder to build a little slack into a summer cost sheet rather than discovering the gap in August's invoice.
