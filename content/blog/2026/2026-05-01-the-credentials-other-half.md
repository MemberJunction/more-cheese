Ask a member what the Certified Cheesemonger credential tests, and most guess some version of "can you identify cheeses by taste and tell a good one from a bad one." That's in there, but it's not most of it. A meaningful share of the credential is built around the part of the counter job that never shows up in a tasting panel: reading a customer, adjusting a pitch, and knowing when to lead with a fact instead of a story.

That split surprises members who come into the credential expecting it to be mostly sensory work. It isn't a coincidence. The credential was built around what actually keeps a specialty counter running, and running a counter well has always been at least half about conversation, not just cheese.

## The sensory half, and why it isn't the whole exam

The sensory component is real and it's rigorous — texture, aroma, rind condition, defect identification, and the ability to place an unfamiliar wheel into a family of styles by tasting it cold. Members preparing for this section spend real hours with structured tasting flights, usually working through defects deliberately introduced so they learn to name what's gone wrong, not just that something has. This is the part of the credential that looks the most like what people expect a cheese exam to be, and it's also the part candidates tend to over-prepare for relative to how much of the final score it actually represents.

Recordkeeping shows up in this section too, in a way people don't expect. A monger who can tell you a wheel's age but not when it was last checked for condition, or who can't tell a shift lead which case needs restocking first, is missing something the exam checks for directly alongside flavor identification.

## The customer-facing half people underestimate

The other half covers case management, storage and handling, and — increasingly relevant this year — the skill of matching a pitch to the customer standing in front of you instead of running the same script for everyone who walks up. Trade coverage this spring has repeated the same finding independently: younger shoppers are asking about protein content and where an animal was raised earlier in the conversation than they used to, sometimes before they ask anything about flavor, while longtime regulars still want the origin story first. The credential's customer-facing module spends real time on exactly that kind of read — not because a customer's first question defines the whole sale, but because getting the opening line right is what keeps someone at the counter long enough to hear the rest.

If you're considering the credential, don't assume a strong palate gets you most of the way there. Pair your tasting practice with time actually working a counter under supervision if you can get it, since the exam's customer-interaction component rewards habits built on the floor more than habits built from a study guide alone. The Education Committee maintains the current exam schedule and prerequisite coursework — reach out before you register if you're not sure whether you've covered the case-management material yet.
