A year and a half ago, the member we're spotlighting this week was working an office job with no
connection to dairy at all. Today she runs the vat room three days a week at a small creamery in
the upper Midwest, and this fall was the first time she made a full production run without her
mentor standing next to her. We asked her what that was actually like, on the record but without
her name, at her request — she'd rather members focus on the apprenticeship path than on her
personally.

"The recipe was never the hard part," she said. "I could recite the make sheet in my sleep by
month three. What nobody tells you is how much of the job is judging things that aren't written
down anywhere — whether the curd is a minute early, whether the cave feels a half-degree off from
what the thermometer says, whether a wheel that looks fine today is going to develop a problem in
three weeks." She credits an affinage-focused course through the federation with giving her a
vocabulary for those judgment calls, even if the calls themselves still come down to her own nose
and hands.

## The part of fall nobody warned her about

Cooler nights and shifting humidity make early autumn one of the harder stretches for cave
management even for an experienced maker, and she says her first solo season landed right in the
middle of it. "August was easy. October is when the cave stopped behaving the way it did all
summer, and I had to relearn it in real time instead of on a training schedule." She adjusted her
turning frequency twice in three weeks and called her old mentor once, mostly to confirm she
wasn't imagining a problem that wasn't there. "She told me the cave was testing me, not
malfunctioning. That helped more than the actual advice did."

Her advice for anyone earlier in an apprenticeship is blunt: keep a written log even when it feels
unnecessary. "I wrote down every adjustment I made this fall, even the ones that turned out to be
wrong. Now I have a record of what a wrong call actually looked like, which is worth more than a
textbook description of a right one." She's also become an advocate, somewhat to her own surprise,
for staying connected to the federation's course offerings past the point of finishing a single
credential — she's already planning to enroll in a second course before the next production
season starts.

Part of what makes her story useful to other members isn't the cheese itself but the shape of the
first year: three months of following someone else's judgment calls, then a slow handoff, then one
season where the mentor genuinely isn't in the room. She said the hardest week wasn't the one with
the actual problem — a slightly off-track batch she caught and corrected — but the week before it,
when she wasn't sure yet whether her own instincts were reliable enough to trust.

Apprenticeship stories like hers are common enough that we don't need a name attached to make the
point: a first solo season is where a lot of makers either find their footing or decide the craft
isn't for them, and having a course, a mentor on the phone, and a stubborn written log all in place
seems to matter more than any one of them alone. If you're mentoring an apprentice through their
own first fall, our Education Committee has materials built specifically for that handoff period —
worth asking for before the next cave season starts testing someone new.
