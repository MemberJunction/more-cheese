Bob Kowalski has been a federation member since 2013, which puts him among the longer tenures in a membership that skews younger every year. As a regional sales director for a dairy supply distributor, he's not a cheesemaker himself — but he's spent thirteen years driving a territory of small creameries, which means he's watched more start-ups open, struggle, and either settle in or close than almost anyone we talk to.

We asked him what's actually different about his territory now compared to when he started.

"The easy answer is that there are just more of them," he told us. "When I started this route, I could name every cheesemaker on it from memory, and half of them were doing it as a side business to a dairy farm. Now I've got accounts that are cheese-only, no farm attached, and they're buying serious equipment in year one instead of scraping by on borrowed gear." Recent industry surveys of the artisan and specialty sector back up what he's describing on the ground — the number of small producers has grown steadily for years, and the newer ones tend to be closer to full-time operations from the start rather than a sideline.

## What hasn't changed

Not everything is new, in his telling. The two hardest problems for a small creamery — cash flow in the first eighteen months, and finding a distributor or retail account willing to take a chance on an unknown label — are exactly the same problems he saw when he started. "Nobody wants to be the first name in the cooler for a brand-new maker," he said. "That's still true. What's changed is there's more of a path now — competitions, certifications, this federation — that gives a new producer something to point to besides 'trust me, it's good.'"

That's part of why he's stayed engaged with us for as long as he has, even though his own title has nothing to do with a vat or a press. A supply-side member with a long memory is useful precisely because he's not selling his own cheese — his read on which creameries are thriving and which are struggling isn't shaded by rooting for a competitor to fail.

## Advice for anyone earlier in the territory

Asked what he tells a first-year account, Bob didn't hesitate: get your labeling and food-safety paperwork right before you need it, not after an inspector asks for it. "I've watched good cheese get pulled from a shelf over a labeling problem that had nothing to do with the cheese," he said. It's advice that lines up with what our Food Safety Committee has been saying in its own materials for years, which is probably why he keeps saying it.

If you're newer to the federation and want a read on your own region that isn't coming from a spreadsheet, members like Bob — the ones who've covered the same territory for over a decade — are often the fastest way to get one. Our Membership & Outreach Committee can help connect newer members with a longer-tenured one in a similar line of work; it's a small thing to ask for, and it's exactly what it's there for.
