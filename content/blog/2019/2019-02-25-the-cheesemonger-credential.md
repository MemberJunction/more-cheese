Every winter we get the same email: someone behind a cheese counter, a few years in, wondering whether Certified Cheesemonger is worth the study time. Short answer: yes, if you're staying in retail or wholesale cheese for the long haul. Longer answer takes the rest of this post.

Certified Cheesemonger is not a tasting badge, and it's worth saying that plainly because a lot of members assume it is before they sit down with the study guide. It tests what you'd actually need to know running a counter well — cheese families and how they're made, storage and handling that keeps a case from turning on you, safe cutting and wrapping practice, and enough sales and pairing knowledge to steer a customer toward something they'll actually enjoy. It's closer to a working knowledge exam than a connoisseur's quiz, and members who've sat it tell us the storage and handling section is where people who've only ever tasted cheese — never managed a case — get caught out. One industry-wide credential in this space is administered as a multi-hour written exam of well over a hundred questions; ours is scoped tighter, but the seriousness of the format is a fair comparison.

## Who this is for, and how members are studying

If your job title has "counter," "monger," "buyer," or "cheese program" in it, this is squarely aimed at you. It's also a reasonable credential for a newer cheesemaker who wants to understand the retail side of where their product ends up, and for wholesale reps who spend more time explaining cheese to buyers than making it. It is not aimed at consumers, and we'd steer a curious home cook toward our courses instead of the exam. The credential sits below Certified Cheese Professional (CCP) in our ladder — CCP goes deeper and expects broader industry exposure — so Certified Cheesemonger is the sensible first stop if you're newer to the trade or want something scoped to counter work specifically.

The federation's exam-prep pairing is *Sensory Foundations* plus *Building a Cheese Counter*, both of which run in multiple cohorts through the year — check the current course calendar for the next start date. Members who passed on the first try mostly describe the same routine: work the storage-and-handling material hardest, because it's the section that trips up people with strong palates and thin operations knowledge, and build a habit of tasting with intention rather than just tasting a lot. A rotation through your own case, naming what you're smelling and why it changed since last week, does more for you than an expensive field trip.

Study time varies. Members with a few years already on a counter have told us they were ready in six to eight weeks of steady evening study; people newer to the trade have taken a full season, and that's completely normal — this isn't a credential to rush. A handful of members study in pairs, quizzing each other on family and rind types over a closing shift, and say it made the material stick faster than solo flashcards ever did.

If you're weighing it, the practical first step is enrolling in *Sensory Foundations* — it's the on-ramp for this credential and for several others on our ladder, so it's rarely wasted effort even if you decide to wait on the exam itself. From there, talk to the Education Committee about which cohort fits your schedule, and ask a member who's already certified what surprised them. Most are happy to talk shop, especially about the parts of the exam nobody warns you about.
