Members ask us fairly often whether the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate is worth the time for a small operation, especially one that's been making cheese the same careful way for years without an incident. Our answer is the same every time: the certificate isn't really testing whether you're careful. It's testing whether you can prove it to someone who has never set foot in your plant.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. A hazard analysis and critical control point plan — the "HACCP" in the certificate's name — asks you to walk through your process step by step and write down where something could actually go wrong: a pasteurization step that has to hit a specific temperature and hold it, a cooling step that has to happen fast enough, an ingredient that could carry an allergen your customers don't expect. For each of those points, you write down what you monitor, how often, and what you do if a reading falls outside the range you've set. None of that is new information to an experienced maker. What's new is having it on paper, consistently, in a form someone else can follow.

## Why the preventive-controls framing matters

The certificate's structure leans on the preventive-controls approach that's become the baseline expectation for food manufacturers generally: identify the hazards, put controls in place before the hazard becomes a problem, and keep records that show the controls actually ran, not just that they exist on paper somewhere. A technical amendment finalized this spring cleaned up some of the cross-references in that framework, which is a reminder that the rule text itself keeps getting small updates even years after the original compliance dates passed — worth knowing if you're working from an old printout of the regulation rather than checking it fresh. For an aged raw milk cheese, your plan might lean on documented supplier controls on the milk source, since there's no later kill step for pathogens the way there is with pasteurization. For a pasteurized cheese, the pasteurization step itself typically carries more of that weight, and your plan should reflect where the real risk actually sits in your specific process, not a generic template borrowed from somewhere else.

Members who've gone through the coursework tell us the most useful part wasn't learning the hazard categories, it was the practice of writing a plan for their own plant instead of adapting someone else's. A monitoring log that was clearly written for a different creamery, with the names swapped, tends to fall apart under a real inspection, because the critical control points rarely match exactly.

## Recertification and what changes each cycle

The credential requires periodic recertification rather than a one-time exam, largely because guidance and expectations shift and a plan written five years ago may no longer reflect current practice at your plant, let alone current regulatory expectations. If your last certification predates a significant change to your process — a new product line, a new supplier, a new aging room — that's worth flagging to the Food Safety Committee before your next renewal cycle, since your underlying plan likely needs updating regardless of where your certificate stands.

If you're weighing whether to start the certificate this year, the honest pitch is this: it won't make your cheese any safer than the care you're already putting into it. It will make that care visible and defensible the day someone asks you to prove it. Reach out to the Food Safety Committee if you'd like to talk through where your own plant's biggest gaps in documentation probably are.
