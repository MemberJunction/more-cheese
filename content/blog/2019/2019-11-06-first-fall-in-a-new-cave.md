One of our longer-tenured members moved to a new creamery earlier this year, and this is the first fall she's spending managing an aging room somebody else built. She's run quality programs for over a decade, but she told us the hardest part of a mid-career move isn't the title change — it's inheriting a cave with its own quirks and no one left on staff who remembers why the airflow was routed the way it was.

Her new employer, a farmstead operation that had been running on informal habit more than written procedure, brought her in specifically to get a swab-and-sanitation schedule on paper. She spent her first two months just observing before changing anything, which she says is the part people skip. "Everyone wants to walk in and fix things on day one. But if you don't know why a shelf gets rotated a certain way, you might be about to break something that's actually working."

## What she found

The biggest surprise wasn't a hazard — it was a gap in recordkeeping. The cave had a real, functioning routine, it just lived entirely in one person's memory instead of on paper. That's a common condition in smaller operations, and it's exactly the kind of thing a documented environmental monitoring program is built to catch before it becomes a problem, whether through staff turnover, a bad week, or a health inspection that asks for records nobody kept. Good monitoring programs rotate sampling sites rather than swabbing the same easy spots every time, on the theory that a contamination source hiding somewhere routine never touches will stay hidden indefinitely if you never look there. That was true here — her predecessor's informal habit happened to skip two drains that turned out to need attention.

She's spent the fall converting verbal habits into an actual swab map, with sampling points that rotate through zones instead of repeating the same handful of surfaces, and a written schedule that survives her taking a week off. She's also had to relearn some fundamentals for a building she didn't design. Humidity behaves differently in a converted dairy barn than it did at her last job, and a shelving layout that made sense for one producer's wheel sizes didn't fit hers. Members who've made a similar mid-career jump have told her the same thing: give it a full year before you trust your instincts in a new room, because one season isn't enough to know how a cave behaves through all four.

## What this means if you're the one inheriting a cave

If you're new to a facility this year — whether you changed jobs or just took over more responsibility at the one you're already at — her advice is to resist the urge to standardize everything to what worked before. Write down what's actually happening first, even the parts that seem odd, and ask why before you change it. Only then start building the schedule you actually want, and build in rotation from the start rather than settling into your own set of easy, familiar swab points.

She also flagged something specific to fall: as outdoor humidity drops and everyone starts running heat inside, airflow patterns in an aging room can shift enough to change where moisture collects, which is exactly when a stale sampling routine is most likely to miss something new. If your own facility's monitoring plan hasn't been reviewed since a change in season, staff, or building, this is a reasonable week to take a fresh look at it rather than waiting for a formal audit to force the issue.

For members doing exactly this kind of transition work, the Food Safety Committee has reviewed swab-mapping approaches with several creameries this year and can point you toward the Listeria Control in Small Creameries course, which walks through building a documented sanitation schedule from scratch. If you inherited someone else's cave this year, that's a reasonable place to start.
