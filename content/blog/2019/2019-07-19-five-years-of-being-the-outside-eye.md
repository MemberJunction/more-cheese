Most of our member spotlights land on someone standing over a vat. This one is about someone a lot of vats depend on without ever meeting her: a food safety consultant, an ICF member for five years now, who spends her days telling small creameries what's wrong with their food safety plan before an inspector does.

"Most of the material out there was written with a large processor in mind," she told us this week, asking that we keep her practice unnamed since most of her client list is small and word travels fast in this business. "A ten-page plan built for a plant running three shifts doesn't map cleanly onto someone with one vat and a part-time helper. My job is mostly translation — same underlying requirements, sized to fit an operation that's actually in front of me."

## The mistake she sees most often

Asked what trips up a first-time plan, she didn't hesitate: people write down what they think an inspector wants to hear instead of what they actually do. "I'd rather see an honest, slightly rough description of your real process than a polished plan copied from a template that doesn't match your equipment. The gap between the paper and the floor is exactly where problems hide, and it's exactly what gets flagged."

Raw milk producers, she says, carry a heavier version of this problem, since there's no pasteurization step to lean on as a single point of control. "You're managing risk across several smaller steps instead of one big one — aging time, pH, moisture. A raw milk plan that looks thin is almost always missing one of those, not missing the big obvious step, because there isn't one."

## What five years has taught her about the work

She says the biggest change in her own approach over five years has been slowing down the first conversation. "Early on I wanted to jump straight to fixing the plan. Now I spend the first meeting just watching someone's actual process before I say anything about paperwork. Half the time the real fix is a five-minute change at the vat, not a rewrite of the whole document."

She's quick to draw a boundary around her role, too. "I'm not a lawyer and I'm not the inspector. I can tell you what the requirements generally ask for and help you build a plan that reflects your real operation — I can't tell you you're in the clear. That final call belongs to your regulator, and I'd be doing you a disservice pretending otherwise."

She joined the federation, she says, mostly for the reverse reason most members do — not to find clients, but to stay current on what small creameries were actually running into month to month, rather than relying only on what shows up in her own client calls. "If I only ever heard from the people already worried enough to hire a consultant, I'd have a skewed picture. Being a member means I hear from a wider slice than that."

For members working through a first food safety plan or revisiting an old one that's never quite fit, our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate coursework covers the same fundamentals she starts every client with, and the Food Safety Committee maintains a list of member-recommended templates as a starting point — not a substitute for the work of matching a plan to your own floor.
