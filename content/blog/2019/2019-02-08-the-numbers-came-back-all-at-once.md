The federal agriculture statistics that went quiet in late December came back this morning, and
they came back in a heap. The scheduled February production and supply-and-demand estimates were
published alongside three reports that should have landed in January, and the January
supply-and-demand edition was not published at all — its material was folded into today's.

For members who buy feed, contract milk, or price a year of production against a forward curve,
this is not an abstraction. For about six weeks there was no official read on stocks, on plantings,
or on the size of anything, and the numbers our members use to argue with a lender simply did not
exist. Several of you told us you priced Q1 on instinct. One farmstead member put it more bluntly
than we will reprint.

## Why a data gap costs a small creamery more than a large one

Large operators have analysts, private data subscriptions, and enough volume that being wrong by
a cent is survivable. A creamery taking in milk from two farms and making eight styles has none of
those things. It has a spreadsheet, a bank that wants a projection, and a margin that a trade
press piece this week described — accurately — as slim across most of the artisan sector, with the
overwhelming majority of makers naming profitability as an active worry.

So when the public signals stop, the smallest members absorb the most risk. That is the argument
the federation will keep making, and it is not a partisan one: agricultural statistics are
infrastructure. They should be funded and published the way a road is maintained, whatever else is
happening.

The second piece of this is programme timing. The new farm bill authorised a redesigned dairy
safety net late last year, and the funding lapse arrived almost immediately afterwards. Members
who need that programme are being asked to make coverage decisions for a year that is already
five weeks old, against sign-up dates that have not yet been announced. We have asked, in writing,
for two things: that sign-up dates be published as early as possible even if they are far out, and
that the eligibility window be written so that nobody is penalised for the weeks when the offices
were closed and the guidance did not exist.

## And it has been a brutal winter on top of it

None of this landed on a quiet month. The arctic outbreak at the end of January was the coldest
spell parts of the upper Midwest have seen since the 1990s, with wind chills capable of causing
frostbite in minutes. Members reported frozen lines, hauling delays, grain facilities shut, and
barns run in conditions nobody plans for. The knock-on effects on winter forage will not be fully
visible until spring.

Two practical notes from the Food Safety Committee, which has been fielding calls all week. First,
if a cold room, a wash line, or a milk-cooling system ran outside its normal range during the
freeze, that belongs in your records now rather than reconstructed later from memory. Second, if
you held milk longer than usual because a haul did not arrive, treat it as a deviation and
document how you handled it. Nobody is going to fault you for the weather. Gaps in the log are a
different conversation.

We are not going to give you food-safety rulings from a blog post. What we can do is put you in
the room with the material, and the *Workshop: Prairie Food Safety* session in Lancaster on 27
February is the closest opportunity — it covers exactly the deviation-and-documentation question
several of you have now asked us twice. Members further along that path should look at the Food
Safety & HACCP Certificate, which is the shortest-validity credential we run precisely because
this area keeps moving.

Also on the calendar: *Workshop: Cedar Packaging* in Tandil on 18 February, which is unrelated to
any of the above and a considerably more cheerful day out.

## What we're asking members to do

If the data gap cost you money, or the freeze cost you a make, tell us — specifically, with
numbers if you have them. Advocacy work that says "our members were harmed" gets a polite
hearing. Advocacy work that says "here are eleven creameries and what it cost each of them" gets
a meeting.

Tom Reyes at Speltmoor Farmstead, who has been carrying more than his share of the federation's
legislative load for the last couple of years, made the point at our last check-in that the
members with the strongest case are usually the ones with the least time to make it. That is what
we are here for. Send us the messy version; we will do the tidying.
