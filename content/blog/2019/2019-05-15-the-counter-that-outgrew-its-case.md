One of our retail members runs a specialty grocery counter in a mid-size Midwestern town, and last year she made a bet that felt small at the time: cut the number of eight-pound wholesale wheels in her case and replace them with smaller-format wedges from producers she could call by first name. She wasn't chasing a trend. She was trying to stop throwing away trim.

"We were losing money on shrink," she told us, "and I kept buying the same volume cheddar because it was the safe order. It wasn't actually the profitable order." She started with three small-batch cheeses on a trial basis, priced per wedge instead of per pound at the register, and watched what moved. Within a few months, the smaller formats had taken over roughly a third of her case.

## Why the format matters as much as the cheese

The shift she made lines up with something the trade press has been tracking this spring: retail cheese counters are leaning harder into smaller, more shopper-friendly formats — pre-cut wedges, snack-sized portions, anything that lowers the commitment for a customer who wants to try something new without buying a full wheel. For a producer, smaller formats mean more packaging and handling per pound sold. For a counter like hers, they mean less waste, faster turnover, and a lower price point that makes an unfamiliar cheese feel like a reasonable risk.

It also changed how she buys. Smaller, more frequent orders meant she needed a producer relationship that could tolerate a phone call instead of a standing purchase order. "I know when their goats are calving," she said. "I know when a cave is running warm. That's not information you get off an invoice." She's been an ICF member for a few years now, and says the membership directory has done more for her sourcing than any trade show booth — she's found two of her current producers through introductions made at committee events.

## What she'd tell another counter

Her advice to another retail member weighing the same shift is blunt: don't convert the whole case at once. She kept her volume cheddar and her reliable sellers in place and used the freed-up space for the experiment, so a slow month with a new producer wouldn't sink the department. She also warned that the labor side is real — more SKUs means more cutting, more labeling, and more decisions about how long a cut wedge can sit before it needs to move or get marked down.

"It's more work," she said, "but it's work that actually shows up in the case. Customers notice when you know something about what you're selling."

She's also candid about what didn't work on the first pass. Her first attempt at pricing the smaller wedges copied her wholesale per-pound math directly, and margins on the new format were thinner than she expected once she accounted for the extra cutting and wrapping labor. It took a full pricing pass — and a conversation with another member who'd already made a similar switch — before the smaller format actually paid for the additional handling time. Her advice on that point is equally blunt: price the format, not just the cheese, because a wedge that takes five minutes of counter labor per sale is a different product economically than one that takes thirty seconds.

There's a seasonal piece to this too. She stocks more of the smaller formats heading into the warmer months, when foot traffic through her counter rises and shoppers are more willing to try something unfamiliar for a picnic or a small gathering rather than commit to a full wheel that needs a plan. Come fall, when customers are buying for holiday boards and gifting, she shifts some of that case space back toward larger cuts and wheels that photograph well and split easily among a crowd. None of it was obvious to her a year ago; it came from watching what actually sold and adjusting the following month's order rather than trying to plan the whole year in advance.
