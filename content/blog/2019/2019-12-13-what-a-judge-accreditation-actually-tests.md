Every year around this time, we get a small wave of members asking how to become a competition judge. It's an odd month for the question — spring judging season is still four or five months out — but it's actually the right one. The Competition Judge Accreditation takes longer to earn than most members expect, and the members who start now are the ones ready in time.

The credential is built around two separate skills, and the federation tests them separately on purpose. The first is technical evaluation: identifying flaws and defects against a defined standard, scored down from a perfect baseline rather than up from zero. A crumbly paste in a cheese that should be supple, an off aroma that suggests a contamination issue rather than a style choice, a rind that's cracked from being dried too fast — a technical judge has to name the defect precisely, not just note that something is "off." The second skill is aesthetic evaluation: recognizing what a cheese is trying to be, on its own terms, and scoring how well it gets there. A washed-rind cheese and a fresh chèvre are not held to the same aesthetic bar, and confusing the two standards is one of the most common mistakes among first-time judges.

## Why the credential takes real hours

Candidates work through a calibration process using reference cheeses with known, documented flaws, because the whole point of a shared credential is that two accredited judges scoring the same wheel should land close to each other. That kind of consistency doesn't come from a single afternoon of training — most candidates need several practice rounds with feedback before their scores stop drifting. We'd rather a candidate take an extra month than pass through fast and score inconsistently at their first real competition.

This fall's international cheese awards, run alongside a major dairy festival overseas, drew one of the largest entry lists in the competition's history — enough that organizers spoke publicly about the logistics of scaling up judging teams without losing consistency across them. That's the same problem our own accreditation program is solving for at a smaller scale: more accredited judges only helps if they're all reading the same scoresheet the same way.

There's also a comportment component that surprises people: how a judge gives feedback to an entrant matters as much as how they score the cheese. A harsh note delivered without context can discourage a first-time competitor from ever entering again, and part of accreditation is learning to write a critique that's specific and useful without being a discouragement. Members who've been through it describe the written-feedback portion as the part that actually changed how they taste, not just how they judge.

## Starting now, for spring

If you're weighing whether to start, the practical argument for December is scheduling, not urgency. The calibration sessions run in small groups, they fill up as competition season approaches, and candidates who start in the new year are often judging their first practice rounds the same week they'd rather be finishing their own spring production. Getting the credential work done now, while your own cave is quieter, means you walk into spring with one less thing competing for your attention.

Reach out to the Awards & Competition Committee if you want to start the process, or to ask whether your event attendance record already covers part of the prerequisite. It's a shorter conversation than most members expect.
