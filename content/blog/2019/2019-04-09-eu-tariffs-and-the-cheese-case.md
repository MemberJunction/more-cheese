Members who carry both domestic and imported cheese have been asking us the same question this week: what happens to the imported side of the case if a new round of tariffs actually lands. It's a fair question. A proposed federal tariff list surfaced this month covering a wide slice of European goods, and it's tied to a dispute over aircraft subsidies that has nothing to do with dairy at all — but cheese, among other food categories, has landed on the target list before, and it may again.

We want to be careful here about what we do and don't know. The list at this stage is a proposal, not a finished tariff schedule, and the products and rates that end up on it can change substantially before anything takes effect. What's useful right now isn't the specific percentage — it's understanding how exposed your own case is, and starting to plan for a range of outcomes rather than waiting for a final number.

## Who actually feels this first

If your program leans on imported washed-rind, alpine, or blue styles from continental producers, you're the one with the most immediate planning to do. A tariff on an import line doesn't just raise your landed cost — it can also disrupt supply timing, since some distributors respond to a proposed tariff by front-loading inventory before any rate takes effect, which can mean a short-term glut followed by a gap. If you buy through a distributor, ask now, not later, whether they're already adjusting order volumes in anticipation.

For domestic producers, the picture is more mixed than "tariffs are good for us." A tariff on an import doesn't automatically become a sale for a comparable domestic wheel — buyers who wanted a specific French or Italian style for a reason (provenance, a specific culture, a story they tell customers) don't always substitute cleanly. But it can open real space in a case that's been full for a while, and if you make a style that competes on the same shelf, this is worth watching closely over the next several months.

## What we're doing, and what you can do

We don't have a position on the underlying aircraft dispute — that's well outside our lane — but we do track how food categories get swept into disputes that started somewhere else entirely, and we'll keep members posted as the list firms up. If your business would be meaningfully affected either way, the most useful thing you can do right now is model two or three tariff scenarios against your actual purchasing or production volumes rather than reacting to headlines as they come. Members who buy or sell across the affected categories are welcome to reach out through their regional contact if they want help thinking through the exposure.

Milk production in the broader domestic market has stayed close to flat through the first part of the year, and margins have been recovering slowly off a tight winter — a backdrop that makes any additional swing in the import side of the case land harder than it would in a looser market. We'll keep watching both threads and update members as the proposal moves.
