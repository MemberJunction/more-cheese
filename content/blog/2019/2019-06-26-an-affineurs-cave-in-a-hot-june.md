Not every member spotlight starts with a name. This one starts with a thermometer, because the member who agreed to talk with us this week asked us to keep her creamery out of it — she didn't want competitors reading about a problem she'd rather just solve quietly. We'll call her what she is: a farmstead affineur running an aging room built into an old dairy barn in the upper Midwest, aging cheddar and a handful of washed-rind wheels on wooden boards.

Her aging room has run warm this spring, and it turns out that's a conversation a lot of our members can join this month.

## A room doesn't care what you built it for

"People assume an aging room just holds whatever temperature you set it to," she told us. "Mine is supposed to sit at fifty-four. Some afternoons this month it's wanted to climb to fifty-eight, fifty-nine, and that's enough to change how a rind behaves." Regional reporting on this spring's milk supply has pointed to the same underlying pressure from a different angle — a smaller dairy herd and hotter stretches squeezing production upstream, before a single wheel ever reaches a cave.

The wheels she's most protective of are older cheddar blocks aging past a year, where a few extra degrees can speed up moisture loss at the surface faster than the paste beneath is ready for. Her fix was low-tech: a portable evaporative cooler running through the hottest afternoon hours, and moving her longest-aged wheels to the coolest corner of the room until the weather settled. "Nothing clever. Just paying closer attention than I usually have to."

It's a smaller-scale version of a pressure a lot of our members are watching this month as warmer-than-usual stretches push at herd comfort and, further upstream, at milk supply itself. Her problem is affinage; a dairy farmer's version of the same week is a herd that's off its feed and a milk check that shows it a month later. Different link in the same chain, same underlying weather.

## What travels, and what doesn't

She's direct about the limits of comparing notes with anyone whose setup differs from hers. Humidity control, board material, even the microbial population that's built up on her aging boards over years don't translate cleanly into advice for a newer creamery working with stainless steel and refrigeration. "I wouldn't tell someone with a brand-new plant to do what I do. This room has had fifteen years to become what it is."

What does travel, she says, is the habit of checking more often when conditions shift rather than trusting that a system dialed in for an average year will hold in an unusual one. She checks her room's humidity and temperature by hand, twice a day, through anything unusual — heat, a stretch of rain, a change in the outside air. "The room tells you. You only have to be listening."

If you keep an aging room or a cave and want to compare notes on managing a hot stretch without full climate control, the Standards Committee has been fielding exactly these questions this month and can point you to members who've dealt with it.
