Milk checks have had a good year, and the cheese case is closing it out with some noise. The federal outlook released this month put the full-year average milk price well ahead of last year's, on slower production growth and domestic demand that held up better than exporters did. That's welcome news for members who spent two of the last few years underwater on their milk price. But block cheddar spent most of the fall trading above two dollars a pound before easing back into December, and barrels held even longer at that level before softening too — which means a lot of the margin gain this year has been made and lost in narrower windows than usual. If your pricing sheet feels like it's chasing a moving target, it has been.

None of this is happening in isolation from trade. A round of tariffs on a range of European goods, cheese included, went into effect in October, and it's still working through import pricing three months later. Members carrying European cheeses or ingredients — cultures, molds, specialty rennets — have told us the line item shows up less as a sudden shock and more as a steady tax on every reorder.

## Who's absorbing the cost

Exporters on the other side of that tariff line have mostly been absorbing part of the added cost rather than passing all of it straight through, which has kept product on the shelf but has squeezed margins in a way that won't hold indefinitely. European cheese exports to the U.S. actually rose this fall in volume, on the strength of new capacity coming online overseas, even as the tariff made every case less profitable to ship — a sign that exporters are choosing to hold shelf space now and worry about margin later. A trade agreement finalized earlier this fall covering a separate set of dairy tariff lines is a reminder that these numbers can move again with little warning, in either direction.

We don't have a read on whether the tariff line gets adjusted, expanded, or left alone into next year. Actions like this one have moved on their own schedule before, and a parallel dispute is still working through the same trade channel, which could change the picture again with little warning. What we'd ask members to do now is straightforward: if a European product is part of your case, confirm your landed cost with your distributor before you lock in January pricing, not after.

## What this means at the counter

For domestic producers, a firmer milk price and steady cheese demand into the holidays is mostly good news, if margins stay tight enough to watch closely. Production is up modestly over last December in several major categories, which suggests supply isn't the constraint — pricing discipline is. For anyone stocking imported cheese, the tariff line is the number to track, not the headline about it.

Either way, close out December pricing with your actual landed cost in hand, not the number from your last order. If you want a second opinion before the year turns over, the Membership & Outreach Committee can point you to a member in a comparable channel who has already been through this math and is willing to compare notes.
