Monday's post covered what this year's flush looks like from the market side. This one is about the herd side, which is the part that actually shows up on your make sheet. Even a modest flush, like the one federal data points to this spring, still means milk composition moving under you week to week, and reading that movement correctly is the difference between a wheel that turns out consistent and one that leaves you guessing why this batch behaved differently than last month's.

## Why flush milk isn't just "more milk"

As animals move from stored winter feed onto fresh pasture, fat and protein percentages shift, somatic cell counts often move too, and the balance that determines how your milk sets and drains can change in the space of a couple of weeks. None of that is a problem if you're watching for it. It becomes a problem when a maker assumes April's milk behaves like March's and gets a soft curd or a slower drain with no obvious explanation.

That's the gap our Milk Quality & Herd Health course is built to close. It's not a herd-management course in the veterinary sense — we leave animal health decisions to your own vet and your own judgment. What it covers is the milk-quality side: how to read a bulk tank report for the signals that predict a make-sheet adjustment before you're standing over a vat wondering what went wrong, how somatic cell count trends interact with rennet set time, and how to build a rough seasonal calendar so you're adjusting culture and coagulant dosage on purpose rather than after the fact.

Members who've taken the course describe it less as new information and more as a framework for information they were already half-noticing. "I knew the milk changed every spring, I just didn't have a system for tracking it," one recent participant said. "Now I actually write down the tank numbers before I start a batch instead of trusting my memory from last year." That kind of small habit change is most of what the course is trying to build — not a dramatic new technique, just a more deliberate way of watching for the thing that's already happening.

If you buy milk on contract rather than milking your own herd, the course is still useful groundwork for the conversation with your supplier — knowing what a shifting composition report actually means makes it a lot easier to ask the right question when your make isn't behaving the way you expected. It also gives you language for that conversation that goes beyond "the milk seems different this week," which tends to get you a more useful answer from a supplier who's watching the same numbers you are.
