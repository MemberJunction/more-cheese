Registration is now open for the next Pasta Filata Techniques workshop, running as part of the Clover cohort starting July 1. If stretched-curd cheeses — fresh mozzarella, scamorza, provolone-style aged versions — have been on your "someday" list, this is the session to put on your calendar rather than your maybe pile.

We're timing this note to some numbers that came out this month, because they matter to anyone weighing whether a new product line is worth the equipment and the learning curve. A market report released earlier this month put the U.S. natural and specialty cheese market at more than sixteen billion dollars in retail sales last year, with analysts projecting it will keep growing past what looked, on paper, like a mature category. Separate coverage of new product launches earlier this year told a similar story from the retail side: fresh, whole-milk stretched-curd cheeses keep showing up in new formats — string cheese, snack-size chunks, shred blends — as larger producers chase the same convenience-snacking demand that's been driving growth across the category. That's exactly the demand a smaller creamery's hand-stretched product can compete against on flavor and story, if the technique is solid.

Pasta filata cheeses sit right in the middle of that growth — approachable to a lot of buyers, differentiated enough from commodity mozzarella to carry a specialty price, and forgiving enough as a technique that a creamery already comfortable with basic cheesemaking can add it without retooling the whole operation. It's also one of the more accessible ways to move a wholesale-only operation toward a retail case: a fresh, hand-stretched product photographs well, sells well at a farmers' market table, and doesn't require the multi-month aging investment that a bloomy-rind or washed-rind line would.

## What the workshop actually covers

This isn't a one-technique session. The Clover cohort curriculum walks through the stretch-and-knead process across several finished styles, troubleshooting the two failure modes almost everyone hits on their first few batches — curd that won't stretch cleanly, and curd that stretches but won't hold shape once it cools. Instructors spend real time on water temperature and pH windows specifically, since that's where most of the early frustration actually lives, not in the stretching motion itself. There's also a module on scaling a hand-stretched product for a retail case without losing the texture that makes it worth buying over the shrink-wrapped version.

If you make fresh cheeses already, this is a natural next step. If you don't, the instructors say it's still approachable — you'll want basic curd-handling experience going in, but nothing more specialized than that. Bring a thermometer you trust and, if you have one, whatever pH meter you use in your own make room; a fair amount of the workshop's value comes from comparing your instrument readings against the instructor's over a shared batch, and small calibration differences show up fast once you're stretching curd side by side.

Expect a full day on your feet. Mornings run through the chemistry — why the stretch works at all, and why it stops working if your curd is too acidic or not acidic enough — before moving into hands-on stretching in small groups. Afternoons are almost entirely bench time, working through the finished styles one at a time so you leave with muscle memory, not just notes.

## Getting registered

Seats for the Clover cohort are limited, as they are for every in-person workshop, and past sessions have filled before the registration window closes. If you've been waiting for the right moment to add a stretched-curd product to your case, a growing specialty market and an open registration window make this as good a moment as any. Sign up through the Events Committee page, and reach out if you have questions about whether the session is the right fit for where your operation is right now.
