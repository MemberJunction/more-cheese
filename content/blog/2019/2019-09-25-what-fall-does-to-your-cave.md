Every affineur we've talked to this month has a version of the same story: the cave ran itself all summer, and now it doesn't. Outdoor air in September and October swings harder, colder, and drier than at any other point in the year, and a room that was quietly self-correcting in July will start drifting the moment the intake air changes character. If your humidity log has more gaps than entries lately, you're not alone, but it's worth closing them before the swing gets worse in November.

The mistake we hear about most often isn't a broken humidifier. It's a room that's fine on paper — the readout says 88 percent — while the airflow pattern has shifted enough that one shelf is drying out and another is sweating. Fall is when uneven air distribution finally shows up on the rind, because summer's steadier outdoor conditions were masking it. A rind that looked fine in August can turn patchy in three weeks once the seasonal air starts moving through the room differently.

## What to check before you troubleshoot the equipment

Before assuming a humidifier or a compressor is failing, walk the room and check where the air actually moves, not just what the sensor near the door reads. Cave geometry, shelf spacing, and where your intake and return sit relative to each other all change how a seasonal shift lands on different wheels. A room with poor air distribution will show it fastest right at the seasonal turn, because that's when the incoming air is working hardest against whatever the room was already doing.

Our **Humidity & Airflow Control** course exists specifically for this stretch of the calendar — it walks through reading a room's actual airflow pattern rather than just its average numbers, and how to retune before a full seasonal swing turns a minor unevenness into lost wheels. "I used to check one sensor by the door and call it done," a small-creamery member who took the course last fall told us. "Now I walk the whole room with a handheld meter every time the weather turns, and I catch the dry corner before it costs me a shelf."

## A small, cheap habit that saves you money

If you do one thing this week: pull your last three months of humidity and temperature logs and look for the exact date the pattern started drifting, not just the current number. That date tells you when the outdoor air shifted enough to matter for your specific building, which is more useful than any generic "fall checklist," because your cave's seasonal turn point isn't the same as your neighbor's. Once you know your date, you can get ahead of it next year instead of chasing it.

If your room is already showing uneven rind development, the Education Committee can point you toward members nearby who've solved a similar layout problem — reach out through the member portal and we'll make the connection.

Members running smaller rooms have told us the seasonal turn hits harder every year their cave ages, not less — gaskets loosen, fans wear, and a room that compensated for a drafty door in its first year may not compensate as well in its fifth. That's part of why we built **Maturation Planning for Small Rooms** as a companion course rather than folding it into the airflow class: a five-year-old cave and a brand-new one need different diagnostic questions, even when the seasonal symptom looks identical from the outside.

None of this requires new equipment before you've even diagnosed the problem. Most of the fixes members report back to us are cheap — a repositioned fan, a relocated sensor, a gasket replaced a season earlier than planned — and the expensive fixes almost always turn out to be for rooms where nobody walked the space and mapped the airflow first. Start there, and let the diagnosis tell you whether you actually need to spend money at all.
