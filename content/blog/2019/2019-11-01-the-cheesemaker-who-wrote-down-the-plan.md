One of our members runs a small foothill creamery in California, the kind of operation where the
owner is also the cheesemaker, the affineur, and most weeks the one answering the phone. When
this past weekend's planned wildfire-prevention power shutoff hit her county, she didn't lose a
single wheel. That's not luck. It's the direct result of a one-page plan she wrote back in the
spring, taped inside the cave door, and hoped she'd never need to use.

## The plan that was already sitting there

The plan itself is unglamorous: a rented generator sized to the cave's compressor load, a
written order of what gets powered first if the generator can't run everything at once, and a
paper log sheet clipped next to the thermometer for exactly this situation. "I wrote it after a
regular equipment failure, not because I was thinking about wildfire shutoffs specifically," she
told us. "Turns out the plan doesn't care why the power's out. It just needed to exist."

When the shutoff notice came through with about a day's warning, she didn't spend that day
scrambling for a generator rental — she'd already identified a supplier and kept the number on
hand from the spring incident. The cave held its temperature range for the first several hours on
thermal mass alone, and the generator picked up the load well before humidity control became a
real concern. Two days later, when power came back, her log sheet showed a clean, documented
record of every reading in between.

## What almost went differently

She's quick to point out this wasn't a flawless run. The generator she'd lined up in the spring
had since been rented out to someone else for an earlier scare, and she spent an anxious
afternoon calling around before finding a backup source — a gap she's since closed by confirming
a standing rental agreement instead of a casual understanding. "The plan was right. My assumption
that the same generator would always be sitting there waiting for me was the part that was
wrong," she said. It's a small correction, but it's the kind of detail that only shows up once
you've actually run the plan under real conditions instead of just writing it down.

Her creamery isn't unusual in the risk it faces — plenty of our California members sit in the
same utility footprint, and plenty more deal with their own version of the same problem when a
storm or an equipment failure does the same thing a shutoff did to her. What's unusual is that
she'd already done the work before she needed it. If you've been meaning to write your own
cold-chain contingency plan and haven't gotten past a mental checklist, her advice is the
simplest kind: write it down, confirm your backup power source actually exists rather than
assuming it, and keep a paper log within reach of the thermometer. The Food Safety Committee is
glad to look over a draft if you want a second set of eyes before you need it for real.
