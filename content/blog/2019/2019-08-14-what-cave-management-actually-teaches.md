If your aging room has felt like it's working against you the last few weeks, you're not imagining it. Record heat moved across parts of Europe late last month, and while our members' caves here weren't anywhere near those extremes, the same underlying pattern has been showing up in temperate-climate creameries all summer: buildings that coast through most of the year on passive cooling suddenly can't keep up. Most cheeses hold best somewhere around 50 to 55 degrees and 80 to 95 percent relative humidity, and a lot of small caves get that balance from the building around them as much as from any equipment. In August, the building isn't helping. Outside heat pushes into basements and cellars, compressors run longer, and the air inside a small room dries out faster than the cheese wants it to.

That's the exact problem our Cave Management course was built to walk through, and it's a good one to flag now, mid-summer, while the problem is live rather than theoretical.

## Why humidity, not temperature, usually breaks first

Most new affineurs watch the thermometer and miss the hygrometer. Temperature swings in a small cave tend to be slow and forgiving; humidity swings are fast and much less forgiving. A rind that dries out too quickly cracks and lets in air it shouldn't; one that stays too wet turns slick and invites the wrong mold. The course spends real time on reading a hygrometer correctly, where to place it in a room that isn't uniform, and how to tell the difference between a reading problem and an actual humidity problem — those are not the same failure, and they don't have the same fix. A hygrometer mounted near the door will read differently than the air three shelves back, and members are often surprised how much that gap widens once the compressor is working overtime.

The second half is about intervention that doesn't require new equipment: damp towels and open water trays as a cheap humidity buffer, shelf spacing that lets air move without drying a whole rack at once, and a rotation schedule that compensates for the wheels closest to your door, which always dry fastest. None of it is expensive, and most of it can be set up in an afternoon once you know what you're compensating for.

A cave that holds steady humidity most of the year usually isn't managing humidity directly at all — it's benefiting from a stable building envelope and a compressor that only kicks on occasionally. Push that same compressor into near-continuous operation, which is exactly what happens during a stretch of unusually hot outside air, and the air inside starts behaving differently. Refrigeration pulls moisture out of the air as a side effect of pulling out heat, so a room that felt comfortably humid in June can turn noticeably drier in August without anyone changing a setting. The course walks through why this happens and, more usefully, how to recognize it early — usually from rind texture changes before the hygrometer confirms it.

## Who this is for

This course reads as ambitious for the size of some members' operations, and it isn't. It's aimed squarely at the smallest rooms — the converted closet, the root cellar, the single reach-in — because those are the setups with no buffer when the outside temperature spikes for a week. A larger commercial cave has redundancy built in; a farmstead cave usually has one compressor and one door.

Members who've taken it tell us the return isn't dramatic technique, it's confidence — knowing which reading to trust when the room feels wrong and you're deciding whether to act tonight or wait until morning.
