A slow-moving hurricane took its time up the East Coast this year, and by the time it finally moved off the Carolina coast last week it had left ports closed, parcel carriers rerouting, and more than a few refrigerated trucks parked in places their drivers hadn't planned on. For a lot of industries that's an inconvenience. For anyone shipping cheese, it's a temperature problem with a clock attached.

We heard from several members in the storm's path this week, and the story was consistent: it wasn't the wind that hurt, it was the wait. A truck sitting outside a closed port for two extra days is a truck whose cargo has aged in ways nobody planned for and nobody can sell as intended. One distributor member put it simply: "We don't worry about losing a shipment to a storm. We worry about a shipment arriving fine and having no idea what happened to it in between."

## The cold chain has more joints than people think

A cheese's trip from cave to case usually looks like one line on a spreadsheet — ship date, arrival date — but it's actually four or five handoffs, each with its own truck, its own dock, its own thermometer. Under normal conditions those handoffs are invisible. Under storm conditions, they're where things go wrong: a reefer unit idling too long waiting for a closed facility to reopen, a rerouted load crossing a state line it wasn't documented for, a pallet redirected to a warehouse that wasn't expecting soft-ripened wheels at all.

None of that shows up as a dramatic loss. It shows up two weeks later as a retailer complaint about a wheel that "wasn't right," with nobody able to say exactly where the chain broke. Members who ship wholesale, especially anyone working with distributors along the Southeast coast, told us this is the part of storm season that doesn't make the evening news but costs the most: the shipments that technically arrived, just not in the shape they left in.

## What held up better than expected

A few members with pre-built storm contingency routes — a second distributor on standby, alternate delivery windows built into their contracts — came through with less spoilage than they braced for. That's not a coincidence. A distributor relationship that only gets tested once a year, during hurricane season, is a relationship worth stress-testing before the season, not during it. If your current agreements say nothing about what happens when a route closes for 72 hours, this is a reasonable week to ask.

We don't have aggregate loss figures for the region yet — those tend to surface in trade reporting a few weeks after a storm clears, once claims are filed — and we won't guess at a number. But the qualitative read from members is clear enough: cold-chain resilience is a wholesale-account question as much as a cave-humidity question, and it's worth putting on the agenda before next year's season, not during it.

If your business ships wholesale and you don't have a written contingency plan with your primary distributor, the Membership & Outreach Committee can point you toward members who've built one and are willing to talk through what worked.
