Most quality-management conversations we run on this blog start with a lab result. One of our
members' doesn't. She's the quality manager at a mid-sized aged-cheddar creamery in the upper
Midwest, and the tool she trusts most heading into fall isn't a test kit — it's a clipboard hanging
by the cave door, where she logs temperature and relative humidity by hand, twice a day, on a
schedule she's kept for six years.

"The sensors tell me the same number the clipboard does, most days," she told us. "The clipboard is
there for the days they don't agree. That's usually the day something's actually wrong."

It's a habit that predates the digital monitoring system her creamery installed three years ago,
and she never gave it up once the sensors arrived. Plenty of other quality managers we talk to have
gone the other direction — trusting the dashboard and letting the paper log lapse once the numbers
started streaming automatically. She thinks that trade was a mistake for anyone running a
lower-volume, higher-touch aging program, and she's not shy about saying so on committee calls.

## Why a hand log still earns its place

Her creamery runs digital sensors throughout the cave, the same as most facilities her size. What
the automated system can't do is flag a reading that's technically within range but wrong for the
season — a humidity number that would be fine in July and is a warning sign in October, because the
outside air has shifted and the cave hasn't caught up yet. She built her own set of seasonal
benchmarks over years of comparing the hand log against finished wheels, and she checks new
sensor data against that memory before deciding whether a reading needs action.

That habit matters most right now. Fall is when a lot of caves see their hardest humidity and
airflow swings, as outside temperatures drop faster than the cave itself cools, and a routine
calibrated for a dry August can quietly fall out of step with reality before anyone notices in a
finished batch. "I don't rebuild my whole program every October," she said. "I just tighten how
often I'm checking the rooms that have drifted on me before. You only know which ones those are if
you've kept the log long enough to see the pattern."

She's also quick to point out that the trade press's steady drumbeat of stronger milk-production
and pricing news this fall hasn't changed anything about her day-to-day routine, and she thinks
that's the point. A firmer market doesn't make a cave forgiving; it just means more people are
paying attention to whether your wheels turn out the way they're supposed to.

## What she gets out of committee calls

She isn't chasing a title on the Food Safety Committee's mailing list — she's there to compare
notes with other quality managers running similar programs at a different scale, and she's quick to
say most of what shaped her current routine came from hearing another member describe a problem
she hadn't hit yet. "I've adjusted my own checks because of somebody else's bad October," she said.
"That's what the calls are actually for."

If your cave program still runs on sensors alone, her advice is plain: the technology is worth
having, but it's not a substitute for someone who knows what a normal reading looks like in your
own building in your own season. The Food Safety Committee keeps a basic seasonal-benchmark
worksheet on file for members who want a starting point for building that kind of judgment rather
than working it out the hard way.
