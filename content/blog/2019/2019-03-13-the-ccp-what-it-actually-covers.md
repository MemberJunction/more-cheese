We get some version of this question every month: "Am I ready for the CCP, or should I wait?"
It's worth a straight answer, because the Certified Cheese Professional credential is the one
members ask about most and understand least before they start it.

The CCP is our flagship credential, and it's built to be comprehensive rather than quick.
Unlike the Cheese Foundations Certificate — the entry-level credential most members earn
first, covering milk handling, basic make procedures, hygiene and cheese styles — the CCP
goes end to end: make procedure, affinage, food safety, and business practice. It assumes you
already have the Foundations Certificate in hand, and it asks for documented working hours
on top of that. This isn't a credential you cram for over a weekend. Most members who've gone
through it describe it as roughly a year of steady effort layered on top of their actual job,
not a course you finish and forget.

## Who it's actually for

If you're running make on a regular schedule, doing your own affinage decisions, or making
calls about lot releases and recalls, the CCP maps closely to what you're already doing —
it mostly formalizes and stress-tests knowledge you're applying daily. If you're newer to the
trade, or your role is narrower — you're focused entirely on the cave, say, or entirely on the
retail counter — the Foundations Certificate or a narrower credential like the Sensory
Evaluation Certificate might fit better first. There's no penalty for taking credentials in
order. The ladder is there because it works better than skipping steps.

One thing members ask about specifically: the CCP is valid for three years before it needs
renewal, shorter than the five-year window on Foundations. That's intentional — the business
and safety-practice content in the CCP tends to move faster than basic make technique, and we
want the credential to mean something current, not something earned once and coasted on.

A cheesemaker at Highland Meadow Curds who went through the CCP last year told us the part
that surprised her wasn't the exam itself — it was realizing how much of her day-to-day
judgment she'd never had to explain out loud before. "I knew why I pulled a wheel off the
shelf," she said, "I just hadn't ever had to write down the reasoning." That's a fair summary
of what the credential is actually testing: not whether you can make good cheese, but whether
you can articulate the decision-making behind it clearly enough that someone else could
follow your reasoning and your paper trail.

## What it doesn't cover

To be direct about the limits: the CCP is a professional credential, not a legal or medical
one. It teaches you what the food-safety rules require and how to build a compliant process
around them — it does not make you a food-safety inspector, and it isn't a substitute for
your state's actual licensing requirements, which vary and which you should confirm directly
with your regulator. If a make decision has real safety stakes, the CCP gives you the
framework to think it through carefully; it doesn't give you a shortcut around getting expert
guidance when something's genuinely uncertain.

If you're weighing whether this is your year for the CCP, start by pulling your logged hours
and checking them against the documentation requirement — that's usually the piece that
takes people by surprise, not the material itself. Members who've been through it before
tend to underestimate how long it takes to reconstruct hours after the fact, so the earlier
you start tracking, the less painful the application gets. If your creamery keeps informal
time records, or none at all, this is the week to start a simple log rather than trying to
reconstruct a year of work from memory next winter.

The Education Committee keeps office hours most weeks if you want to talk through timing
before you commit, and they can also tell you honestly whether your current mix of make,
affinage, and business responsibilities lines up with what the CCP is actually testing. It's
a better conversation to have before you register than after you've paid the fee and started
studying material that doesn't match your day-to-day work.
