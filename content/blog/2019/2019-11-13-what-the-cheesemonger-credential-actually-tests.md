Every fall we hear from a batch of retail staff asking the same question in different words: is the Certified Cheesemonger credential worth the time it takes to prepare for it. The honest answer is that it depends on what you're hoping it does for you, so this week we're laying out what the credential actually covers, because the assumption we hear most often — that it's mostly a tasting exam — isn't quite right.

The exam does include a substantial tasting and identification component, and you should expect to be asked to place unfamiliar wheels into a style family and describe what you're tasting in terms a customer would understand, not just technical jargon. But roughly half of the credential is retail operations: proper cutting and wrapping technique to minimize waste and preserve rind integrity, case rotation and temperature management, safe handling around allergens and cross-contact at a shared cutting board, and basic label literacy so you can explain to a customer what a standardized cheese name does and doesn't guarantee about how it was made.

## Who tends to take it, and why

Most members who pursue this credential aren't brand-new to the counter — they're a year or two in, comfortable with the daily rhythm of the job, and looking for something that formalizes what they already know while filling in the gaps they've picked up unevenly on the job. A few shop owners have told us they use it as a structured onboarding tool for staff who are already competent but haven't had a reason to learn the handling and food-safety half of the job in a deliberate way. It's less common as a first credential and more common as the one that turns "I work the cheese counter" into something you can put on a resume.

The other pattern we see is retail staff who are eyeing a move toward buying or category management down the road. The credential doesn't certify you as a buyer, but the retail-operations material overlaps meaningfully with what a buyer needs to understand about how a case actually behaves day to day, and several members have told us it was a useful stepping stone before they took on purchasing responsibility.

## Preparing without overdoing it

You don't need a formal course to prepare, though our Sensory Foundations coursework covers the identification and pairing material in more structured depth if that's the part you're least confident in. What trips people up more often is the retail-operations half, simply because it's less glamorous to study — nobody sits down at home and quizzes themselves on cross-contact procedures the way they'll happily blind-taste six wheels of tomme. If you're prepping on your own time, budget real attention for that half rather than assuming it'll take care of itself because you already do it at work.

If you're not sure whether this is the right credential for where you are versus something like the Cheese Foundations Certificate, the Education Committee can walk you through the difference in about ten minutes on the phone, and it's worth that call before you commit study time to the wrong exam.
