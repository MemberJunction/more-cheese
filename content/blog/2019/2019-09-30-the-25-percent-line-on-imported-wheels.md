A long-running dispute over aircraft-industry subsidies is about to land on cheese counters
that have nothing to do with aircraft. Trade officials have spent this year building a list of
European goods eligible for retaliatory duties, and cheese has been on every draft of it — not
as a side note, but by name, wheel by wheel. Gouda, edam, emmentaler, gruyère,
parmigiano-reggiano, gorgonzola, Stilton, and a run of British cheddars all appear on the
published categories, alongside wine and a handful of other specialty foods. A final ruling on
the size of the award is expected within days, and members who track this closely are hearing
mid-October floated as the likely date the duty — as high as 25 percent on the affected lines —
actually takes effect.

If your case doesn't carry imported cheese, this is a one-paragraph story for you: read it,
note the date, move on. If it does — and a meaningful share of our retail and specialty-shop
members carry at least a few European lines as anchor products — it's worth understanding the
shape of the change before your next order lands.

## Why this one is different from the last one

We wrote in August about a new round of duties on cheese exported *from* the US into China.
This is the mirror image: a tariff on cheese imported *into* the US, and it hits a different
part of our membership. Exporters and importers are not always the same members, and the
advice is not the same either. An exporter can sometimes shift a shipment toward a domestic
account. A retailer who has built a cheese program around a specific Alpine or English cheese
doesn't have that option — the wheel is the wheel, and there's no substitute SKU that tastes
the same to a customer who's been buying it for years.

The other difference is timing pressure. Larger importers and distributors have had weeks to
stockpile ahead of the effective date, and some of the volume you're seeing move through
regional warehouses right now is exactly that — buyers building inventory before the new rate
applies. Smaller shops don't have the freezer or cave space, or the working capital, to do the
same thing at scale. If you run a small cheese counter that leans on two or three import lines,
you are, structurally, at a disadvantage against a competitor five times your size doing the
same math with more room to store product.

## What to actually check before the date

Ask your importer or distributor two plain questions: which of your specific SKUs fall under
one of the affected tariff lines, and whether the new rate is being absorbed anywhere in the
chain or passed straight through to your invoice. Industry estimates we've seen put a
meaningful number of import-dependent jobs at risk if margins compress the way some analysts
expect, which tells you this isn't a rounding error some distributors plan to eat quietly.

If a good share of your case relies on cheeses named on the list, this is a reasonable week to
sketch out a menu or case-card change before the invoice forces one on you. That might mean
introducing a domestic or non-EU alternative alongside the import rather than instead of it, so
a regular customer has a bridge rather than a gap. It might also mean simply raising the price
on the affected wheel and being straightforward with customers about why — most people
understand "tariff" better than they understand a quiet quality substitution they didn't ask
for.

We don't have a crystal ball on how long this particular duty stays in place; these things get
negotiated, appealed, and sometimes rolled back faster than anyone expects. What we can tell
you is that members who ran this same exercise in past rounds — checking their SKU list against
the published schedule before the date, not after — came out of it with fewer surprises on
their October invoices. The Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded a few of these
questions already this month and is glad to compare notes if you're trying to figure out your
own exposure.
