Every affineur we've talked to this month has said some version of the same thing: the cave that
practically ran itself in September just stopped cooperating. That's not a coincidence, and it's
not a sign anything went wrong in your make room. It's the seasonal shift, and it happens to
almost everyone whose aging space takes any cues at all from the outside air.

## Why the room changes even when you haven't touched it

Most aging rooms hold cheese somewhere in the 48–57°F range, with humidity set by style — drier,
around 80–85 percent, for a hard natural-rind wheel; wetter, up near 90–95 percent, for a
bloomy or washed-rind cheese that needs to stay slick. A brine room or wash-rind space held at
close to full saturation and a steady 50°F is where cultures do their best work, but that kind of
steadiness usually takes deliberate control, not luck. Through the fall, a lot of aging spaces
coast close to target without much intervention, because outside air and the thermal mass of the
room are doing a lot of the work for you. Once outside temperatures drop hard, that cooperation
ends. Cold outside air is drier air, and if your cave pulls any ventilation from outside — even
passively, through a door seal or a vent that's been fine all season — you'll start seeing
humidity fall faster than your climate control was built to correct for on its own.

The result shows up on the rind before it shows up on a hygrometer if you're not checking daily.
A washed-rind wheel that's been developing evenly for weeks can start drying and cracking at the
edges within days once the room dips below its target humidity, and by the time you notice the
visual change, you're already behind on correcting it. This is the point in the year when "check
the cave" needs to move from a weekly habit back to a daily one — the wash-flip-wash rhythm that
carries a rind through its early months only works if the room underneath it is holding steady.

## What the course covers, and what to check this week

Affinage Fundamentals walks through exactly this seasonal transition as one of its core modules:
how to read a rind for early signs of humidity drift, how much lag to expect between a
hygrometer reading and a visible change on the cheese, and how to adjust misting or ventilation
schedules incrementally rather than overcorrecting and swinging the room the other direction. It's
aimed at members managing their first cave, or their first cave in a climate they haven't aged
through before, but longtime affineurs tell us they still pull the seasonal-transition section back
out every November as a refresher, because it's easy to assume a room that behaved all fall will
keep behaving into winter without a check.

In the meantime, a few things worth doing this week regardless of where you are in the course:
walk your cave daily rather than every few days until you've confirmed your winter humidity
holds; check door seals and any passive venting for drafts that weren't a problem when outside
air was warmer and wetter; and if you're running a wheel that's especially sensitive to rind
drying — a young washed-rind, in particular — consider moving it to the most stable shelf in the
room until your winter routine is dialed in. None of this requires new equipment for most home and
small-commercial setups; it mostly requires attention, at a time of year when attention is easy to
let slide.

If you want the full module rather than the seasonal-transition summary, Affinage Fundamentals is
open for enrollment now through the Education Committee, and members who've taken it say the
rind-reading section alone is worth the time before your first hard freeze catches your cave
off guard.
