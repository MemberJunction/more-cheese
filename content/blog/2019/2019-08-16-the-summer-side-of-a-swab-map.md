A quality manager at a small farmstead creamery in the upper Midwest called our Food Safety Committee last week with a problem that doesn't show up in most winter training material: her environmental monitoring log, built around the condensation and drainage risks of a cold-weather make room, wasn't catching anything useful in August. "The zones I mapped in January are still the right zones," she told us. "But the thing I'm actually worried about right now isn't in any of them."

Her aging room, like a lot of small-creamery caves, borrows its stability from an old building rather than a dedicated refrigeration system. That works fine most of the year. In the hottest stretch of summer, she says, the compressor runs nearly continuously just to hold temperature, and running equipment that hard pulls moisture out of the air faster than the wheels can tolerate. The monitoring program that used to flag condensation risk at drains and gasket seals now spends August flagging something almost opposite: rinds drying and cracking near the vents where dry, cold air blows hardest.

## The fix wasn't new equipment

Her adjustment was to move her swab and moisture-check points to follow the airflow rather than the fixed zones she'd mapped for winter — checking the shelves nearest the compressor twice as often as the ones farthest from it, and logging humidity readings at cheese height rather than at the thermostat on the wall, which she found reads several points drier than the air actually touching the rind. She also started keeping a second, independent hygrometer in the room after her first one drifted without her noticing for most of July.

None of this required a capital expense. It required treating the monitoring program as a living document that tracks the season, not a form filled out the same way every month regardless of what's actually stressing the cave.

## Why she's telling other members now, not in the fall

She's sharing this mid-heat-wave, rather than waiting for a tidy year-end writeup, because the decisions are happening now for anyone whose cave is struggling. "If you wait until October to fix your summer setup, you've already lost a whole season of wheels," she said. Her practical advice for other members running small aging rooms this month: check your hygrometer against a second, independent one before you trust it, watch the shelves closest to your cooling equipment more than the ones farthest away, and don't assume a room that held steady in June will still hold in August.

She's also candid that this took her a full season to notice the first time. Her first summer running the program, she says, she kept troubleshooting the wrong shelves for weeks because she was still thinking in the winter zones. It wasn't until she started walking the room with a handheld meter, rather than trusting the wall-mounted reading, that the pattern became obvious.

Members wrestling with their own August caves can find this kind of seasonal thinking built into our Cave Management coursework, or can reach out directly through the Food Safety Committee for a second opinion on their monitoring setup before the next heat spell arrives.
