Now that the results from Madison are official, we've had a steady trickle of members asking some version of the same question: what does it actually take to be one of the people at the judging table, and is it something they could work toward. The short answer is the Competition Judge Accreditation, and it's worth walking through what it actually requires, because it's more layered than a lot of members assume.

## Sensory fundamentals first, then supervised reps, then a slow clock

The accreditation isn't a standalone exam. It sits on top of the Sensory Evaluation Certificate, which is where the actual vocabulary and method get built — distinguishing a style-appropriate character trait from a genuine defect, scoring body and texture consistently across a flight, and doing it without your own make preferences leaking into the scoresheet. That certificate is the prerequisite, not a nice-to-have; the committee doesn't accept judge applications without it on file.

From there, the path runs through supervised panel experience rather than a single test day. Candidates sit alongside accredited judges on real competition flights, scoring in parallel without their sheets counting toward the result, until the panel lead is confident the candidate's scores track closely enough with the room's. That's a deliberately slow on-ramp, and it's the part that surprises people who assumed a strong palate and a few years of cheesemaking would be enough on their own. Knowing what a good washed rind tastes like and being able to defend a score against three other judges' notes are related skills, but they aren't the same one. It's the same logic behind sensory certification generally, which is built around standardizing how professionals evaluate and describe cheese rather than testing personal taste: the credential tests whether your palate agrees with a room full of trained ones, not just whether it's a good palate.

The accreditation is also only good for two years, which is short compared to some of our other credentials, and that's intentional rather than an oversight. Judging is a calibration skill more than a knowledge one — it degrades if you're not actively doing it, in a way that, say, a HACCP plan's underlying principles don't. A judge who sat out two full competition cycles would be scoring against a memory of the last flight they judged rather than the current field, and the field moves. This year's soft-ripened category alone was deep enough that judges asked for extra time to get through it properly; a judge whose calibration had gone stale wouldn't have been equipped to sort that field fairly.

Renewal is built around the same logic as the original path: active judging counts, sitting out doesn't. Judges who work at least one sanctioned competition within the two-year window keep their accreditation current without a re-test. Judges who don't have to go back through supervised panel sessions before they're eligible again, the same as a first-time candidate. We know that feels strict to members who judged for years and then had a season or two get away from them, but the alternative — grandfathering people back in without a recalibration check — is exactly the failure mode the two-year window exists to prevent.

If Madison left you wanting to be closer to the scoresheet next year, the realistic timeline starts with the Sensory Evaluation Certificate if you don't already hold it, then a conversation with the Awards & Competition Committee about supervised panel openings at an upcoming regional event — the annual conference floor isn't where new candidates start. It's a multi-year path for most people, not a single application, and the committee would rather walk you through it honestly now than have you find out the hard way that a strong palate alone doesn't get you a seat at the table.
