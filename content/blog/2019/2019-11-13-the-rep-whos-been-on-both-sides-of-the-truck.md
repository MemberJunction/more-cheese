Most of the member profiles we run here start on the make-room side of the business: a
cheesemaker, an affineur, someone with their hands in curd. This one starts on a loading dock. Our
member has spent twenty-two years in the cheese trade, the last thirteen of them as an ICF member,
working as a regional sales director for a specialty dairy distributor that moves traditional
cheddar and a handful of other domestic styles between producers and the retailers who stock them.
He's been a member long enough to have watched three or four full cheddar pricing cycles come and
go, and he says that's exactly why he stays.

## Why a distributor bothers with a cheesemakers' federation

It's a fair question, and he's been asked it by colleagues more than once. His answer is that a
distributor sits in a strange spot: close enough to production to feel a bad milk month before it
shows up on a retail shelf, but far enough from the make room that nobody's inviting him to
committee meetings about affinage technique. What the federation gives him instead is the market
read — the margin picture members are reporting from their own operations, the advocacy notes on
tariff lines and labeling rules that affect what he can put on a truck, and a rough sense, week to
week, of whether the producers he works with are feeling stretched or steady. "I read the
federation's notes before I read my own company's," he told us, "because ours are written for
sales targets and theirs are written for what's actually happening in a vat."

That market literacy runs in both directions. When cheddar prices firmed up earlier this year
after a soft stretch, he says he heard it from ICF members before he heard it from his own supply
contacts, and it changed how he priced a run of contracts that quarter. Firmer international dairy
prices through 2019, driven in part by tight European supply working against strong import demand,
showed up first as chatter among members before it showed up in a formal price sheet — which is
about as good a case as we've heard for why a distributor should be paying dues to a producers'
association in the first place.

## Thirteen years, no committee seat, no complaints

He's never chaired a committee and doesn't especially want to. What he does, consistently, is show
up: renewing every year without being chased, reading the market notes, and occasionally forwarding
one to a producer he works with when it's directly relevant to their account. That's a quieter kind
of membership than the ones that get profiled most often — no advocacy tally, no competition medal,
no credential in progress — but it's a genuinely useful one, and it's the kind that keeps a
federation's market read grounded in more than just the production side of the business.

If you're a distributor, broker, or buyer reading this and wondering whether ICF membership is
worth it from where you sit, his answer is straightforward: it depends on whether you want the
unfiltered version of what producers are dealing with, three or four weeks before it turns into a
line in a trade publication. For him, thirteen years running, that's been worth the dues on its
own. If that sounds useful and you're not yet a member, or you know someone on the distribution
side who should be, the Membership & Outreach Committee can walk through what a distributor-tier
membership looks like.
