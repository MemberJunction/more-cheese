Cheesemakers spend a lot of time refining recipes and not nearly enough time asking what's actually in the milk they're starting with. Fat and protein ratios move with the season, with what the herd is eating, and with basic animal health — and every one of those shifts changes how your curd sets before you've done anything to it. That's the premise behind Milk Quality & Herd Health, one of our more consistently enrolled courses this cycle, and it's worth a plain explanation of why it matters even if you buy your milk rather than milk your own cows.

Late winter is a good time to notice this. Feed costs have been running high enough relative to milk prices that federal figures released this quarter put the income-over-feed-cost margin at under nine dollars per hundredweight for three months running — tight enough that some producers are trimming ration quality to manage the bill. A cheaper ration doesn't always show up as a visible problem. It shows up three weeks later as a slower set or a softer curd, and by then it's easy to blame the recipe instead of the input. The course spends real time on somatic cell counts and butterfat trends for exactly this reason: they're an early warning system, not paperwork.

Most of our members aren't the ones filing for dairy safety-net payments, but the margin figure still tells you something useful: when it's this tight, feed substitutions become more common upstream, and that's precisely when milk composition gets less predictable. A supplier under margin pressure isn't doing anything wrong by adjusting a ration — they're managing their own business the way you'd manage yours. But it means the assumptions your recipe was built on six months ago may not hold today, and the only way to catch that early is to actually look at the numbers instead of waiting for a bad batch to tell you.

## The make-sheet connection

If you buy milk from a co-op or a specific supplier, you're not exempt from this — you're just one step removed. Ask your supplier for a monthly composition report if you don't already get one, and log your set times against it. Two months of that comparison will tell you more about your process than a dozen recipe tweaks. Members who've done this consistently report catching seasonal dips before they show up as a batch of cheese they're not proud of.

The course also covers what to actually do when you spot a problem: how to talk to a supplier about a feed change, what questions to ask if somatic cell counts creep up, and where the line is between a milk issue and a make issue. That last distinction saves a lot of wasted troubleshooting — no amount of culture adjustment fixes milk that came in different than what your recipe assumes. One exercise has students walk through a real set of monthly composition numbers and mark, in advance, which months would have required a recipe adjustment. Most students underestimate how often the answer is "more than they'd assumed."

## Where to go from here

The Lark cohort of Milk Quality & Herd Health just wrapped, and we expect another section later this year — watch the course catalog for the date. In the meantime, if you're not already tracking composition data against your make results, this month is as good a time as any to start the habit. It costs you nothing but a spreadsheet, and it's the fastest way to tell whether next month's slow set is your process or your milk.
