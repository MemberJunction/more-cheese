We get a version of the same question most weeks: "isn't sensory evaluation just tasting
cheese?" We understand why it looks that way from the outside. From the inside, it's closer to
learning a second, more disciplined vocabulary for something you've been doing by instinct for
years — and for a lot of members, that's the harder skill to build.

The Sensory Evaluation Certificate asks candidates to assess appearance, texture, and
aroma-and-flavor against a shared standard, then describe what they found in language another
evaluator would agree with. That last part is the whole exercise. Anyone can tell you a wheel is
"off." The certificate tests whether you can identify why — a fermentation fault versus an
affinage problem versus a packaging issue — and put a name to it that a judge, a buyer, or a
co-worker three states away would recognize the same way.

## Who it's actually for

This credential draws two different kinds of candidates, and both are a good fit. The first is
cheesemakers who want a formal check on their own palate before they start training junior staff
to taste alongside them — you can't teach consistent evaluation if your own baseline hasn't been
tested. The second is buyers and cheesemongers who need a defensible way to explain a rejection or
a reorder to a supplier. "This doesn't taste right to me" doesn't hold up in a vendor conversation
nearly as well as "this shows a bitter finish consistent with over-ripening at this affinage
stage."

It is not, on its own, a competition judging credential — that's a separate accreditation with its
own track, and we get the two confused often enough that we want to say so directly here. Sensory
evaluation is about description and consistency. Judge accreditation adds scoring, category
standards, and the specific mechanics of a competition table. Plenty of members hold both, in
either order, and neither one substitutes for the other on an application.

Application windows for this cycle close at the end of March, which is sooner than most members
expect once they start assembling references and work history. If you've been thinking about it
"sometime this year," this is the reminder that sometime is now, not June.

## Getting ready

The exam rewards practice under real conditions more than it rewards reading. If you're
considering it this year, start a habit now: taste the same style of cheese from two different
makers side by side, write down what you notice before you say it out loud to anyone, and compare
notes with a colleague afterward. Do that for a few months and the exam stops feeling like a test
of talent and starts feeling like a test of a habit you already have.

If you want a structured version of that practice before you sit the exam, our Sensory
Foundations course runs candidates through exactly this kind of paired tasting with feedback from
an evaluator who's already certified. It won't replace months of your own practice, but it will
show you what a passing description actually sounds like before you're the one writing it under
time pressure.

Reach out to the Education Committee for the next cohort date, and bring questions about how the
certificate fits with anything else on your credential path — that conversation is worth having
before you register, not after. Recertification runs on its own clock too, so if you're not sure
whether yours is current, that's a five-minute question worth asking this week rather than a
surprise you find in your inbox next spring.
