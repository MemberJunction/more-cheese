Six weeks ago, a new import duty took effect on a long list of European cheeses, added on top of
whatever tariff line a given wheel was already carrying. It grew out of a long-running dispute
that had nothing to do with cheese at its center, but the retaliation list that came out of it
reached deep into the dairy case: soft-ripened, blue-veined, hard grating cheeses, and a good
share of the aged wheels our members either import or compete against on the retail floor. We
said at the time it was worth watching past the initial headlines. Six weeks in, it's still worth
watching, because the effects are showing up in slower, less dramatic ways than a one-time price
jump.

## What members are actually seeing

For the importers and distributors in our membership, the additional duty has meant a choice
between passing the full cost through, absorbing part of it, or renegotiating terms with a
supplier who is facing the same math from the other direction. Several members have told us
they're seeing all three responses happening at once, sometimes on the same order sheet — a
supplier holding price on a flagship product while quietly trimming volume on a smaller-margin
line. That's a rational response to an additional cost that landed with six weeks' notice and no
sunset date attached, but it also means the shelf isn't adjusting in one clean motion. Expect the
case to keep shifting into early next year as contracts come up for renewal.

For domestic producers, the picture is less direct but not neutral. A handful of members who
make styles that compete head-to-head with the affected imports — a washed-rind or a bloomy-rind
made in a similar tradition — have said retailers are asking harder questions about their pricing
now that the imported comparison point costs more. That can read as an opportunity, and for some
members it has been. It can also mean a retailer expects the domestic wheel to hold the line on
price even as the domestic producer's own input costs move, which is a less comfortable
conversation to have across a loading dock.

## What we're watching next

The duty applies to a defined list of tariff lines, and lists like this get revisited, sometimes
on short notice, as the underlying dispute moves. We don't have a prediction for when or whether
that happens, and we're wary of members making purchasing or pricing commitments based on an
assumption that the current rate is either permanent or temporary. What we can say is that this
is exactly the kind of shift the Membership & Outreach Committee wants to hear about directly
from members who are living with it — a specific tariff line, a specific supplier relationship, a
specific number on an invoice. General industry commentary is useful for context, but a comment
letter or a testimony request lands harder when it's grounded in an actual order that got more
expensive on a specific date.

If your business touches imported cheese on either side of the transaction — buying it, selling
against it, or supplying into a market where it competes — and you've seen a concrete effect this
fall, we'd like to hear the specifics. It helps us represent the membership accurately if this
comes up again in a hearing or a comment period, and it helps other members get a clearer read on
a market that right now is moving more by rumor than by data. Reach the Membership & Outreach
Committee through the usual channels, and if you're new to advocacy work, this is a low-effort way
to start: describe what changed on your invoice, not what you think should happen in Geneva.
