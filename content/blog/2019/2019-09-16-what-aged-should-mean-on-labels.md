If you make cheddar, the federal government has an opinion about what cheddar is. The standards of identity that cover roughly seventy cheese and cheese-product names spell out moisture content, milkfat, and manufacturing steps in enough detail that a "cheddar" sold in one state has to resemble a "cheddar" sold in another. That part of the label is locked down. What isn't locked down is everything printed around it, and this month the Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding more questions about that gap than about almost anything else.

"Aged." "Farmstead." "Traditional." "Raw milk." None of these are standards-of-identity terms. They're descriptive, and descriptive language is largely left to the producer's judgment and whatever the buyer down the supply chain is willing to take on faith. A twelve-month cheddar and a sixty-day cheddar can both say "aged" on the wrapper, legally, because nothing sets a floor. Members who invest in long-cave affinage have told us this flattens the market for everyone: the shopper can't tell from the label alone which wheel earned its price, and the producer who cut corners pays no penalty for it.

A regional creamery cooperative spent part of this summer defending exactly this kind of claim in court, after customers argued that its packaging and advertising implied the milk behind it came from small family farms when much of it, in fact, came from a large industrial-scale operation. We aren't taking a side on that case — it isn't ours to take, and it's still working through the courts — but members keep bringing it up anyway, because it's the clearest recent example of how expensive an undefined origin claim can get once someone decides to test it.

## Where the state lines complicate things further

Layer state grading marks on top of the federal standards and the picture gets messier. Some states apply their own grade stamps to cheese made and packed within their borders, and expressly prohibit using that stamp on anything shipped in from elsewhere. That's a reasonable rule inside one state's dairy program. It becomes a headache for a member whose product crosses three or four state lines between the vat and the retail case, because the label that was accurate in the plant may not be the label that's accurate — or even legal — by the time the wheel reaches a shelf two states over.

We hear from small creameries on both sides of this. Some want a federal floor under "aged" and "farmstead" so the terms mean something specific, the way "cheddar" does. Others worry that any new definition, once written, will be calibrated around large-scale production timelines and will quietly disqualify smaller, slower operations from using language they've used honestly for years. Both concerns are legitimate, and neither has an obvious fix. That's exactly the kind of question we'd rather members answer collectively, through committee, than leave to whichever trade group gets to a public docket first.

## What we're doing with it

The Standards Committee has opened an internal comment file for members who want their specific labeling situation — a term you use, a claim you make, a conflict you've hit crossing a state line — logged before we take a position publicly. This isn't a petition yet. It's the fact-gathering stage, and it only works if the facts come from working creameries and not from us guessing on their behalf.

If you've had a label rejected, questioned, or flagged by a retail buyer over a descriptive term, tell us. If you've built your own internal standard for what "aged" means on your own wheels and you'd be willing to share it as a model, we want that too. Reach the Standards Committee through the member portal, or bring it to the next Membership & Outreach Committee call — details on the events calendar. The stronger the file, the more precisely we can ask for the fix members actually need, rather than the one that sounds cleanest from the outside.
