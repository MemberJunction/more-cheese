Our Judging Standards webinar runs this week, and the question we get most from members thinking about signing up isn't about the webinar at all — it's about what comes after it, if they decide to go further and pursue Competition Judge Accreditation. So this week we're pulling that credential apart a little, since the two scoring roles it trains you for aren't the same skill wearing two hats. They're closer to opposites.

Judging panels typically work in pairs, and the two seats score from opposite ends. One judge starts at a perfect score and takes points away for flaws — an off aroma, a cracked rind, a paste that's grainy where it should be smooth. The other starts at zero and builds the score up, awarding points for what the cheese does well: a clean finish, a rind that matured the way the style intends, balance between salt and fat. Both scores land on the same fifty-point scale. They rarely land on the same number, and that's expected, not a sign something went wrong.

It's been a big autumn for competition judging generally — entry counts at the major fall shows are up again this year, and members who've judged more than one season tell us the volume alone is changing how panels manage their time. More entries per category means less time per plug, which raises the stakes on a judge's ability to name a characteristic quickly and precisely instead of circling around it.

## Why the accreditation trains both seats

Most candidates walk in assuming the deduction side is the real skill — you catalog defects, you know your off-flavors, you're done. It's the harder-earned half of the credential, certainly, and it leans hard on what the Sensory Evaluation Certificate already taught: naming a flaw precisely enough that another judge would name it the same way. But the accreditation won't sign off on a candidate who can only subtract. The addition side asks you to defend why a cheese is good, in language specific enough to be useful to the maker who submitted it — not "nice" or "solid," but which particular characteristic the style calls for and how well this wheel delivered it.

Anonymity is the other piece that surprises people who haven't judged before. Entries reach the panel without a maker's name or creamery attached, scored on the cheese in front of them and nothing else. It's a small structural choice with an outsized effect: it's the reason a first-year hobbyist entry and a twenty-year professional's entry get read the same way, and it's part of why members tell us the written feedback that comes back with a competition score is worth more to them than the ribbon.

## Getting there from where you are

The path runs through the Sensory Evaluation Certificate first — you can't sit for judge accreditation without it, since the deduction-and-addition scoring above assumes you can already isolate and name what you're tasting. From there, accreditation adds supervised panel experience alongside a credentialed judge before you score independently. It's also the shortest-lived credential we offer at two years, because judging is a skill that erodes fast without regular practice; members who go a season or two without sitting on a panel usually tell us they feel rusty picking it back up.
