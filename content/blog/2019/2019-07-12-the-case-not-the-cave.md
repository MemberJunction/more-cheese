Most of our member spotlights land on someone standing over a vat. This one is about someone standing over a case.

We caught up recently with a program director at one of our retail member shops, who has spent the last several years proving that a cheese program lives or dies at the counter, not the make room. Her job has almost nothing to do with making cheese and almost everything to do with what happens to it after it arrives: building the case layout, training staff to cut and wrap without wasting a good wedge, managing relationships with a dozen producers at once, and fielding the question every retail cheese buyer dreads in July — what happens when the delivery truck runs two hours behind and the wheel that's supposed to hold at 50 degrees has been sitting on a loading dock in the heat.

"People think the hard part of this job is knowing which cheese pairs with which wine," she told us at a recent chapter meetup. "The hard part is knowing which of your producers ships in a truck with a working reefer unit and which one you need to call the second the forecast says ninety degrees."

## Summer is a supply-chain problem before it's a flavor problem

That's the piece of retail cheese work that doesn't show up in most cheesemaking curricula but shows up constantly in a shop's summer. A wheel that travels well in April can arrive weeping and out of temper in July if the cold chain has any gap in it — a late truck, a warehouse without enough dock space, a case that isn't pulling enough refrigeration on the hottest afternoon of the month. Her approach has been to push that conversation upstream, to the producers themselves, well before peak heat arrives: confirm transit windows, confirm who's responsible if a shipment shows up compromised, and settle the return-and-credit conversation before there's a wheel on the counter that nobody wants to sell.

There's also a merchandising side to the season that's easy to miss. Trade coverage of summer cheese retail has pointed to lighter, fresher styles — chèvres, young alpine-style wheels, anything that doesn't need six weeks in a warm case to show well — as the smarter bet for a July display, precisely because they turn over fast and don't sit exposed to heat and light for long stretches. Our spotlight member has leaned into that rotation deliberately: fewer big cut wheels sitting under the lights in July, more frequent small deliveries of things that move.

It's not glamorous work, and it rarely gets the credit that a well-timed rind wash or a clever affinage program gets. But a cheese program that loses product to bad handling in July is a program that's quietly eating its own margin, and retail members like her have made the case — literally and otherwise — that they deserve as much craft attention as anyone standing over a vat.

It doesn't help that cheese plants have been running at or near full capacity heading into summer, with national inventories finally coming back into regional balance after a stretch of being long nationwide. More product moving means more volume flowing through distributors and more trucks on the road at exactly the time heat is hardest on a cold chain — good for sales, harder on logistics. Members running a retail case should expect a busier delivery schedule for the rest of the summer, not a quieter one, and that's exactly when a gap in the chain is most likely to show up.

## If this sounds like your July

If you run a counter and you're staring down the same seasonal cold-chain headache, reach out through the Membership & Outreach Committee and we can connect you with members who've been through it.
