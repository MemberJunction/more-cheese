"Environmental monitoring" is one of those phrases that sounds like it belongs to a plant with a sanitation department and a line of stainless steel, not a three-person cave with a dozen wheels on the shelf. It applies anyway, and this is the month we hear from the most members about it — cave humidity is settling into its winter rhythm, and Listeria concerns tend to surface in the same conversations as "why does this rind keep going soft in the back corner."

Our Food Safety Committee runs a quarterly webinar on exactly this, and the current run of our Listeria Control in Small Creameries course walks through what a monitoring program looks like at a scale that doesn't require a dedicated QA hire.

## What the course actually covers

The short version: swab your drains, your floor mats, and anything with a squeegee or a hose attached to it, on a schedule, and write down what you find even when the answer is nothing. Listeria monocytogenes lives in wet environments and moves on equipment and boots, not through the milk itself, which is why the highest-value swab sites in a small cave are almost never the cheese — they're the floor drain, the condensation line, and the mat under the wash sink.

The course also spends real time on what to do with a positive result before it happens to you, which is the part most first-time cave operators skip until they're standing in front of one. A documented response plan — hold the affected lot, deep-clean and re-swab the site, and know who you're calling — turns a bad Tuesday into a manageable one instead of a guessing game.

None of this is new regulatory ground. The preventive-controls compliance dates that brought environmental monitoring into small and very small facilities' routines finished phasing in a little over a year ago, and most of our members have already built some version of a swab schedule to satisfy an inspector. What the course adds isn't the requirement — it's a template for doing the recordkeeping in a way that actually holds up, rather than a folder of loose swab results nobody can find in February.

## Where this connects to interstate raw-milk aging

If you're aging a raw-milk cheese toward the 60-day mark for interstate sale, environmental monitoring and your aging documentation are really the same file. A clean swab log alongside your aging records is the strongest thing you can hand an inspector, and it's also just good practice for a cave you're trusting your business to. Members who've been through both a state inspection and one of our environmental monitoring reviews tell us the paperwork overlap is almost total — you're mostly building one system, not two.

If your cave doesn't have a swab schedule yet, this is the month to start one — not because anything's gone wrong, but because January is when most of you have the slower stretch to actually build the habit.
