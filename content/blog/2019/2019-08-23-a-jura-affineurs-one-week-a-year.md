An affineur who joined us this year from a small aging house in the French Jura was back in the country this week for his annual visit — a single trip that has to cover a year's worth of cave tours, supplier meetings, and catching up on what's changed since he was last here. He runs the affinage program at a family fromagerie that's been aging comté-style wheels for longer than the federation has existed, and membership, for him, mostly means one dense week of in-person time rather than the steady weekly rhythm of a domestic member.

That compresses everything. In four days he toured two members' aging rooms, sat in on a cave-humidity discussion he called "the same argument we have at home, just with different weather," and asked more questions about export paperwork than he has in any previous visit. That last part isn't a coincidence. A federal comment period on tariffs touching a wide swath of imported cheese closed earlier this month, and while the outcome is still unsettled, the uncertainty alone has already made European producers and their American partners more careful about the deals they're willing to sign this fall.

## What travels and what doesn't

His visits are useful precisely because so little of what he does translates directly. Humidity, culture strains, even the shape of a typical wheel are different enough between a Jura cave and an upper-Midwest one that most of his advice needs translating rather than copying. What does travel, he says, is the discipline of treating aging as an active process rather than a waiting period — checking, turning, and adjusting on a schedule rather than leaving wheels alone until they're "done." Members who've hosted him for a cave walkthrough over the years say that mindset shift sticks longer than any specific technique he demonstrates.

The trade uncertainty complicates the other half of his week: the business conversations. A domestic distributor he's worked with for years described this month's tariff talk as making every new purchase order feel provisional, since a price that pencils out today might not by the time a shipment actually crosses. Our member's read on it, delivered with the shrug of someone who has watched trade policy swing before, is that the wheels themselves don't change — only what they cost to bring here, and how much nerve a distributor has to commit six months out.

## Why one week is enough, for now

He's candid that a single week a year is a real constraint on how connected he can be to the federation day to day — he misses most webinars because of the time difference, and he's never been able to attend the Annual Conference in person. But he says the trip earns its keep every year specifically because it's concentrated: a week of full attention beats a year of half-attention squeezed around a workday on the wrong continent. If your own cave could use a set of eyes that's seen a different aging tradition, the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a list of members open to a walkthrough during his next visit — worth flagging interest early, since his week fills up fast.
