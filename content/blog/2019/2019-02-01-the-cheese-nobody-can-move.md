A number has been circulating in member calls this month that is worth sitting with for a second: roughly 1.4 billion pounds of American, cheddar, and Swiss cheese are currently sitting in cold storage across the country, the largest stockpile on record. That is not a rounding error in a spreadsheet somewhere. It is warehouse after warehouse of finished product that made it all the way through a make room and a cave and still has nowhere to go, and it is the reason several of you have told us your wholesale buyers are suddenly a lot less eager to talk price.

The surplus did not appear overnight. Export volumes to two of the country's largest overseas cheese markets have fallen sharply over the past year as tariff lines have shifted, and domestic demand, while steady, was never going to absorb the difference on its own. Consumers are also buying differently than they used to — more natural and imported styles, less of the processed, shelf-stable cheese that drove a lot of the production growth over the past decade. Put those two things together and you get a warehouse problem that eventually becomes a milk-check problem, because the price a processor is willing to pay for raw milk moves in the same direction as the size of the pile they cannot sell.

## Why this is squarely an advocacy issue, not just a market one

Our production-volume members — mostly the wholesale-scale cheesemakers among you, though the effect reaches smaller operations through the co-ops and processors you sell through — have been asking us what the federation is doing about it. The honest answer is that a stockpile this size is not something any single trade association fixes; it took years of price-support policy nudging production ahead of demand, and it will take more than one good quarter to work off. But there are two things worth pushing on now, and our Membership & Outreach and Standards Committees are doing exactly that in coordination.

The first is distribution. At least one industry group has already floated using surplus stock to support food-insecure households rather than letting it sit in a warehouse depreciating against nothing. We think that idea deserves a serious hearing, and we have said so in the comments we have filed. It does not fix the underlying supply-and-demand mismatch, but it moves product, and moving product is not nothing when the alternative is more of it stacking up next month.

The second is export access. A domestic surplus problem gets worse every time a foreign market that used to take American cheese closes or shrinks over a tariff dispute that has nothing to do with cheese quality or price. We do not have a lobbying budget that rivals the bigger commodity groups, but we do have members in nearly every state, and a federation letter signed by four hundred small and mid-sized producers reads differently to a trade office than one signed by a handful of processors. If you want your operation counted in that letter, write to the Membership & Outreach Committee this month — the next round goes out before the quarter ends.

## What to actually do with your own numbers this week

If you sell wholesale, this is a good week to look hard at your contracts rather than wait for the market to sort itself out. Buyers with leverage right now know they have it. If you sell direct — farm store, farmers' market, wholesale to independent shops — you are somewhat insulated from the cold-storage number specifically, but not from the general softness in what your neighbors' milk is fetching, which shapes your input costs even if you are not selling into the commodity pool yourself.

None of this is a reason to panic, and it is certainly not a reason to change what is in your cave. It is a reason to know the number, know that it is a policy problem as much as a market one, and know that the federation would rather have four hundred names on a letter than forty. If you have not weighed in yet, the Membership & Outreach Committee wants to hear from you before the next filing window closes.
