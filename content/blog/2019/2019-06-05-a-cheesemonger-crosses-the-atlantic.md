Most of our member spotlights feature a producer or an affineur. This one is about the person on the other end of that relationship: a cheesemonger who runs a small counter at an independent specialty grocer and has spent the last two years building a supply chain that skips the usual domestic distributor step for a handful of her import lines. She buys several wheels directly from a small producer overseas, works with a customs broker to get them landed, and ages a portion of them herself in a converted walk-in before they ever hit the case.

"People assume importing is either impossible for a shop our size or something you just hand off entirely to a distributor," she told us. "There's a middle path. It's more paperwork than I expected when I started, and it's absolutely not for every SKU, but for the two or three wheels our regulars actually ask about by name, it's worth it."

## What the paperwork actually involves

She walked us through what a member considering the same thing should expect: an import broker to handle customs clearance and duty classification, a cold-chain plan that survives the actual transit time rather than the optimistic one, and a clear-eyed conversation with the producer about what happens if a shipment gets held at the border for inspection. None of this is federation-specific guidance — it's closer to what any small importer learns the hard way — but she said the biggest gap for her early on was simply not knowing which questions to ask a broker before signing anything.

Trade conditions haven't made this easier this year. Dairy import licensing and the quota categories that come with it are a fixed cost of doing this at all — anyone bringing in more than a handful of shipments a year needs to work through the federal licensing structure that governs cheese, butter, and other dairy imports, and the published quota circulars change enough from month to month that she checks them before every order rather than assuming last quarter's numbers still hold. For a shop working direct-import lines at her scale, a small shift in a quota or duty line can matter more than it would for a larger buyer with more product lines to absorb the difference across.

## Why she stays a federation member

She joined a little over a year ago mainly for the Certified Cheesemonger and Certified Cheese Professional pathway, but said the committee guidance on labeling and cross-market standards has turned out to be just as useful for the import side of her business as for the counter itself. Her advice to anyone else weighing a similar move: start with one line, not five, and expect the first shipment to teach you more than any amount of reading beforehand.

If you're a retail member thinking about a direct-import relationship of your own, the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a running list of members willing to talk through what worked and what didn't — ask us and we'll make the introduction.

One more thing she wished someone had told her at the start: a broker relationship is worth interviewing for, the same way you'd interview a supplier. She spoke with three before choosing one, asked each of them to walk through how they'd have handled a specific held-shipment scenario, and picked the one whose answer was the most specific rather than the most reassuring. "The reassuring answer is usually the vaguer one," she said. "You want the person who tells you exactly which form gets filed and how long that actually takes, not the person who tells you not to worry."

That same instinct — ask for the specific answer, not the comfortable one — is what she says she now applies to reading a quota circular herself instead of taking a broker's summary at face value. She still relies on her broker for the filing itself, but she reads the underlying numbers each cycle so she knows what a delay or a quota shift will cost her before it shows up as a surprise on an invoice.
