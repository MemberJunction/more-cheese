If you run a small or very small cheese facility, you likely already know the preventive-controls rules under the federal food-safety modernization framework apply to you eventually — the compliance dates were staggered by business size specifically so smaller plants would have more runway. What's easy to lose track of is exactly how much runway is left. For many small facilities, the deadline for the allergen-control provisions falls this September. That sounds distant in March. It is not, once you account for the time it takes to actually build and test a program rather than just write one.

## What "allergen controls" means in practice

The rule isn't really about whether you handle the nine major recognized allergens — as a dairy operation, you handle milk, full stop, and probably tree nuts or wheat if you make flavored wheels or use nut-lined molds. It's about whether you can demonstrate, on paper and on the floor, that you're controlling cross-contact between allergen-containing and non-allergen product, and that your labels are accurate for what's actually in the wheel.

For a lot of our smaller members, that means formalizing things that already happen informally. If your team already runs the walnut-cheddar batches last and cleans before switching back to plain, that's the right instinct — but "the right instinct" and "a documented, verifiable allergen control plan" are two different things to an inspector, and only one of them satisfies the rule. The gap between them is usually smaller than people fear, but it does take deliberate work: a written plan, assigned monitoring, and records that show the plan is actually being followed, not just filed.

## The course, and who it's for

Our **Allergen Management** course starts March 29 and runs six weeks, aimed squarely at small-plant owners and quality staff who need to go from "we do this informally" to "we can document this" before fall. It walks through building a facility-specific allergen control plan, setting up practical cross-contact monitoring that doesn't require a full-time hire, and getting your labeling review process into a repeatable shape. It's built around real timelines — you'll leave with a draft plan, not a stack of theory to adapt later when you have more time.

We'd rather members build this program in April and have it tested by August than start it in August under deadline pressure. If you make anything with nuts, or if your facility handles both an allergen-containing line and a plain line on shared equipment, this is the course to prioritize this spring.

Separately from the control-plan work, this is also a good spring to double-check your finished labels against what's actually in the batch, particularly if a recipe has changed even slightly since the label was designed. A label that was accurate two years ago isn't automatically accurate today. It's a quick check, and it's one inspectors look at closely, so it's worth doing before you're asked to.

If you're on the fence about whether your facility needs this level of formality, reach out to the Food Safety Committee — they can help you figure out where your plant sits relative to the deadline, and whether the March 29 cohort or a later one makes more sense for your timeline.
