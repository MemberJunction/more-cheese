One of our longtime members runs a small food-safety consulting practice that works almost exclusively with creameries under twenty employees. She's been a member since 2014, and this is her busiest stretch of the year — not because holidays create new hazards, she told us, but because they compress a normal week's worth of production into three days, and compression is where habits get skipped.

"Nothing about December is inherently more dangerous than March," she said. "What's dangerous is a crew running a gift-box line, a wholesale order, and a retail counter rush at the same time, with the same three people who normally handle one of those things at a time. That's when a hand-off gets missed, not because anyone got careless, but because there wasn't a clean moment to do it in."

## The habit she checks first

Asked what she looks at first when she walks into a client's cave or cutting room in December, she didn't say temperature logs or swab schedules, though she checks both. She said labels. "A gift assortment is often the first time all year a creamery packages three or four of its own cheeses next to somebody else's crackers, honey, or nuts in one box. That's a new allergen picture every time, even if none of the individual cheeses changed."

She's seen small operations get this wrong in good faith — not a mislabeled cheese, but a bundled gift box where the outer packaging didn't carry through the full ingredient list from every component. "The cheese was always safe. The box wasn't giving the customer what they needed to know before they opened it," she said. "That's a paperwork failure, and paperwork failures are the ones you can actually prevent with a checklist."

## What she tells newer members

For members putting together holiday assortments for the first time, her advice is blunt: build the label before you build the box. "Decide what's going in the assortment, pull every ingredient list from every component including the ones you didn't make, and write the combined label before a single box gets packed. If you do it the other way around, you're always reconstructing something after the fact, under a deadline, which is exactly when mistakes happen."

She also pushed back gently on treating this as a once-a-year problem. "Every time your case changes, your allergen picture changes with it. December is just the month it happens fastest."

Trade coverage of this year's holiday gift-basket market noted just how far upscale the category has climbed, with luxury retailers pairing artisan cheeses alongside truffles, charcuterie, and specialty condiments in single boxes aimed at a gifting customer, not a grocery one. That's good news for members with a strong assortment story to tell, and it's exactly the trend she says raises her concern. "The more components you add to make a box feel luxurious, the more allergen combinations you're responsible for tracking. A basket with five things in it isn't five times the labeling work of a basket with one thing in it — it's closer to twenty-five, because you have to think about how they touch each other, not just what each one is."

She's also blunt about the federal baseline every member should know cold before building a combined label: major allergens have to be declared in plain language on the finished package, regardless of how they got there, whether they were baked in, dusted on, or packed next to something else that used them upstream. The federal food-labeling regulator publishes guidance spelling out exactly what counts and how it has to be worded, and she keeps a copy on her desk year-round. "I've never met a cheesemaker who wanted to hide an allergen. I've met plenty who didn't realize their supplier's cracker changed a recipe six months ago and it never made it back to the box copy."
