We heard this story secondhand at a regional chapter meeting last week and asked the member
involved if we could share it, without naming her creamery, because it's a situation more of
you are probably in than you'd think. She took over quality management this year at a small
operation that had changed hands twice in the last decade. The food safety plan on file was
technically current — signed, dated, filed where it should be — and completely useless.

The pasteurizer it described had been replaced three years earlier. The cooling room it
referenced had been reconfigured after a leak. Two of the named critical control points no
longer existed in the layout, and one genuinely hazardous step that had been added when the
creamery started a new bloomy-rind line wasn't mentioned anywhere. Nobody had done anything
wrong, exactly. Ownership changed, staff changed, and each new person assumed the plan they
inherited had been kept current by whoever came before them. Nobody had.

## Why this happens more than you'd guess

A food safety plan is only as good as the last time someone walked the floor with it in hand.
That's easy to do when a creamery is new and small enough that one person holds the whole
picture in their head. It gets much harder after an ownership change, an equipment upgrade, or
simply enough years passing that the person who wrote the original plan has moved on and
nobody left remembers exactly why a given control point was set where it was.

The member's fix was blunt: she started over, not by editing the old document but by walking
the entire production floor with a blank page, mapping every hazard from the raw milk intake
forward, and only afterward comparing it to what the old plan said. She told us the gap
between the two documents was bigger than she expected — not because anyone had been careless,
but because a food safety plan doesn't decay all at once. It decays one small, reasonable
change at a time, until the document and the building have quietly stopped describing the same
place.

## What to check if you haven't lately

If your plan predates your current equipment layout, your current staff, or your current
product line, it's worth the afternoon to walk it against reality rather than assume it's
still accurate. Regional initiatives supporting artisan-scale producers with exactly this kind
of hazard-mapping work exist specifically because this gap is common, not rare — you are not
behind if you find one in your own plan. Our Food Safety Committee can put you in touch with a
member who's done this rebuild recently if you want to talk through the process before you
start your own.

The Listeria Control in Small Creameries course spends its first session almost entirely on
this kind of floor-level hazard mapping, precisely because so many members arrive already
holding a plan that looks complete on paper and doesn't match their building. If a walkthrough
turns up gaps in your own plan, that's the place to go next — not to panic, but to rebuild the
document so it says what your creamery actually does now.
