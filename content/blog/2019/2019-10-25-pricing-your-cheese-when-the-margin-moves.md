We've heard from a lot of members this month working through their own margin decisions —
whether to change a coverage election, whether to hold a wholesale price, whether a firmer cheese
market is a reason to finally move on a price increase they've been putting off. It's the right
week, then, to talk about a course a fair number of members ask about once they're already
mid-decision, when it would have helped more three months earlier: Pricing & Margin for Specialty
Cheese.

The course exists because most makers learn pricing the hard way, backing into a number that
covers the vat and hoping it also covers everything else. It walks through building a price from
the actual cost stack — milk, cultures, labor, aging loss, packaging, the portion of overhead a
wholesale account should really be carrying — rather than starting from what a competitor charges
and working backward. For a small creamery, that distinction matters more than it sounds like it
should, because "what the market will bear" and "what your cost stack requires" are two different
numbers, and only one of them tells you whether you're actually making money on a given wheel.

## Why this fall specifically

The timing lines up with two things going on right now. Cheese prices have moved up since
midsummer, which tempts some makers to hold their existing prices rather than push for an
increase — understandable, but the course spends real time on why a firmer market is actually a
better moment to raise a wholesale price, not a reason to skip it, since buyers are less likely to
push back when the whole category is moving. At the same time, members working through this
year's margin-coverage election are already doing cost-side math for their milk supply; the
course extends that same discipline to the cheese-room side of the business, where a lot of
producers have never built the equivalent worksheet.

Across its sessions, the course covers landed cost per wheel by format and age target, the
difference between wholesale and direct-to-consumer margin structures, how to model a price
increase's effect on volume before you make it, and — a section members mention often afterward —
how to talk to a longtime distributor or retail buyer about a price change without it reading as
a shakedown. It doesn't tell you what to charge. It gives you the arithmetic to defend whatever
number you land on, to a buyer, to a lender, or to yourself at year-end.

## A note on the numbers you'll bring

Members sometimes show up to the first session without any real cost data, expecting the course
to hand them a number instead of a method. It won't, and that's deliberate. Two members who took
an earlier cohort told us the most useful exercise wasn't the pricing formula itself but a simple
worksheet forcing them to write down what they were actually spending per batch, several of them
for the first time since they started the business. One said she'd been pricing a washed-rind
wheel below her own cost stack for over a year without realizing it, because she'd copied a
neighboring creamery's shelf price rather than building her own.

That kind of gap is more common than members like to admit, and it's a big part of why the course
keeps a waitlist even in a season with plenty of other demands on people's calendars. Aging loss
alone — the weight a wheel sheds over months in the cave — is a line item a lot of new makers
forget to cost in at all, and it can be the difference between a wheel that's profitable on paper
and one that isn't once it actually leaves the cave.

If your own pricing hasn't been rebuilt since you started the business, or since your cost stack
last changed meaningfully, this is a reasonable place to start. Check the course calendar on the
member portal for the next cohort, and bring last year's cost numbers with you — the exercises go
faster with real data instead of guesses.
