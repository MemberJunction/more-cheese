Federal milk price reports this month put Class III well above where it sat in January, and members who buy their milk on a formula tied to that number have felt it in their cost sheet all year, especially in the last few months. Trade coverage this week described farm margins improving on the back of those higher prices — good news if you're the one milking, but it also means the cheesemakers buying that milk are paying more for it than they were in the spring. That's not news to anyone doing the buying. What's easy to miss, in the scramble of a holiday production run, is whether your finished-cheese pricing actually caught up to it, or whether you're still selling this year's higher-cost wheel at a price you set when the input cost was lower.

This is the week to check, while the number is still fresh and before you're deep into planning next year's price list from memory instead of from your actual numbers.

## The one number worth pulling before December 31

Pull your cost per pound for your highest-volume product, using this fall's milk price rather than an average across the year, and compare it to what you actually charged for wheels shipped in the last quarter. If the gap between cost and price is smaller than it was in the spring, your pricing moved with the market. If it's the same or wider, you priced this year's cheese at last year's economics, and the difference came straight out of your margin without anyone deciding it should.

This isn't a call to raise prices reflexively — some members have room to hold steady because they built in cushion earlier, and some categories are more price-sensitive than others at retail. It's a call to know the number before you set next year's list, rather than carrying an assumption about your margin that hasn't been true since sometime in the spring.

A quality manager at a small Midwestern creamery told us this fall that she'd priced her flagship wheel the same way for three years running, on the theory that customers had come to expect the number and would balk at a change. When she finally ran the comparison, her cost per pound had climbed enough that she was effectively subsidizing every wheel she sold. She adjusted before the holiday run, not after, and said the harder part wasn't the math — it was admitting she'd been avoiding it.

## Where to take it from here

Packaging and labor have moved too, and both belong in the same check — a pricing model that only tracks milk will still drift if your box cost or your hourly rate went up without a matching adjustment on the shelf. If you want a structured way to work through all three at once, our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks a working cost sheet start to finish, cohort by cohort, using real product categories rather than a generic template.

You don't need the course to do the December check, though. You need one afternoon, this fall's milk number, and your own invoices from the last quarter. Do that much before the year turns, and next year's price list starts from what actually happened instead of what you assumed.
