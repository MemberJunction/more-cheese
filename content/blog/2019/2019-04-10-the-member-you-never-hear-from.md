Most of our member spotlights feature someone who shows up: a regular at conference sessions, a familiar face on a committee, someone with a story about a make that went sideways. This one is a little different, because Victor Sandoval, VP of Procurement at Reedmere Dairy Systems, has been a federation member since 2017 and has never attended a single event we've held.

That's not a complaint. It's a genuinely useful case study in what a membership is for, because Victor's is paid by his employer, renewed automatically every year, and used for exactly one thing: keeping Reedmere's procurement team current on the standards their supplier network is expected to meet.

## What the membership is actually buying

Reedmere moves dairy processing equipment and supply into small and mid-sized creameries across a wide territory, and Victor's job is to make sure the vendors on that list meet expectations that keep his own customers out of trouble — sanitation standards, labelling requirements, the kind of allergen-control practice that shows up on an inspection report. He doesn't need a conference badge to do that. He needs the standards themselves, kept current, and a way to check when something in the underlying rules has shifted.

"I read the bulletin when it lands, and I forward the parts that matter to three people on my team," Sandoval told us, when we finally tracked him down for this piece. "That's most of what the membership does for us. I don't need to be in the room for it to work." It isn't glamorous, and it isn't meant to be. It's closer to how a lot of larger dairy-adjacent businesses actually use trade-association membership: less community, more infrastructure.

## Why this matters for how we think about engagement

It would be easy to read low event attendance as low value, and for an individual member that's sometimes a fair signal — someone drifting toward a lapse often looks exactly like this on paper. But a corporate account renewing without fail for years, attached to a real procurement function, is a different pattern entirely. The membership isn't idle. It's just working quietly, in the background, the way a lot of infrastructure does.

There's a reason a distribution business like Reedmere treats standards access as worth paying for even when nobody on staff shows up to a session. Global milk supply growth largely stalled this year, and reporting out this month has pointed to soft retail demand for some dairy categories alongside continued trade friction limiting how much of that tighter global supply US producers and distributors can actually capture. In a market like that, a distributor's margin increasingly depends on things that aren't visible on a price sheet: whether their supplier network can pass an inspection cleanly, whether their labelling is defensible, whether a single bad actor upstream creates a recall that costs far more than a membership ever would. That's exactly the kind of risk Victor's job exists to manage, and it's a reasonable bet that it matters more, not less, when the broader market is this unsettled.

For members on the distribution and supply side of this business, it's worth asking the same question Reedmere effectively answered years ago: does someone on your team actually own the relationship with the standards you're expected to meet, or is your organization's membership just sitting on an auto-renew with nobody reading what comes through it? If it's the latter, that's a fixable gap, and it starts with picking one person to be the Victor on your team — not because it's exciting work, but because somebody has to be the one who reads the bulletin.

It's also a useful reminder for our own committees. The Membership & Outreach Committee hears mostly from the members who show up, because those are the members it's easiest to hear from. Victor's account is a quiet argument for building at least one channel — a short annual survey, a standing line in the renewal invoice — that gives a silent corporate member an easy way to say what they actually need, even if what they need is simply that the bulletin keeps arriving on schedule and the standards inside it keep being worth forwarding.
