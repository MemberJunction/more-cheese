Several members wrote in over the past two weeks with a version of the same question: production is down, the herd looks fine on paper, and nobody can quite say why. The short answer, this time of year, is usually the weather. A sustained stretch of heat and humidity pulls milk yield down well before a cow shows any obvious sign of distress, and a state extension bulletin on recognizing heat stress in dairy herds makes a point worth repeating: the visible signs — heavy panting, reduced feed intake, cows bunching near water — often show up after the milk numbers have already started to slip, not before. It can take more than a week of cooler nights before output climbs back to where it was.

What tends to surprise members isn't the volume drop — most herd managers plan for some of that every summer. It's the component shift underneath it. Butterfat and protein both tend to soften during a heat run, which matters twice over if you're paying on a component basis and matters again if you're pulling milk straight into a make room that was calibrated on spring numbers. A vat that behaved predictably in April can throw an unfamiliar curd set in July on milk that looks, by volume, almost the same. Trade coverage of this summer's conditions has been blunt about the stakes: persistent heat doesn't just trim output, it can widen the gap between what a herd could earn under normal conditions and what it actually earns once the fat and protein numbers are factored in.

## Reading the herd, not just the tank

The practical fix isn't dramatic. It's paying closer attention to a few numbers you may already be tracking loosely: respiration rate, rumination time if you have the tools for it, and — the one people skip — actual barn temperature and humidity rather than the number on the weather app for the nearest town. Shade, airflow, and water access do most of the real work; supplemental cooling helps but loses ground fast once conditions get extreme, so it's worth checking your setup before the worst weeks hit rather than during them. One thing worth building into your routine now, before the peak of summer: a simple daily log of barn conditions alongside your bulk tank numbers. It sounds tedious in June and pays for itself in August.

On the make-room side, this is a good stretch to run a slightly more conservative set time and to taste-check more often than usual. If your moisture content keeps landing high, don't chase it by extending affinage on instinct alone — go back to your make sheet and see whether the milk itself has already told you something. A curd that behaves differently isn't always a mistake in your process; sometimes it's the last stage catching up to a change that happened upstream, in the barn, days earlier.
