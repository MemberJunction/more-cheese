Holiday gift assortments are, for a lot of members, the single most profitable thing they'll sell all year — and the thing they spend the least time thinking about as marketing. The cheese is usually the easy part. What separates a box that gets bought once from one that gets bought every December is almost always the card, the insert, or the two paragraphs on the website that tell a customer what they're actually giving.

Our Storytelling & Provenance in Marketing course exists for exactly this gap. It's built around a simple premise: a gift buyer is rarely a cheese expert, and they're not buying flavor notes, they're buying a reason the gift feels considered. The course spends its early sessions on a distinction a lot of members find useful once they hear it named — the difference between a fact about a cheese and a story about it.

## Facts aren't stories

"Aged eight months in a cave-conditioned room" is a fact. It's accurate, and it belongs on a spec sheet. It is not, by itself, a reason someone picks your cheese over the one next to it in a gift catalog. A story does something a fact doesn't: it gives the buyer a sentence they can repeat when they hand the box over. "This is made by a family that's been doing this on the same land for three generations" is a story, even when the underlying fact — years in operation, herd size, cave conditions — is the same information dressed differently.

The course is careful not to tip this into invention. Every story it teaches you to build has to trace back to something true about your operation: how you actually source milk, why you actually chose a particular rind treatment, what actually changed the year you started direct sales. Members who've taken it describe the real skill as translation, not creativity — taking something true and technical and finding the sentence that makes a non-expert care about it.

## What this looks like on a gift box

Concretely, the course walks through building a one-paragraph story for an assortment card: who made it, one specific and true detail about how, and one sentence about why it's worth giving as a gift rather than just buying for yourself. It also covers a mistake many members make without noticing — writing the story for other cheesemakers instead of for the recipient's mother-in-law. Technical language that would impress a judge often falls flat, or worse, feels intimidating, on a card meant to sit under a tree.

Trade coverage of this year's holiday product launches offered a useful example of the principle at work, even outside gift boxes specifically. Among the new seasonal releases reported this fall, one creamery built its entire holiday marketing around a single operational detail — how the milk was produced — rather than around the cheese itself. It's a reminder that the most memorable story on a package is rarely the most obvious one. A luxury gift-basket roundup covering this year's high end of the category made a similar point from the buyer's side: the baskets that got singled out for attention weren't the ones with the most items in them, they were the ones with a clear point of view about why that specific combination existed.

That's the gap the course is trying to close for members who've never had to write copy before. A good make sheet tells you everything true about a cheese. It doesn't tell you which one true thing a stranger will actually remember.
