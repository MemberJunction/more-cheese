We get a version of this question every few months, and we got it twice this week alone: "I already wrote a food safety plan for my facility — why would I also need the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate?" It's a fair question, and the short answer is that the plan and the credential are doing two different jobs.

Your written food safety plan is a document specific to your operation: your hazards, your critical control points, your monitoring records. The certificate is evidence that you — the person, not the facility — understand the framework well enough to build that document correctly, defend it to an inspector, and update it when your process changes. A facility can have a technically compliant plan sitting in a binder that nobody on staff actually understands. The credential is aimed at closing that gap.

## HACCP and the newer preventive-controls language

Here's where it gets genuinely confusing, and we don't think members are wrong to find it confusing. Traditional HACCP identifies critical control points — the specific steps where a hazard can be controlled, like a pasteurization temperature or a pH check before aging. The newer preventive-controls framework that's layered on top asks for a broader set of controls: process, sanitation, allergen, and supply-chain, not just the classic CCPs. Our certificate curriculum teaches both frameworks side by side, because in practice you're expected to speak both languages — the older HACCP vocabulary that shows up in a lot of legacy paperwork, and the newer preventive-controls vocabulary that's in current guidance.

If you took a HACCP-only course years ago and haven't looked at it since, that's the gap worth closing. The material didn't get harder; there's just more of it, and inspectors increasingly expect a facility to show both.

## Who this is for, and what it isn't

The certificate is built for anyone with hands-on responsibility for a facility's safety plan — not just the owner. We've had assistant cheesemakers and quality staff take it specifically because they're the ones actually filling out the daily monitoring logs, and the exam rewards people who understand *why* a control point matters, not just how to fill in the form. It pairs naturally with our Listeria Control in Small Creameries course, which several members have taken as direct preparation before sitting the certificate exam — the course goes deep on environmental monitoring, which is one of the sections people tell us they underestimate.

As one assistant cheesemaker who sat the exam this spring told us, "I'd been filling out the same monitoring log for two years without really knowing what half of it was protecting against — the course was the first time it clicked." That's the outcome we're aiming for.

One thing the certificate is not: a substitute for your facility's own written plan, and not a guarantee of compliance on its own. It's evidence of understanding, not a document you can hand an inspector in place of your own paperwork. If you're weighing whether to enroll, the Food Safety Committee holds office hours most months and can tell you honestly whether it's the right next step for where your operation is now, or whether Listeria Control alone gets you what you need this year.
