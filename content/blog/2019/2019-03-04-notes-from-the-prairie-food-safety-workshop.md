A little over two dozen members spent last Wednesday in a conference room outside Lancaster for the federation's Prairie Food Safety workshop, and by the feedback we've gotten since, it was worth the drive. This wasn't a lecture on why food safety matters — everyone in that room already knew that. It was three hours on the paperwork that proves it, which turns out to be the part most small creameries actually struggle with.

The workshop leaned hard on environmental monitoring: where to swab, how often, and what to do with a result you don't like. A surprising amount of the discussion was about the "don't like" part. Several attendees admitted they'd never written down what happens after a positive swab — they just handled it, informally, and moved on. That's the gap regulators care about, and it's the gap that gets creameries in trouble even when the cheese itself is fine.

## What changed by Thursday

We heard from a handful of attendees who went home and rewrote their sanitation logs before the weekend. The most common fix wasn't dramatic: a one-page corrective-action form, filled in every time a swab comes back positive, kept in the same binder as the results. It sounds small. It's also exactly the kind of documentation an inspector asks for first, and the kind that's hardest to reconstruct after the fact if you didn't keep it in real time.

A smaller group asked about qualified exemptions — the provisions that let very small manufacturers meet a lighter version of the hazard-analysis requirement if they can show they qualify and keep the required records. It's a real option for creameries under the relevant production and revenue thresholds, but "lighter" doesn't mean "none." You still need to document your process, still need to train your people, and still need to be able to produce the paperwork on request. We'd rather members hear that from us than from an inspector.

A few attendees pushed back gently on the time commitment, and it's a fair complaint: this quarter's federal feed-cost figures put the income-over-feed-cost margin under nine dollars per hundredweight for three months running, which means plenty of small creameries are watching every hour of unpaid staff time closely. Our answer was blunt — the paperwork is cheaper than the alternative. A documented corrective-action process takes an afternoon to build. An undocumented food-safety failure, on a margin this tight, can take a creamery down.

The workshop leader kept coming back to one idea: a food-safety plan that only exists in someone's memory isn't a plan, it's a habit, and habits don't survive a change in staff or a bad week. Write it down while it's easy, not after an inspector asks for it. That applies as much to a two-person creamery as to a facility with a dedicated quality manager — arguably more, since a small operation has fewer people to catch a gap if the one person who knows the process is out sick.

Attendees also spent time on traceability basics: lot codes, batch records, and how far back you'd actually need to trace a wheel if a customer called with a complaint. Most rooms could answer "what's in this batch" but stalled on "which other batches touched the same equipment that day." That's the harder question, and it's the one that matters most if something does go wrong.

## Where this goes next

If you couldn't make it to Lancaster, the same material is folded into our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate coursework, and the Food Safety Committee is collecting workshop feedback to shape what gets covered at future sessions. If your corrective-action process is still "in someone's head," that's the fix to make this month, not after your next inspection. Reach out to the committee if you want a copy of the corrective-action template we handed out on the day.
