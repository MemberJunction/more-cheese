If you've been following the debate over which products get to use words like "milk" or "cheese" on their packaging, you already know it's been a busy few months. The federal food regulator closed a public comment period earlier this year on how dairy names are used to label plant-based products, and it landed over ten thousand submissions — a sign of how much attention the underlying labelling rules are getting right now, from all sides of the aisle.

We don't have a position on the broader policy fight, and we're not going to pretend it's simple. But the attention is a useful prompt for a much smaller, much more practical question that applies to every member regardless of where you land on that debate: do you actually know what your own label is required to say, and can you defend it if someone asks?

## The rules were already more specific than most people think

Dairy product names are governed by federal standards of identity — definitions that specify what has to be true about a product before it can carry a given name. Most members who've been making cheese for years know the broad strokes instinctively, but the specifics matter more than instinct once an inspector or a retail buyer asks a pointed question. What counts as the required moisture content for a style claiming a specific name. Whether an ingredient substitution changes what you're allowed to call the finished product. Whether your net-weight statement, your ingredient list order, and your allergen declarations are formatted the way the rule actually requires, not the way a template you copied years ago happens to format them.

Small producers are the ones most likely to get this wrong innocently, not because they're careless but because label compliance rarely gets revisited once a design is finalized. A label built correctly in year one can quietly go out of compliance in year four if an ingredient supplier changes something in the recipe, or if a labelling requirement is updated, and nobody circles back to check the packaging against the current rule.

## What to actually check this spring

Pull your current labels and compare them line by line against the standard of identity for whatever name you're claiming, not against what your last inspection happened to catch — an inspector checking one thing doesn't mean everything else on the label is correct. Pay particular attention to any product where you've changed a supplier, an ingredient, or a formulation in the last year or two, since that's where drift most often creeps in unnoticed. If a retail buyer has ever asked you a labelling question you couldn't answer cleanly on the spot, that's a signal worth taking seriously rather than shrugging off.

If your team has never gone through this kind of review, or if you inherited your labelling program from someone who's since left the business, our *Labelling Law & Compliance* course walks through the standards of identity and the required label elements in enough detail that you can check your own packaging with confidence, rather than hoping nobody looks too closely. Whatever happens with the broader plant-based labelling debate, getting your own label right is squarely within your control, and it's worth treating as a spring task rather than something you get to eventually.
