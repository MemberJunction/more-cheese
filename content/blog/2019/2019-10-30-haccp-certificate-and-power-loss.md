Monday's post on this month's California power shutoffs drew more replies than we expected, and
a few of them asked the same follow-up question: does the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate
actually prepare a cheesemaker for something like a multi-day outage, or is it mostly paperwork
for a hazard that never shows up? It's a fair question, and the honest answer is: mostly the
second part, framed correctly.

## What the credential is actually built around

The Food Safety & HACCP Certificate isn't a generator-sizing course, and it was never meant to
be. What it teaches is the discipline behind identifying a critical control point, setting a
measurable limit for it, and — the part that matters most in an outage — knowing in advance what
you do when that limit gets crossed. Standard HACCP guidance treats a power failure the same way
it treats an employee error or an equipment malfunction: as one of several possible causes of a
loss of control at a critical control point, all of which get the same written response. A
cave's temperature and humidity range is a textbook critical control point, and a utility-ordered
blackout is just one of many things that can push it out of range. A compressor failure or a bad
thermostat does the same thing on a smaller, less dramatic scale, and the corrective-action
response is structurally identical either way.

Where the certificate earns its keep during an actual event isn't the moment the power goes out.
It's the plan written weeks or months earlier that tells you, without debate, exactly what
corrective action to take and what to document when a limit is exceeded — how the affected
product gets evaluated, whether it can be reprocessed or diverted, and what record proves you
made that call deliberately rather than after the fact. Members who've been through the
credential told us this fall that the outage didn't feel like an emergency requiring new judgment
calls — it felt like executing a plan they'd already written down, which is close to the exact
outcome the certificate is designed to produce.

## Where the credential stops and your own plan starts

The certificate teaches the framework — hazard analysis, critical limits, monitoring,
corrective action, verification, recordkeeping — but it doesn't hand you a finished plan
specific to your cave, your compressor, or your region's utility risk. That part is homework,
and it's homework worth doing outside of an actual outage rather than during one. A generator
sized to your equipment load, a written order of what gets powered first, and a paper temperature
log for when your monitoring system itself loses power are all decisions the credential trains
you to make deliberately, not decisions the credential makes for you. One member finishing the
program this fall described it as "less about the binder they hand you at the end and more about
the argument you have with yourself, in advance, about what you'll actually do."

If you're weighing whether to pursue the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate, this fall's run of
outages is as good a case study as any for why the discipline pays off before the emergency, not
during it. The credential doesn't require an existing food-safety plan to enroll, and members
who complete it often say the coursework is the first time they wrote one down instead of
carrying it in their head. Enrollment runs on a rolling basis, and the coursework typically pairs
well with a hands-on session like Cave Management or Maturation Planning for Small Rooms if
you're building out a cold-chain contingency plan alongside the certification itself. The
Education Committee can walk you through enrollment, and the Food Safety Committee is the right
group to loop in once you're building the plan itself.
