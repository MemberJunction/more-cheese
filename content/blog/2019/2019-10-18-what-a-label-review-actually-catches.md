Two weeks into this fall's Labeling Law & Compliance cohort, the instructors tell us the
same thing every year: almost nobody who takes this course is trying to mislead a customer.
The mistakes they catch are almost always inherited ones — a label template built by a label
printer five years ago, copied forward every time a new batch goes out, never checked against
the rule that changed in the meantime.

The federal standards-of-identity framework that governs what you can legally call a cheese —
what moisture content, what fat-in-solids ratio, what process a wheel has to go through before
it earns a name like "cheddar" — is older than most of the businesses making cheese under it
today, and it rarely moves fast. What does move, more often than members expect, is the
supporting guidance around it: nutrition-facts formatting, allergen declaration language, and
the deadlines producers have to hit to comply. The compliance date for the current round of
nutrition-facts changes was just pushed back this year to give smaller manufacturers more
runway, which is a reminder that "the rule" isn't one fixed thing — it's a moving target with
its own calendar, separate from your recipe.

## The five things that come up every cohort

Course instructors keep an informal running list of what trips people up most. Aging claims
are near the top: a label that says "aged" needs to mean something specific and defensible,
not just "however long we happened to hold it this batch." Allergen statements are next —
easy to get right, easy to let go stale when a recipe changes and the label doesn't catch up.
Net weight declarations, especially on hand-cut retail wedges, are a perennial source of
warning letters. Country-of-origin language on anything with an imported component needs to
be precise, not approximate. And "raw milk" language has its own rules entirely, separate from
everything else on the label, that a lot of small producers don't realize apply to them until
someone points it out.

None of these are about catching bad actors. They're about the gap between what a label said
when it was designed and what the product actually is now — a gap that opens quietly, one
recipe tweak or one new cheese at a time, until nobody remembers exactly why the label reads
the way it does.

## If you haven't had a label reviewed in a while

You don't need to wait for a full course cohort to get a second set of eyes on a single label.
Members can request a labeling review through the Food Safety Committee at any point in the
year, and it's one of the fastest ways to catch a problem before a distributor or a retail
partner catches it for you. If you want the fuller grounding — the reasoning behind the rules,
not just a pass/fail on your current label — the Labeling Law & Compliance course runs again
in the spring, and this fall's cohort still has a few seats open for members who want to sit
in on the remaining sessions.
