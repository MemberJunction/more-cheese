Most of our member spotlights ask what someone makes. This one asks what someone buys, and specifically how she decides. A federation member who runs a mid-sized retail cheese program in the upper Midwest has spent years on the buying side of a relationship most producers only see from the selling side, and she agreed to talk through her process on the condition that we not name her shop, since she'd rather her suppliers hear this from her directly than read it first in a newsletter.

"Everyone leads with the cheese," she told us. "The cheese matters, but it's rarely what keeps a producer in rotation." What she keeps a running record of instead — an informal spreadsheet she's updated for years — is closer to reliability data: how consistent a wheel is batch to batch, how a producer handles a bad delivery, whether they tell her about a problem before she finds it or after a customer complains. "A gorgeous wheel from someone who goes quiet when something's wrong isn't worth the shelf space long-term."

## What the scorecard actually tracks

Her system isn't formal in the sense of a shared industry standard — it's her own habit, built up delivery by delivery — but the categories are consistent: timing, batch-to-batch consistency against the producer's own description, and how a producer responds when she flags an issue. She weights that last one heavily. "A producer who argues with me about a delivery that showed up soft is telling me something about every future delivery, not just that one," she said.

She's candid that the system has cost her relationships she liked personally. A couple of small producers she was genuinely rooting for didn't make the cut because their consistency never caught up to their quality on a good day. "That's the hardest part of the job. Liking someone and being able to stock them reliably are two different questions, and you have to be willing to answer them separately."

She's also watching the broader farm economics more closely than she used to. With federal dairy safety-net enrollment shifting this spring as producers move between coverage programs, she says a supplier under margin pressure is often the same supplier whose deliveries start slipping first — not because they're cutting corners deliberately, but because a stretched operation has less slack to absorb a bad week. It's one more reason she treats the scorecard as an early-warning system rather than just a report card after the fact.

## Advice for members building their own version

For members earlier in building a retail program, her advice is to start the record before you think you need it — by the time inconsistency becomes obvious, you've usually already lost track of which delivery was the exception and which was the pattern. She keeps hers simple on purpose: a line per delivery, a short note, nothing that takes more than a minute to fill in after a box comes off the truck. It also gives her something concrete to bring to a producer conversation, rather than a vague impression that something has felt off lately.

If you're weighing how to structure supplier relationships for your own counter or wholesale account, the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with members who've been keeping that kind of record for years and are usually willing to compare notes on what they track and why. It doesn't need to be elaborate to be useful — the value is almost entirely in doing it consistently over a season or two, not in how sophisticated the spreadsheet looks on day one. Start simple, keep it current, and let the pattern tell you which relationships are worth deepening long before a bad delivery forces the question.
