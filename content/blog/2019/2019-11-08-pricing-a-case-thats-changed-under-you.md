Members running a retail case have been fielding a version of the same question all fall: when one input cost shifts — a supplier price increase, a shipping surcharge, a currency move on an import line — do you raise the price on the whole case, or just the item that actually got more expensive?

Most members who've been through a cost shock before land on the second answer, and it's worth walking through why. A specialty case isn't one product — it's a set of items that each carry a different margin, sell at a different pace, and mean something different to a regular customer. Retail margin on specialty and artisan cheese generally runs well above what a conventional block commands, and that spread exists because customers are already paying for scarcity, provenance, and a specific flavor they can't get elsewhere — not because the category tolerates careless pricing. Raising every price by the same percentage to cover one supplier's increase punishes the wheels that didn't get more expensive and can make your whole case look like it moved overnight, which is its own kind of customer-relations problem.

Wholesale markup and retail markup aren't the same number either, and members new to running a counter sometimes conflate the two when they're deciding how much of a cost increase to pass through. What a distributor adds on the way to you is a separate line from what you add on the way to a customer, and a single-supplier cost increase usually lands earlier in that chain than people expect — often before it ever reaches your invoice as a clean, single number.

## Separating cost shock from case strategy

The more useful exercise, and the one our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course spends real time on, is separating what actually costs more from what you're choosing to reposition. If a specific line got more expensive from one supplier, that cost belongs on that line, full stop — pass it through, absorb part of it temporarily, or find a substitute, but keep the decision scoped to the product that actually changed.

Everything else on your case is a separate question: are your margins where you want them generally, independent of whatever prompted this particular increase? That's a good exercise to run once or twice a year regardless of what's driving costs at the moment, and members who already have a rhythm for it told us a cost shock is easier to absorb when they aren't repricing their whole case from a standing start — they already know which items are carrying weight and which aren't.

A rough but useful way to sort a case is by how replaceable each item is to your regulars. A cheese with three or four close substitutes on your shelf can absorb a modest price move without much friction, because a customer who balks can just buy the one next to it. A cheese with no real substitute in your case — the one style you carry that nobody else nearby does — has more room to move on price precisely because a regular who wants it has nowhere else in the shop to go. Sorting your case this way before a cost shock hits means you're not making that judgment call for the first time under pressure.

## The conversation at the counter

The other piece worth planning for is what you tell a regular who asks why their favorite wedge costs more. "The supplier raised prices" is true but thin. Members who've had this conversation well tend to be specific: this particular cost went up on this cheese, we're holding the rest of the case steady, and here's another option in a similar style if you want to try something new while it works itself out. That last part matters — it turns a price complaint into a chance to introduce a customer to a producer they might not have tried otherwise.
