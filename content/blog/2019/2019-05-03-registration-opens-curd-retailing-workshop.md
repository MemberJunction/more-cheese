Registration opened this week for our Workshop: Curd Retailing, scheduled for June 4, and it's a course we've wanted to run for a while for a fairly specific reason: fresh curds are one of the only products in a member's case that's supposed to be sold days, not weeks, after it's made. That changes almost everything about how you talk to a retail buyer.

## Why curds are a different retail conversation

Most of what we teach about wholesale and retail relationships assumes a product with some shelf life to plan around — an aged wheel that can sit in a case for a while without losing what makes it good. Fresh curd doesn't work that way. Its signature squeak, the thing curd buyers actually check for, fades within days as the product ages, which means a retailer stocking curds is managing something closer to produce than to most of what else is in the cheese case. Recent market reporting on the curd category has pointed to steady growth driven partly by flavored and snack-format curds moving through supermarket and grocery channels, which is good news for members chasing that business — but it's growth built on a product that punishes a slow supply chain more than almost anything else we make.

That means the retail pitch has to be different too. A buyer stocking an aged cheese can plan a reorder around a slower cadence. A buyer stocking curds needs a producer who can commit to a delivery schedule tight enough that what's on the shelf on a Tuesday still squeaks on a Thursday, and needs to trust that commitment before they'll give curds real shelf space instead of treating them as an occasional novelty item.

## What the workshop covers

The June 4 session is built around that timing problem specifically: how to structure a delivery schedule a small operation can actually keep, how to talk a retail buyer through why curd freshness windows are shorter than they're used to hearing, and how to price a fast-turnover product without underselling the extra logistics it demands. We'll also walk through packaging and in-store handling questions retail buyers ask before they'll commit shelf space — the kind of question that sinks a first pitch when a producer hasn't thought it through in advance.

This one is aimed squarely at members who are already making curds, or considering it, and want the retail side to catch up with the make side. It pairs well with anything you've already taken on wholesale negotiation, but it doesn't require it — we've built the session to stand on its own for a producer who's never sold into a retail account before. We're asking registrants to bring a rough sense of their current production capacity and delivery radius, because a good part of the session is working through those specifics rather than talking in generalities. A producer two hours from a metro retail cluster faces a different scheduling problem than one who's an easy same-day loop from three grocers, and the workshop leader will spend time with each attendee's actual situation rather than a single hypothetical case. If you've been turned down by a retail buyer before over delivery consistency, bring that story too — it's useful for the group, and it's exactly the kind of objection this session is built to help you answer differently next time.

We've also had members ask whether this workshop is only useful for curds specifically, versus fresh cheese more broadly. The honest answer is that most of what we cover — freshness-window pricing, delivery cadence, and talking a buyer through a shorter shelf life than they're used to — applies to any fast-turnover fresh product, curds included. Curds just happen to be the clearest example, and the one enough members asked about that we built the session around it directly.

Seats are limited, and this workshop has filled before its registration deadline in past years, so if June 4 works for your schedule, don't wait until the week before to sign up. Registration and the full agenda are on the events page of the member portal now, along with a short pre-workshop questionnaire we'd appreciate you filling out ahead of time — it's what lets the instructor tailor the session to the room instead of starting from scratch on the day.
