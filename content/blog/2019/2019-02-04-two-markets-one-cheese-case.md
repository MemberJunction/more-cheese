If you read the dairy trade press over the last fortnight you could come away with two entirely
opposite impressions of the cheese business. One says the sector is buried — commodity blocks
piling up in cold storage, fluid milk prices that have been punishing for three years running,
farms leaving the business every month. The other says demand for good cheese has never been
better and the constraint is supply.

Both are accurate. They are just describing different industries that happen to share a word.

## The numbers our members recognise

A survey of the US artisan and specialty sector published last month is the clearest picture
anyone has had in a while, and the shape of it will be familiar to most of you. The sector has
passed a thousand makers. Roughly three-quarters of them produce 50,000 pounds a year or less.
Specialty cheese is the single largest category in specialty food, comfortably past four billion
dollars at retail.

And then the part that stops the room: around one in five makers did not operate profitably in
the most recent year surveyed, and more than nine in ten named maintaining profitability as an
active concern. Not "a risk to monitor" — a concern, now.

Read those two halves together and you get the actual condition of the sector: growing, admired,
increasingly written about, and thin. Thin margins, thin staffing, thin room for a bad batch or a
cold room that fails in February. A maker turning out 30,000 pounds a year with a waiting list is
not the same animal as a plant with a surplus problem, and policy written for one rarely fits the
other.

The surplus does still reach you, though, and it reaches you through the case. When commodity
cheddar is discounted hard, some share of shoppers who would have picked up a wedge of your
washed-rind buy the cheaper block instead and feel fine about it. Members on the retail side have
been telling us for months that they are holding specialty margin by holding their nerve on price
and doing more talking at the counter, not less. That is a strategy, but it is a strategy that
runs on staff hours.

## A tariff line opened last Friday

The other development worth your attention this week happened on 1 February, when the
Europe–Japan economic partnership entered into force. For cheese specifically it is a genuine
change rather than a rounding error: duties on hard cheeses that had sat near 30% begin a phased
walk down to zero, and a substantial tariff-rate quota opens for fresh, soft, processed and
blue-veined styles.

If you are a European member with any export ambition at all, this is the most interesting thing
to happen to your addressable market in years, and the paperwork is the hard part rather than the
demand. If you are a member outside Europe selling into the same Japanese accounts, you have just
watched a competitor's landed cost fall while yours stayed put. Several of you have already
written to us about exactly that, and it is now firmly on the agenda for the federation's trade
work this year.

We are not going to pretend a trade agreement is a level playing field for a farmstead operation
making eight styles by hand. Export readiness is a real threshold: documentation, labelling,
cold-chain evidence, a buyer who will hold a slot. What we can do is lower the cost of finding out
whether you are close to it. Members thinking about a first export conversation should look at
*Export Documentation Essentials* in the course catalogue, and the Standards Committee is a
sensible first stop on labelling questions before you commit to artwork.

## What we're doing about it

Three things, briefly. The Membership & Outreach Committee is pulling together a picture of where
our small-producer members actually sit on profitability, because a sector-level average is not
much use when you are the one deciding whether to add a second make day. The Education Committee
is reviewing whether *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* is pitched at the right size of
business — early feedback says it assumes a slightly larger operation than most of you run. And
we will keep saying, in every room that will have us, that "dairy" and "specialty cheese" are not
interchangeable words when rules and support programmes get written.

If your own numbers for last year look nothing like the survey's, we would genuinely like to hear
it. Both directions.
