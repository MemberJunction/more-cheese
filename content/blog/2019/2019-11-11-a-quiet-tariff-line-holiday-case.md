A new federal tariff line on a category of imported specialty cheese took effect in mid-October, and it's been the quiet undercurrent of every wholesale conversation we've had with members this month. It's not the kind of thing that shows up as a headline on your invoice — importers absorb some of it, distributors absorb some of it, and by the time a wheel lands in a retail case, the increase has been smoothed out across a few different line items. But it's real, and members who buy or sell imported cheese as part of a mixed case are telling us they're feeling it now, right as holiday ordering ramps up.

We've heard two different reactions from members, and both are reasonable. Retailers who lean heavily on a handful of imported staples are watching their landed cost per wheel move for the first time in a while and are having to decide whether to pass that through on the price tag or eat it through the holiday season and revisit pricing in January. Domestic producers, on the other hand, are seeing a little more room in the case next to their own product, since a tariffed import doesn't undercut a domestic wheel on price the way it did in September. Neither group should expect this to be dramatic. Trade volumes on the affected category were down only modestly through the fall, which suggests the market is absorbing the change rather than reshuffling around it.

## What this means for your case planning

If your holiday case usually leans on a specific imported category, now is the moment to check your supplier's current landed cost rather than working off a price sheet from before mid-October. A few distributors have already reissued updated wholesale pricing for the categories affected; if yours hasn't, it's a reasonable thing to ask about directly, especially before you finalize a holiday order that's larger than your usual monthly volume.

For domestic producers, this is a decent window to make the case for a wheel you might not usually push as hard. A buyer who's absorbing a cost increase on an import line is often receptive to a domestic substitution conversation right now, particularly if you can speak plainly about how your product compares on flavor and aging profile rather than just price. That's a conversation worth having before the holiday order gets locked in, not after.

## What we're watching through December

Cheese sales tend to steady out heading into the holiday season even as production keeps climbing, and this year's wholesale cheddar market has behaved about the way you'd expect for mid-November — neither remarkably tight nor remarkably loose. We don't expect the new tariff line to change that broader picture much on its own, but it's one more variable in a season that already has enough of them. If you're seeing something different on your own supply side, whether that's a supplier quietly raising prices beyond what the tariff alone would explain, or a domestic substitution opportunity that's working better than expected, the Membership & Outreach Committee likes hearing about it. Patterns we hear from three or four members in the same week are exactly the kind of thing that's useful to flag before it becomes a surprise for everyone else in January.
