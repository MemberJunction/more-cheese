One of our members owns a small farmstead operation, and if you asked, they'd tell you they
don't have time for much beyond the herd, the make room, and the paperwork that comes with
both. They haven't been to an ICF conference in years. What they have done, going back most of
this decade, is sign nearly every advocacy petition the Membership & Outreach Committee has
circulated, add their name to letter campaigns on interstate shipment rules and raw-milk aging
standards, and show up — sometimes literally, sometimes in writing — when a state committee is
taking public comment on something that touches their business.

That adds up. Across their years as a member, this producer has logged more than thirty
separate advocacy actions: petition signatures, coalition meetings, and letters on topics
ranging from FSMA small-producer exemptions to dairy pricing reform. Ask why, and there's
nothing lofty in the answer. "Nobody else is going to write the letter for you" is about as
close as it gets to a mission statement.

## Why the low-visibility work matters

It's easy to assume advocacy happens on a stage — a member testifying at a hearing, a keynote
about the state of the industry. Most of it doesn't. It happens the way this member does it:
reading a notice that a state agriculture committee has opened a comment period, deciding the
issue actually affects the operation, and taking twenty minutes to write something specific
instead of signing a form letter and moving on. Federal legislation touching interstate raw-milk
sales has come up more than once in recent years, and each round, letters from working
farmstead operators have reportedly carried more weight with legislative staff than a generic
industry position — because they came from someone who could describe exactly how the current
rule affects a real production schedule.

This member is also, by their own description, not looking for credit. They've turned down
invitations to speak at events more than once, and the federation has stopped asking, because
pushing someone toward a podium isn't the point. The advocacy record is the contribution. Not
every engaged member looks like the person at the microphone.

## What this means if you've been meaning to get involved

If you've been putting off signing a petition or replying to a call for comment because you
assume it needs to be someone with more time or a bigger operation, this record is worth
sitting with. This member hasn't attended a workshop in ages. They have, however, made
themselves easy to find whenever the Membership & Outreach Committee needs a working producer's
voice on something concrete — raw-milk aging rules, small-producer compliance deadlines,
interstate shipment questions.

The committee circulates advocacy alerts by email when a comment period opens or a letter
campaign is running, and you don't need a title or a speaking slot to take part. If you've been
meaning to add your name to something and haven't gotten around to it, that's the whole ask:
twenty minutes, a specific example from your own operation, and a signature. Reach out to the
Membership & Outreach Committee if you're not already on the advocacy alert list.
