Most of our members are on a calendar that runs January to December, but one of them told us years ago that his real year starts in a cave in December, not on a spreadsheet in January. He's an affineur and owner who joined the federation back in 2019, running a small operation in the Jura region of France, and he ages a limited run of alpine-style wheels each winter that he won't release, sell, or even fully commit to, until he's stood in that cave this week and made a call.

That call is mostly about humidity, not flavor. Alpine wheels this size want to sit in the high-80s to mid-90s percent relative humidity while they develop, and a natural cave in a French winter doesn't hold that number on its own — it drifts as the outside air gets colder and drier, and a keeper who isn't paying attention loses the rind before he loses anything else. Research on cave-aged hard cheeses has shown how much finished-product quality tracks with exactly this kind of temperature and humidity control, not just with the make itself. He checks his hygrometer daily this time of year and adjusts by hand: a few gallons of water worked into the air, sometimes literally poured across the floor, to keep the room from drying out the surface he's spent months building.

## What a December check actually decides

The decision this member makes in mid-December isn't whether the cheese is good — it's whether it's ready to travel. A wheel that's rushed out with a rind that hasn't fully set won't survive shipping the way one that's held the extra few weeks will, and for a member whose main market is a long way from his cave, that distinction matters more than it would for someone selling across a farmers' market table. He's told us before that the easiest mistake in this business is confusing "it tastes right" with "it's ready to move," and December is when that mistake gets expensive if you make it.

He's also one of the members who reminds the rest of us that "the industry" doesn't run on one country's calendar or one country's rules. What counts as a standard aging period, what a label has to say, even what counts as an acceptable moisture reading, shifts depending on where a wheel is made and where it's headed — and members shipping across those lines are usually the first to notice when a rule on one side of a border doesn't line up cleanly with the other. He's mentioned more than once that a wheel he's proud of at home still has to clear a completely different set of expectations once it lands somewhere else.
## If your own cave is fighting you this week

If you're dealing with the same seasonal drift — a cave or box that's drying out faster than it did in October — you're not imagining it. Winter air holds less moisture, and an aging space that was stable all fall can need active correction once the cold really sets in. A saturated salt solution in a small container, a humidifier, or simply water added on a schedule can hold the line, and it's worth checking your readings twice as often as you did a few months ago rather than trusting that nothing's changed.
