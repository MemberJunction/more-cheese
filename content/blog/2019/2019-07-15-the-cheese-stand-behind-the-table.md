Mid-July is peak season for the market stand, and a lot of you are still doing this. Producers who've built a following at farmers markets tell a consistent story: going deep in one place, building a following of regulars who know your name and your cheese, does more for a small operation long-term than chasing a wider footprint before you're ready for it. The folding table hasn't gone anywhere. It's just gotten more competitive.

We hear from a lot of newer members that a market stand feels like the easy entry point — no wholesale contract, no distributor relationship, just a table and a cooler. It is the easy entry point. It is not, several of you have told us, the easy job. A market table is also, for a lot of small producers, the closest thing they have to direct customer research: the conversations you have across that table tell you faster than any spreadsheet which cheese is actually working and which one just sounded good on paper.

## What actually separates a good stand from a struggling one

The producers who do well at this tell us it isn't the cheese that changes their weekend, it's everything around it. A consistent, legible sign. A price list that doesn't make a customer do math. Samples cut small enough to move a line along instead of turning into a picnic. And — this comes up constantly — knowing your own numbers well enough to answer "why does this cost more than the grocery store" in one sentence, without sounding defensive about it.

Temperature control is the other recurring theme, and it's less optional than it looks from behind the table. A wheel that's fine in a cooler at 8 a.m. is a different product by 1 p.m. on a July Saturday, and a market customer who buys a warm, weeping piece of cheese doesn't come back next week to give you a second chance. If your cooler setup hasn't been rethought since your first season, this is a good week to rethink it.

The producers who scale successfully, by most accounts, don't abandon the market table once wholesale or online orders pick up — they keep it, because it's still the fastest, cheapest way to hear directly from the people buying their cheese. That's worth remembering if you're weighing whether to add a second market day or finally build out an online order form: the answer for most of you is both, not either.

## A course built for exactly this

If any of this sounds like the gap between "I make good cheese" and "I run a good market business," our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course covers the ground in between — pricing a table for margin instead of guesswork, building repeat customers instead of one-time sales, and the cold-chain basics that keep a Saturday from turning into a loss. It runs in short cohorts through the season; check the course calendar for the next start date, and if you've got a specific stand problem you want a second opinion on, the Membership & Outreach Committee can put you in touch with a member who's been selling at market for years.

A few practical habits worth adopting before your next Saturday: weigh and price your most popular cut ahead of time instead of eyeballing it at the table, keep a simple tally of what sells out first so next week's cut list isn't a guess, and if you're running the same market all season, ask the organizer whether a better pitch location has opened up. None of this is glamorous, but it's the difference between a stand that breaks even and one that funds the rest of your week.
