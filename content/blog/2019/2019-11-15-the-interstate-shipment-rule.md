If you've been to a committee call or read a recent bulletin, you've probably heard some version of "the interstate shipment rule" mentioned without a full explanation of what it actually is. It's come up enough this fall that we want to lay it out plainly this week, because the underlying rulemaking affects a real slice of our membership and the record is still open for comment.

At issue is how aged raw-milk cheese is treated when it crosses state lines for retail sale, specifically the aging-period standard that determines whether a wheel made from unpasteurized milk can be shipped and sold outside the state where it was produced. The standard has existed in roughly its current form for decades, and periodically a regulator revisits whether the science underlying it still holds up, or whether newer testing and monitoring practices justify a different threshold. This is one of those periodic revisits, and it's drawn more attention than usual because it touches both food-safety substance and a fair amount of interstate commerce for small producers who've built a mail-order or regional wholesale business around a raw-milk product.

Members outside the raw-milk category sometimes ask why this one gets so much airtime at committee meetings compared with rules that touch more of the membership on paper. Part of the answer is that raw-milk cheese punches above its weight in public attention — a labeling or aging-standard change here tends to get covered well beyond the trade press, in a way that a change to, say, moisture-content tolerances for standardized cheeses generally doesn't. That visibility means the outcome of this particular rulemaking can shape how the public and other regulators think about specialty cheese more broadly, even for members who've never made a raw-milk wheel.

## Where members actually stand

We've deliberately not taken a single federation-wide position on this one, because our members don't agree, and pretending otherwise wouldn't serve anyone. Some producers who age well past the current minimum see no reason the standard should change and worry that any softening gets misread by the public as a safety shortcut. Others, particularly smaller operations using modern environmental monitoring that didn't exist when the current standard was written, feel the threshold hasn't kept pace with what good testing can actually verify. Both positions show up in the comments we're hearing member by member, and both are the kind of thing a regulator benefits from hearing directly rather than through a summary.

There's also a quieter, third group: members who don't have a strong opinion on the science but are worried about the compliance burden of any change, regardless of direction. A shifted aging threshold means updated batch records, revised standard operating procedures, and in some cases retraining staff who've followed the same protocol for years. That operational cost is worth putting into the record too, even if it isn't framed as a position for or against the rule itself.

## What we're asking members to do before the record closes

If interstate shipment of raw-milk product is part of your business, whether you're for, against, or genuinely undecided about changing the current standard, we'd rather you put a short, specific account of your own practice into the record than sit this one out because the topic feels contentious. A paragraph describing your actual aging protocol, your testing regimen, and how the proposed threshold would or wouldn't change what you already do is worth more to a regulator than a general statement of support or opposition.

The Standards Committee has been fielding questions on this rule for a few weeks now and can walk you through the specific docket, the current comparison between the existing standard and what's proposed, and how to frame a comment around your own operation rather than the broader policy debate. If raw-milk aging and shipment isn't part of your business, this one's easy to skip — but if it is, the window to be heard is closing faster than the topic's complexity suggests it should.
