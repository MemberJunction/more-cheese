A first export order feels like good news right up until the paperwork shows up, and then it
feels like a second job. Members who've done it tell us the cheese was never the hard part. The
hard part was the stack of documents that has to be complete, correctly filled out, and in the
right hands before that cheese is allowed to leave the country — and the fact that a single wrong
field can leave a refrigerated pallet sitting at a port while someone tracks down a signature that
should have been there from the start.

Our Export Documentation Essentials course exists because most of what trips people up isn't
exotic. It's the ordinary stuff done slightly wrong: a health certificate signed by the wrong
party, a product classification code that doesn't match what's actually in the box, a cold-chain
log with a gap in it that nobody noticed until customs did. The course walks through each
document a small creamery is likely to need for a first shipment, in the order a customs office
actually wants to see them, rather than the order they occur to a first-time exporter.

## What the course actually covers

Four pieces come up in nearly every member call about a stalled shipment, and the course spends a
full session on each one.

**Health certificates.** Who is authorized to sign one for your specific product category, how
long it stays valid, and why a certificate written for the wrong destination country is worse than
no certificate at all — it gets flagged as incorrect rather than simply questioned as missing, and
that flag can hold a shipment far longer.

**Product classification codes.** The tariff classification that determines duty rates and which
import rules apply on the other end. Cheese has more sub-codes than people expect, split out by
moisture content and milk type, and picking the closest-sounding code instead of the technically
correct one is the single most common error we see in member accounts of a held shipment.

**Cold-chain documentation.** Proof that the product stayed within its required temperature range
for the entire trip, not just at pickup or at the border. A gap in the log is treated the same as
an actual break in the chain by most customs authorities, whether or not the cheese itself was
ever affected by it.

**The destination country's own import rules.** Every market layers its own labeling, testing, or
pre-notification requirements on top of the exporting country's paperwork, and this is the piece
that changes most often — which is why the course is refreshed for each cohort rather than handed
out once and reused for years.

## Who this is for

If you've shipped internationally before, most of this will be familiar and the value is
concentrated in the country-specific updates. If you haven't shipped yet, we'd push this course
earlier in your planning than most people schedule it. Several members have told us they wished
they'd taken it before they said yes to an export order, not after the pallet was already packed
and the clock was already running against them.

The current cohort is open through the education calendar. If you want the syllabus before you
commit a seat, reach out to the Education Committee — they've been fielding a steady stream of
export questions this quarter and can point you to whichever module answers yours fastest.
