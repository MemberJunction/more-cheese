Every year we hear from a handful of members who've just landed their first export order and are, for the first time, staring down a health certificate, a certificate of origin, and a customs broker's questions about tariff codes. It's a good problem to have. It's also a genuinely different skill set than making the cheese, and it's not one most small creameries have in-house.

Dairy trade between countries runs on quota systems as much as it runs on price. A neighboring market's supply-management framework, for instance, allocates a fixed volume of tariff-rate quota space to cheese and other dairy categories each year — meaning that even a buyer who wants your wheels can be blocked by a quota that's already full, through no fault of either side. That's not something a producer can negotiate around; it's something a producer has to know about before signing a contract, so a shipment doesn't get built around a lane that isn't actually open.

Shipping costs themselves have also been unusually restless lately. A surprise cut to global oil production announced this week is a reminder that ocean freight and air freight rates don't hold still just because your cheese is ready to travel. A quote you got in January isn't guaranteed to hold in April, and a producer who locks in a shipping rate too early can end up eating a difference that a more flexible contract would have absorbed.

## What actually goes wrong

The paperwork failures we hear about aren't dramatic. They're small mismatches: a certificate that lists the wrong establishment number, a product description that doesn't match the tariff classification a broker expects, a missing signature on a form that was otherwise correct. Any one of those can hold a shipment at a border for days, which is a real problem for a perishable, temperature-sensitive product with a shelf life that doesn't pause for customs review.

"I lost a container to a labeling mismatch on my first try," one member told us, a mid-sized creamery that's since become a regular exporter. "Nothing about the cheese was wrong. The form said one thing and the box said another, and that was enough." Their advice to a first-time exporter: build the paperwork before you build the shipment, not after, and get someone who's done it before to check it.

Another member, a smaller farmstead producer who exported for the first time last year, told us the quota question caught her off guard more than the forms did. "I assumed if a buyer wanted my cheese and I had the cheese, the deal would happen," she said. "Nobody mentioned there was a ceiling on the whole category that had nothing to do with either of us. I found that out three weeks into negotiating, and it cost us the timeline we'd planned around."

## Where to start

Our Export Documentation Essentials course covers the actual forms — health certificates, certificates of origin, commercial invoices — and how quota systems and tariff classifications affect what you can ship and when. It's built for a producer who's shipped domestically for years and is looking at an international order for the first time, not for a logistics specialist.

If you've got a first export inquiry sitting in your inbox and aren't sure what to ask the buyer before you say yes, that's exactly the situation this course is built around. Talk to a customs broker early, ask about quota availability before you quote a price, and don't let excitement about a new market outrun the paperwork that has to clear before the cheese does.
