Some of our members make cheese. This one sells it. A regional sales director who has held federation membership for a decade, she covers a mix of small farmstead operations and a couple of larger regional creameries, and after ten years on the road she's spent more hours in other people's caves than most of the people who built them. We caught up with her this week to ask what a tight-margin year like this one looks like from the passenger seat of a sales route.

Her territory means she sees the margin squeeze we wrote about earlier this week from both ends. "A farmstead producer feels a bad feed month immediately," she told us. "A bigger operation feels it two or three months later, once it works through their contracts. Either way, everybody's doing the same math this fall, just on a different clock." Federal forecasts for cheese and Class III milk prices were revised downward earlier in the fall, and she says that revision showed up in buyer conversations before it ever showed up in an official number - retail buyers started asking sharper questions about cost long before the data caught up.

## Ten years of the same case, restocked

What's kept her renewing every year since she joined isn't a single big win - it's the accumulation of small ones. She credits the member directory with more sales leads than any trade show booth, and she says the biggest shift in a decade of route-driving has been how much more sophisticated buyers have gotten about asking for provenance. Industry research this year has tracked the same shift more broadly: specialty cheese buyers are paying closer attention to where a cheese comes from, how it's made, and what claims a label can actually back up, not just how it tastes on a cracker. "Ten years ago I'd get asked what a cheese tasted like. Now I get asked what the herd's forage looks like and whether the creamery has a food-safety plan on file. That's a good change. It means the case is doing its job."

She's also candid about the parts of the job that haven't gotten easier. Convincing a new specialty shop to take a chance on an unfamiliar maker still takes the same slow, relationship-first sales cycle it always has, tight margins or not. "You can't rush trust into a cheese case," she said. "You can only keep showing up with a good product and a fair price until they stop asking for a sample and start asking for a case." She's also noticed retail buyers holding smaller, more frequent orders this fall rather than the larger seasonal orders they used to place, a habit she attributes to everyone up and down the chain watching their own margin more closely than usual.

## What she'd tell a newer member

Her advice to newer sales-side members is unglamorous but consistent: track everything, keep your producer relationships honest about what's actually selling versus what you wish were selling, and use the federation's directory before you cold-call. "Half my job is just matching a buyer's actual problem to a producer who already solved it. The federation makes that part easier than it used to be."
