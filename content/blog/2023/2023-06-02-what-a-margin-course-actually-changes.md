A market week like this one tends to send more members toward our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course than a quiet month does. That's a little backwards — the best time to understand your own margin is before a wholesale price swings, not during one — but it's also just how people work, and the course is built to be useful either way.

The course isn't a spreadsheet template you fill in once and forget. It walks through how to separate the costs that move with volume (milk, cultures, packaging) from the ones that don't (rent, the cave's climate control, your own labor), and then how to build a per-wheel or per-pound number you can actually defend when a buyer pushes back on price. For members who've only ever priced by "what the shop down the road charges" or "what feels fair," that separation alone tends to be the part that sticks.

## Why members put it off

We hear the same hesitation every cohort: "I already know what things cost me." Most of the time that's true for the big line items and not true for the small ones — a return shipment, a batch that didn't set right, the hour spent redoing a label because a distributor's spec changed. Those add up quietly, and they're exactly the kind of thing a formula pricing conversation exposes if you haven't already priced for it. One instructor puts it this way in the first session: the course doesn't teach you to charge more, it teaches you to know what you're actually charging for.

The exercise most members remember afterward is a simple one: line up your milk, culture, and packaging costs against your labor and overhead, then see what share of your final price each piece actually represents. For a lot of small operations, milk and direct inputs eat up somewhere close to half the total, and labor on a handcrafted batch takes another meaningful slice — which leaves less room than people expect for the margin that's supposed to cover spoilage, a slow week, or the batch that just doesn't set right. Retail-side members go through a parallel version of the same exercise, comparing what a wedge actually costs to land in the case against what it needs to sell for once shrink and short shelf life are priced in.

Nobody in the course argues for a single "correct" margin, because a farmstead selling direct and a counter buying wholesale are solving different problems. What the course does insist on is that you know your own number cold enough to defend it out loud, whether that's to a distributor asking for a break or a customer asking why the price went up since last spring.

## When the Wheel cohort runs

The Wheel cohort opens later this summer, running as a mix of recorded lessons and a few live working sessions where members bring their own numbers rather than a hypothetical example. It's open to members at any stage, though people running a wholesale account or two tend to get the most immediate use out of it — timing that lines up, this year, with a spring where more than a few of you have had that exact pricing conversation with a buyer already.

If you want to sit in on a session before committing, our Education Committee keeps a short waitlist for auditors on each new cohort. Reach out through the member portal and they'll add you to the list for the next opening, and bring a real month's numbers if you can — the course is far more useful with your own figures in front of you than with a worked example that isn't yours.
