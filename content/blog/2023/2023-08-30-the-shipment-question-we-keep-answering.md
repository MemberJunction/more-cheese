We track which topics generate the most member calls to our advocacy line, and this summer one has stayed on top for three months running: interstate cheese shipment, and specifically what a small producer is and isn't allowed to send across a state line once raw-milk cheese is involved. It's not a new question, but the volume of it lately tells us something about how many members are trying to grow a mail-order or regional-wholesale side of their business for the first time.

## Where the confusion actually sits

The federal baseline is simple to state and complicated to apply: unpasteurized milk itself can't cross state lines for direct human consumption, but hard cheeses aged past a minimum period are treated differently, and states layer their own permitting and labeling rules on top of that federal floor. A member in one state can legally ship a sixty-day aged raw-milk cheddar to a customer two states over; the same member might not be able to ship a fresh raw-milk cheese at all, and the aging clock that makes the difference is measured slightly differently depending on which state's inspector you ask.

That inconsistency is exactly what our advocacy volunteers spend their time on — not trying to change the underlying safety logic, which most members support, but pushing for aging-period definitions and labeling language that read the same way in every state a small producer might actually ship to. A handful of members have submitted testimony this year on comment periods that touch this exact question, describing what it actually costs a two-person creamery to keep a separate label template for every destination state.

## What we're asking for, and what you can do

Our position, consistent with what members have told us directly, is narrow: harmonize the aging-period definition used for interstate raw-milk cheese shipment, and let a single label satisfy every state that already permits the category. We're not asking anyone to loosen a safety threshold, and we've been careful to say so in every comment letter we've filed.

There's a related wrinkle that a lot of smaller operations aren't aware of until it affects them directly: food-safety rulemaking generally includes a qualified exemption for the smallest producers, based on average annual sales over the prior three years, and that threshold shows up differently depending on which rule you're reading and which state agency is enforcing it locally. A member who qualifies for an exemption under one framework can still be fully covered under another, and the paperwork proving which category you fall into is exactly the kind of thing that trips up a two-person creamery scaling into its first out-of-state orders. We'd rather members find this out from us in a newsletter than from an inspector during a shipment review.

If your business ships — or wants to start shipping — raw-milk cheese out of state, two things are worth doing before your next order goes out. First, confirm your destination state's current aging-period rule directly rather than relying on a summary you found last year; several states have adjusted the wording, not the substance, in ways that still matter for compliance. Second, if you've run into a labeling requirement that genuinely doesn't make sense for an operation your size, tell us. The strongest paragraph in any comment letter we've filed this year came from a member describing exactly what a rule change would cost them, not from us describing it in the abstract. The advocacy page on the member portal lists the open comment periods we're currently tracking and a short guide to writing testimony a regulator will actually read.
