If you buy milk under a federal order and you've only ever looked at the headline all-milk price, this is the week to look one line further down. The latest federal mailbox price report shows the announced all-milk price sliding from where it sat a year ago, and the gap between that headline number and what producers actually collect after hauling and marketing deductions is worth understanding before you set your own spring case prices.

We hear from members every January who budget off the headline number because it's the one that gets quoted in the trade press. It's an understandable habit, but it undersells what's actually happening on a farm's books. A wider mailbox spread usually means higher hauling distances, more competition for trucking capacity, or a marketing cooperative absorbing costs it used to pass along more evenly. None of that shows up if you're only tracking the number in the headline.

## The headline number isn't the whole number

If you buy milk directly rather than through a co-op pool, don't budget off the announced all-milk price alone. What producers actually collect after hauling and marketing deductions — often called the mailbox price — typically runs meaningfully below the headline figure, and that gap tends to widen in cold months when trucking costs run high. A few members have told us they build their own supply agreements around the net number their producer actually receives rather than the number quoted in trade coverage, and that habit tends to prevent unpleasant renegotiations later in the year.

We flagged last week that most of you are bracing for a tighter margin year than 2022 gave us. This is one of the quieter reasons why. A wider gap between the announced price and the actual net price usually means higher hauling distances, tighter trucking capacity, or a cooperative absorbing costs it used to spread more evenly across its pool. None of that shows up if you're only tracking the single number that makes it into a headline.

## Build the extra line into your own sheet

One small-batch buyer in the upper Midwest told us she renegotiates her per-hundredweight terms every January specifically because this gap moves before the headline price does. "I ask my supplier what they're actually netting, not what got announced," she said. "If I priced off the announced number, I'd be pricing against money that never actually changed hands." That's the kind of detail worth building into your own margin model — a second line, not just the one everyone quotes.

If your creamery buys directly from a handful of producer relationships rather than a co-op pool, ask your supplier what they're netting this month. That answer tells you something useful about the year ahead that the headline price won't. If you want a plain-language walkthrough of how federal order and mailbox pricing work, the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a short primer on the member portal — worth ten minutes if you've never read a federal order report end to end.

The practical move stays simple either way: when you set your case prices for spring, build them off what your supplier tells you they're actually netting, not off the number in the trade headline. Your margin will thank you later, even if your spreadsheet needs an extra column to get there.
