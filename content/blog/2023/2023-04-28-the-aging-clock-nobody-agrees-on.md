Every few years, the minimum aging period required before raw-milk cheese can be sold comes back up for review, and every few years it doesn't quite resolve. The current federal standard requires a minimum aging window before sale, and the research behind it has been argued over for more than a decade — some studies say the aging period meaningfully reduces pathogen risk, others say the reduction is real but incomplete, and neither side has produced anything that's settled the question for good. That unresolved middle is exactly where a lot of our raw-milk members live, and it's why this keeps showing up on our advocacy agenda instead of getting checked off it.

It's not just a federal question either. Several state legislatures have been moving on unpasteurized-dairy rules this year, from licensing pathways for small on-farm producers to how aging and testing requirements apply once product crosses into retail. None of that activity changes the federal minimum on its own, but it shapes the environment our advocacy work sits in — a state easing its own on-farm sales rules while the federal aging question stays unresolved creates exactly the kind of patchwork our members have to navigate cheese by cheese, state by state.

We've filed comments on aging-period questions in past review windows, and members have kept the conversation alive between them, because a change to the minimum aging period would land differently depending on the size of your operation. A large producer with dedicated aging rooms can usually absorb a longer requirement without much trouble. A small farmstead cave running close to capacity feels a longer window immediately, in tied-up shelf space and cash sitting in inventory that isn't sellable yet.

## What our members are actually asking for

The federation's position in past comment periods hasn't been "leave the rule exactly as it is" or "eliminate it" — it's been narrower: that any change account for the real difference between a large commercial aging operation and a small cave working with a fraction of the space and a much smaller batch size. One of our longtime members, a Midwest farmstead owner who's filed testimony on raw-milk questions for years, put it plainly in a recent committee call: "Nobody arguing this from a lab is thinking about what an extra thirty days does to a cave the size of a garage. I'm not asking for the science to bend. I'm asking for someone to do the math on my actual shelf space before they pick a number."

That's the kind of input the Food Safety Committee is trying to collect systematically rather than case by case, because a single well-documented example from a small operation tends to carry more weight in a comment filing than a general objection does. If you run a raw-milk operation and have real numbers on what a longer aging window would cost you in space or cash flow, that's exactly the kind of detail worth putting in writing now, before the next review window closes.

## Where this stands and what to do

There's no final decision pending as we publish this, which is normal — these reviews tend to move slowly and reopen periodically rather than resolving once. What members can do now is make sure their specific operational impact is on record before the next comment period, not during the scramble at the end of it. If you want help drafting that kind of comment, the Food Safety Committee has reviewed dozens of member submissions over the years and can tell you honestly what makes one land versus what gets skimmed past.
