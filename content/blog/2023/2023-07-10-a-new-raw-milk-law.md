Earlier this month, a new state law took effect allowing direct-to-consumer sales of raw milk under a specific set of conditions — regular bacteria and antibiotic testing, on-farm sale only, and storage and delivery windows written directly into the statute. It is the kind of change that lands quietly in one state legislature and then gets asked about by members in six other states within the week, because everyone wants to know whether their own state is next.

We want to be precise about what this is and what it isn't. States, not the federal government, decide whether raw milk can be sold within their own borders; the sale or shipment of unpasteurized milk across state lines remains restricted at the federal level regardless of what any one state allows locally. A law like this one changes the rules for a producer selling directly to a consumer inside that state — it does not open any new interstate channel, and it does not change the labeling or safety obligations for anyone shipping cheese across state lines.

## Why the federation gets involved

Our Food Safety Committee doesn't take a position on whether raw milk sales should be legal in a given state; that is a legislative and public-health question outside our lane. What we track closely is the compliance burden a new law creates for members who choose to sell under it — what testing cadence is required, what records need to be kept, what temperature the product has to be held at, and how enforcement typically works in the first year after a law like this passes. In the state we're watching this month, raw milk has to be stored at 45 degrees or below and sold within seven days of production, with bacteria counts capped at a specific threshold before a batch can be distributed at all. Producers cannot sell through a farmers market, a retailer, or a restaurant — only directly from the dairy itself.

Tom Reyes, one of our longest-active members on the advocacy side, has sat through more of these state hearings than almost anyone we know, and his read is consistent: the laws that survive their first legal challenge tend to be the ones with clear, specific testing schedules written into the statute itself, not left to a later regulation written by an agency after the fact. Vague enabling language, in his experience, is what gets contested first.

## What to do if this affects you

If you're in a state considering similar legislation, your public comment period is the moment to push for specificity on testing frequency and recordkeeping, not after the bill has already passed and the details are locked in. If you already sell raw milk products under an existing state allowance, this is a good week to confirm your labeling language still matches your state's current requirements, since these do get amended even after a law is considered settled.

If you already sell raw milk products under an existing state allowance, double-check your on-farm sale restrictions specifically — several states that permit raw milk sales still draw a hard line at farmers markets and retail counters, and a member selling through the wrong channel can lose their allowance entirely over what looks like a small technicality.

If you want to testify or submit written comment on pending legislation in your own state, the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a running list of open comment periods across the country and can help you find the right contact before a bill moves out of committee. We'll keep watching this one as other state legislatures pick it up heading into the fall session.
