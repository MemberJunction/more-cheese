If you sell cheese wholesale, you have probably already felt this in your inbox: a buyer asking whether a price that held all spring is still good. Bulk cheddar block prices spent the second quarter of this year near multi-year lows, then climbed hard through July and into early August, tacking on well over thirty cents a pound in a matter of weeks. For members who set list prices off that benchmark, even loosely, that is not a rounding error. It is a renegotiation.

A few things appear to be driving it. Milk production softened as a hot, dry stretch pushed herds to produce less and cost more to keep comfortable, which tightened the raw milk available for vat-scale cheesemaking. At the same time, some market watchers have started asking whether the run-up went further and faster than the underlying supply story really supports, the kind of question that tends to come up right before a market gives some of its gains back. None of that is unique to any one creamery. It is a market-wide swing, and it landed on everyone's spreadsheet at roughly the same time, whether or not their own costs moved by the same thirty cents.

## What this means if you price off the block

For members whose contracts or price lists reference a benchmark cheddar price, even a legacy habit from years ago, this is a good week to check whether that reference is still doing the job you want it to do. A number that moved thirty-plus cents in a month, and that some in the trade already suspect overshot, is not a stable anchor for your own pricing conversations with a distributor or a retail buyer. If your price list has not moved since spring, you are quietly eating that gap; if it moves again on the way back down, you want to have already decided how you will respond.

It is also worth separating what happened to bulk commodity cheddar from what is happening to your own aged or specialty product. A hard, sudden run-up in a widely traded benchmark says more about milk supply and short-term trading behavior than it does about the cost of running your own cave. If a buyer points to "cheese prices are up" as leverage in either direction, ask which cheese, and which market, before you agree the comparison applies to your wheels. A benchmark built for high-volume commodity cheddar was never designed to price a twelve-month clothbound wheel, and treating it as if it does tends to hurt whichever side of the table did not do the math first.

## Where to take this

Several members have told us they are re-checking their own cost sheets rather than reacting to the benchmark directly, and we think that is the right instinct. If pricing has not been a formal exercise at your operation, ask your rep whether a margin-focused course has an open seat this fall; a surprise thirty-cent swing is exactly the scenario that kind of course is built to help you plan around instead of react to. And if a buyer is leaning on this month's headlines to renegotiate a standing order without your having agreed the comparison holds, our Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded similar questions before and is a reasonable first call before you concede a number you have not checked yourself.
