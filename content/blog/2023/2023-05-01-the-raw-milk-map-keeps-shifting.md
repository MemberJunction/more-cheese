If you make cheese from unpasteurized milk and sell it anywhere near a state line, this is the week to reread your labels. Two more states adopted new on-farm raw-milk sale rules this spring, and the patchwork most of our members already navigate got a little more patchy.

The federal baseline hasn't moved. Milk that crosses a state line still has to be pasteurized, a rule that's been on the books for decades, and the long-standing carve-out for cheese made from raw milk still applies the same way it always has: a minimum 60-day age on the wheel, held at a temperature no warmer than 35°F, and clear labeling that it's unpasteurized. That part of the map is stable. What isn't stable is everything underneath it — the states are where the real variation lives, and that variation is what trips members up.

It's worth knowing where the 60-day figure actually came from, because members ask us this more than you'd expect. It dates back to work done in the late 1940s, when researchers found that certain disease-causing organisms couldn't survive the acidifying, drying environment of an aging wheel past a certain point, and 60 days was chosen with a healthy margin built in on top of that. Food-safety writers and researchers have argued for years about whether the number still holds up against pathogens that behave differently than the ones studied at the time, and that debate hasn't been settled either way. What matters for a working creamery is that the rule is still the rule: age the wheel, label it, and don't treat "the science is debated" as a reason to shortcut either one.

## Two more states, two different rules

This spring, two more Midwestern states passed new on-farm raw-milk sale laws, joining a handful of others in the region that already permit some version of direct farmer-to-consumer sales. One of the new laws caps eligible herds at a small number of dairy animals, requires monthly pathogen testing with results posted at the point of sale, and limits sales to on-farm pickup. The other allows farm sales, delivery, and farmers-market sales for any size operation, and — notably — exempts those sales from the state's usual labeling, testing, and permitting requirements entirely. Two states, adopted the same season, and two genuinely different risk pictures for a producer to operate inside.

None of it touches your cheese directly if you're aging it 60 days and labeling it correctly — that carve-out is federal and doesn't change state to state. But if your creamery also sells fluid raw milk on the farm, or you're advising a member who does, the rules you need to follow are the ones in your specific state, not the ones a neighboring member is following in theirs, and definitely not the loosest version you've heard about from a state three time zones away.

We keep seeing the same mistake: a producer assumes that because raw-milk cheese sales are broadly settled at the federal level, raw-milk fluid sales must work the same way everywhere. They don't, and the gap between "posts monthly test results by law" and "exempt from testing requirements entirely" is exactly the kind of difference that a casual comparison between states glosses right over. "I sell it the way my cousin does two states away" is not a defense anyone wants to test in front of an inspector who only enforces the rule in front of them.

## What to actually check before you ship

Start with two questions. First, is what you're shipping cheese or fluid milk — the federal aging-and-labeling carve-out only covers the cheese. Second, if it's fluid milk sold on-farm or at a farmers market, what does your specific state require this year, not what it required when you last checked. State legislatures have been active on this topic for several sessions running, and "active" here means changing, not settling.

The Standards Committee maintains guidance notes on aging and labeling requirements for members working across state lines, and we'd rather you call before a shipment than after an inspector's visit. If you've had a raw-milk labeling question come up this spring — from a distributor, an auditor, or your own second-guessing — reach out to the committee through the member portal. It's a five-minute conversation now or a much longer one later.
