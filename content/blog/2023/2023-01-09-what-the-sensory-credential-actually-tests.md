We get the same question every January, right after the holiday order rush finally lets up and members have a minute to look at their own professional development again: what's actually on the Sensory Evaluation Certificate exam, and is it worth doing before competition season starts?

The short answer is that it tests something narrower and more useful than "can you describe cheese." Members who've been through it describe two distinct halves. The first is appearance, body, and texture assessment against a defined attribute list, so two evaluators land on the same vocabulary for the same wheel instead of one saying "a little dry" and the other saying "chalky" about the same paste. The second half is aroma and flavor identification, working through a set of reference solutions until you can name a defect or a desirable note by smell alone, not just by comparing it to a memory of something you tasted last year.

## Why this is different from tasting notes

A lot of members already keep tasting logs, and that habit is genuinely useful for a make room. But a tasting log is personal shorthand. The credential is built around a shared standard, the kind you need if you're going to sit on a competition judging table with three other people and produce scores that mean the same thing across all four scoresheets. That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. We've heard from more than one competition chair over the years about judging panels where two experienced tasters disagreed sharply on a wheel, not because either was wrong, but because they were using the same word to mean different things.

That's also why the credential leans hard on defect identification. Knowing that a cheese tastes "off" isn't enough on a judging panel or, frankly, in your own quality process. Being able to say whether what you're tasting is a specific off-flavor tied to a particular fermentation issue versus one tied to storage conditions changes what you do about it. The exam's flavor-and-aroma portion is built to force that kind of specificity, working through prepared reference samples until identification becomes reliable rather than a guess.

## Who tends to take it, and when

Two groups show up for this credential more than any others: members preparing to serve as competition judges, and quality staff who want a formal vocabulary to bring back to their own team. If you're aiming to judge at one of this year's regional showcases, now is a reasonable time to start, since most of our judge-accreditation pathways expect the sensory credential as a prerequisite rather than something you pick up alongside it.

It's also, honestly, a good January project. The pace slows down for a few weeks before spring make schedules ramp up, and working through a structured sensory course is a better use of a quiet week than letting it disappear into inventory counts. If your team already runs informal cupping sessions, ask whether anyone on staff has already got the credential — they're often glad to run a practice session using your own current lineup instead of a generic reference set.

If you're weighing whether to start this year, the Awards & Competition Committee keeps a running list of members willing to talk through what studying for it actually looked like for them, defect by defect. It's a better preview than anything we could write here.
