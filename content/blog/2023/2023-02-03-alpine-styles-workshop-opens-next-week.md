Next Monday, a small group of members will spend a day in Greensboro working through Alpine styles — the washed, pressed, cave-aged wheels that take months to show whether a make decision was right. It's a workshop, not a course, which means one long day instead of a multi-week cohort, and it fills up almost entirely by word of mouth every time we run it.

We're previewing it this week because the trade press coverage coming out of early 2023 keeps landing on the same note: demand for artisan and traditional-style cheese is strong, and shops are rebuilding full-service counters after a few years of leaning on precut and grab-and-go. That's good news for members making Alpine styles specifically, because they're exactly the kind of cheese that a full-service counter needs a knowledgeable buyer to sell — nobody grabs a wedge of an eighteen-month wheel on impulse the way they might a flavored soft cheese.

## What the day covers

The workshop is hands-on rather than lecture-heavy. Members bring wheels from their own caves — some finished, some mid-affinage — and spend the morning on a facilitated tasting that compares rind development, paste texture, and eye formation across the room. The afternoon turns to troubleshooting: what a slow-forming eye pattern usually means, how to read a rind that's drying faster on one side of a wheel than the other, and when a batch that looks off at four months is worth pushing to eight before you write it off.

It is deliberately not a recipe class. Alpine styles vary enough by herd, milk, and cave that a single make sheet doesn't travel well between operations, so the facilitators work from what members bring rather than a fixed formula. If you've been making Alpine styles for a while and want a second set of eyes on a specific batch, that's the actual use case for the day.

## Timing, cost, and what happens if you miss this one

The location rotates by region each time we run this workshop, and Greensboro was next in the cycle — not a statement about the regional cheese scene, just where the calendar landed. The February date has more logic to it: it sits far enough past the holiday sales rush that members have bandwidth to travel, and close enough to spring that wheels started now will be ready for late-summer and fall sales, a window that trade coverage on this year's ingredient and category trends has been flagging as a strong one.

A word on cost, since a few members have asked: this workshop, like all federation events, has its own registration fee that covers the venue and facilitators. We don't publish membership dues or program pricing in this space — check the events calendar on the member portal for the current registration fee and any early-bird window still open before Monday.

Seats for Monday are close to full, but the workshop repeats through the year in different regions, and members can flag interest in a future date through the events page even if this round doesn't work. The Events Committee also takes requests for style focus — if Alpine isn't your category, tell them what is.
