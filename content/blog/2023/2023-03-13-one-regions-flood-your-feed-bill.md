A stretch of unusually severe winter storms has left parts of one of the country's largest dairy regions dealing with flooded fields, evacuated herds, and crops grown specifically to feed dairy cattle sitting underwater. It's a regional story on the surface, and a few members outside that region have asked us this week why we're mentioning it at all. The reason is straightforward: a large enough disruption to feed-growing acreage in one part of the country tends to show up, a few months later, in feed prices everywhere else.

This isn't a new pattern for members who've been through it before — a drought, a hurricane, a hard freeze in a major growing region has a way of arriving at a small creamery's feed bill long after the original event has stopped making headlines. What's different this time is the scale some of the early damage estimates describe, with a meaningful share of one county's cropland affected and dairy operations having had to evacuate cattle entirely, not just manage reduced yields. Recovery support programs are starting to take shape for producers directly affected, and if that's your operation, your county agriculture office is a faster path to help than anything we can offer from here.

## What this does and doesn't mean for your cave

If you buy hay, silage, or purchased feed rather than growing your own, it's worth watching regional feed-price reporting over the next couple of months rather than assuming this is someone else's problem. Feed markets are more national than people expect, and a shortfall concentrated in one growing region can tighten supply and lift prices well outside the flooded counties themselves, especially for anything trucked rather than grown locally. Members who lived through a prior drought year have told us the price move often lags the news cycle by six to eight weeks, which is exactly the window worth paying attention to now.

If you grow your own feed, the direct effect is smaller, but it's still worth checking your own supplier relationships for anything sourced from the affected region — some specialty inputs, and some replacement dairy stock, move through exactly the kind of operations dealing with flood damage right now. A supplier who's normally reliable may simply be underwater, literally, for the next several weeks, and a quiet substitution at your next delivery is a worse surprise than a phone call now.

None of this is a reason to panic-buy feed ahead of a price move that may or may not fully materialize. It's a reason to ask your feed supplier what they're seeing on their end, rather than finding out only when a delivery arrives short or substituted.

## Where we'd point you

Our Membership & Outreach Committee has been collecting notes from members who've weathered similar regional disruptions before — a late frost, a drought year, a bad hurricane season — and can connect you with someone who's planned around a feed-supply shock and can talk through what actually worked. If you're a member in the affected region yourself, reach out directly; we'd rather hear from you about what you actually need than guess at it from the outside, and we can help route you to the disaster-relief resources becoming available as recovery work continues.
