Our bloomy-rind workshop filled the room in Burlington this week, and the conversation ran well past the scheduled close. Bloomy-rind cheeses have been one of the busiest categories in the artisan boom of the last two years, and the session drew a mix of first-year makers still dialing in their first batch and longtime members troubleshooting a rind that has stopped behaving the way it used to.

Most of the questions clustered around timing, not recipe. A bloomy rind lives or dies on the gap between when the surface mold takes hold and when the breakdown of fats and proteins underneath it pushes past a pleasant, mushroomy note into something sharper. That later stage is a genuinely chemical shift, not just a matter of taste: as the rind matures, ammonia compounds build up, and past a certain point they turn a soft, gooey paste into something bitter with a smell members in the room kept comparing to glass cleaner. Several people described the same frustration - a wheel that tasted right at day twelve and wrong at day sixteen, with nothing obviously different in the make. The room's working answer, echoed by more than one experienced maker, was to stop trusting the calendar and start trusting a daily nose-and-thumb check. Write down what you smell and feel, not just what day it is.

## Humidity is doing more work than people give it credit for

A recurring theme: members running smaller caves, especially ones converted from a spare room or a walk-in cooler, are fighting humidity swings that a purpose-built maturation room wouldn't have. Bloomy-rind wheels generally want a narrow humidity band to develop evenly while still letting the rind breathe, and a cave that drifts outside that range for even a day or two can set a rind unevenly on the side nearest the door. If your cave has a hot spot or a draft, rotate wheels through it rather than parking your best batch there and hoping for the best.

One thread that came up more than once: pricing. Several members in the room make an excellent bloomy-rind wheel and undercharge for it, in part because the category reads as "simple" next to an aged wheel that's spent a year in the cave. A bloomy rind that's handled correctly took real, daily attention on a much shorter timeline, and the workshop's consensus was that pricing should reflect that labor curve, not just the total aging time on the label.

A few members also raised the retail side of the same problem: a shop selling a bloomy-rind wheel past its peak, without knowing it, does the producer no favors. One counter manager in the room described training staff to check a wheel's give with a light thumb press before cutting it for a customer, rather than relying on the date it arrived. That's a small habit, but it protects both the shop's reputation with a picky customer and the maker's reputation for a cheese that's genuinely at its best.

## Where this goes next

If you couldn't make it to Burlington, the workshop's core troubleshooting checklist - smell, texture, humidity log, and a simple ammonia-taint decision tree - will be folded into the next Affinage Fundamentals cohort. It's also a good companion to the Sensory Evaluation Certificate for members who want a more structured way to describe what they're tasting instead of relying on "it smells off."
