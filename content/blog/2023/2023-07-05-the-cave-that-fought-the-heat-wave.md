The National Weather Service put out heat alerts covering more than 110 million people over
the Fourth of July holiday this year, the start of a stretch of extreme heat that would go on
to break records across the Southwest through the rest of the month. One of our members spent
that opening week doing something almost nobody outside a cheese cave thinks about: fighting a
heat wave one degree at a time. She runs a small farmstead operation, ages mostly natural-rind
tommes, and told us her aging room climbed three degrees above target before she caught it on
a Monday morning check.

"You don't get a day off from the cave just because the road is buckling outside," she said.
"The wheels don't know it's a holiday week."

## What she actually changed

Her fix wasn't dramatic, which is often the case with cave problems. She moved her most
sensitive wheels — the ones furthest along in their aging cycle — to the coolest shelf she
had, ran her humidifier on a shorter cycle to avoid trapping extra heat, and checked her rind
condition twice a day instead of once until the outside temperature broke. She also pushed
back a planned wash-rind batch she'd meant to start that week, reasoning that a fresh, wetter
rind is more forgiving to delay than to rush into a cave that's already running hot.

None of that required new equipment. It required paying closer attention for about ten days,
and being willing to change a schedule she'd already planned around. She said the instinct to
"just keep going" is strong in July, when farmers' market season and holiday orders both want
attention, but the cave has to come first or there's nothing to sell later in the year.

## The part she's not changing

What she isn't doing is chasing a permanent equipment upgrade based on one hot week. Her
reasoning: a single heat event, however uncomfortable, isn't the same as a shift in her
region's typical summer, and she doesn't want to overbuild for a week that might not repeat.
That's a judgment call every member has to make for their own space and budget — the
federation doesn't have a view on what your climate control setup should be, only that whoever
is managing it should be checking it, especially in a week like this one.

If a hot spell has ever bent your aging schedule the way it bent hers, we'd like to hear the
specifics: what you moved, what you delayed, and what you decided wasn't worth changing. It's
the kind of practical detail that helps the next member facing the same thermometer.

Early July is an awkward moment for a heat event to hit a small operation, because it lands
right on top of farmers' market season and the run-up to holiday-weekend retail traffic. Staff
who would normally be checking the cave twice a day are often pulled toward the counter or the
market tent instead. Our member said the discipline that saved her that week wasn't a new
piece of equipment — it was refusing to let the cave check slide to "later" even on the
busiest sales days of the month. She keeps a paper log by the cave door specifically so a
skipped check is visible at a glance, not just a memory she might trust too much.

She also pointed out something worth repeating to newer members: a cave running a few degrees
warm for a few days isn't automatically a lost batch. It's a reason to watch more closely, not
a reason to panic and rework a schedule that was otherwise working. Knowing the difference
between "watch this" and "fix this now" is, in her words, most of what experience actually
buys you in this business.
