Most members who write to us about the Annual Conference talk about a session they sat in. This month a member wrote to us about a table she stood behind for six hours a day, three days straight, and how much it changed what she wants to do next.

She's spent the last two years behind the counter at a small specialty shop, the kind of job where you learn cheese by selling it rather than by studying it — a wedge at a time, one customer's question at a time. This was her first Annual Conference, and she signed up to help staff the competition showcase table mostly because a friend from her cohort asked her to. By the second day, she told us, she'd started keeping her own notes on which entries drew a crowd and which ones a taster picked up, sniffed, and set back down without a word.

## What the table taught her that the counter hadn't

Running a shop counter teaches you to read a customer fast. Running a competition showcase table taught her something different: how to read a cheese fast, in the ten seconds before someone asks what it is. She started noticing rind patterns and paste color the way an experienced buyer might, not the way a clerk restocking a case usually does. By the third day she was fielding technical questions from other attendees who assumed, reasonably, that anyone standing at that table knew more than a shop clerk typically does.

She's not wrong that the job taught her something real. Selling a wedge and describing an unfamiliar entry to a curious taster draw on some of the same instincts — texture, aroma, how a paste changes across a cut face — but the showcase table asked her to hold a dozen of those impressions in her head at once and put them into words for a stranger, over and over, which the retail counter rarely demands in one sitting. Larger specialty retailers have started treating that kind of tasting fluency as a real skill worth training for on its own, not just something a good clerk eventually picks up.

Part of what made the week work, she said, was that nobody at the table treated her differently for being new to it. A more seasoned monger two seats over kept quietly pointing out cheeses worth a second look, the way anyone might mentor a newer hire on a busy shift, without ever making it feel like a lesson.

## What she's doing about it

She's already asked her shop about covering the Sensory Evaluation Certificate this fall, and she's thinking about where a formal cheesemonger credential might eventually fit into a career she mostly built by saying yes to things. What struck us more than the ambition was the ordinary path there: nobody recruited her into the showcase table, she just said yes to a volunteer shift and paid attention.

If you've got a member on your team who's curious about the tasting and evaluation side of this work but hasn't found an entry point, a competition showcase table is a lower-stakes place to start than a scoring panel. It doesn't require a credential, just a willing afternoon and a member willing to let questions come to them. The Awards & Competition Committee is already thinking about next year's staffing, so if this sounds like someone you know, now's a good time to mention it.

We'll check back in on her next spring, once a sensory course is behind her, to see whether the counter still feels like the whole job or just where it started.
