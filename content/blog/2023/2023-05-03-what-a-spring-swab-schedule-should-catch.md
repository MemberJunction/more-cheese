Every spring we get the same wave of questions from small creameries: their swab results came back a little different than winter's, and they want to know whether that's normal or a warning sign. Usually it's both, which is exactly why *Listeria Control in Small Creameries* is one of the busier courses on our catalog this time of year.

Listeria monocytogenes doesn't care about your production calendar, but it does care about moisture, and spring is when moisture becomes harder to control. Rising ground and outdoor humidity changes how hard your cave's dehumidification has to work to hold a target, drains and floor mats stay damper longer between cleanings, and condensation on cold surfaces becomes more common as outside air warms faster than your cave does. None of that means your operation has a problem. It means your monitoring program has more work to do this quarter than it did in February, and a schedule built for winter conditions can quietly stop catching what it's supposed to catch.

## Where the course actually spends its time

The coursework isn't a lecture on the organism — most members who take it already know the basics. It's built around the harder, more practical question: where do you actually put your swabs, and how often. That means walking through zone-based sampling, the difference between a food-contact surface and a non-food-contact surface that's still worth testing, and how to read a pattern across weeks rather than panicking or relaxing based on any single result. A single positive on a floor drain and a single positive on a cutting board mean very different things, and the course spends real time on why.

It also covers what most small creameries get wrong first: treating environmental monitoring as a pass/fail inspection event instead of an ongoing program. A single clean sweep of results doesn't mean your controls are working — it might mean your sampling plan isn't finding what's actually there. The course walks through building a rotation that actually stresses the areas most likely to harbor the organism, rather than the areas that are easiest to swab: drains, floor-wall junctures, equipment cracks and welds, and anywhere condensation collects and sits.

A point the course keeps coming back to is that a monitoring program is only as good as what happens after a positive result, not the swabbing itself. Finding an occasional positive on a non-food-contact surface isn't a crisis — it's the program working as intended, catching a problem in a place where it can be corrected before it ever reaches a wheel. The mistake is either ignoring an isolated positive because "the finished product tested clean," or overcorrecting into a full shutdown for a result that a documented, risk-based response would have handled in an afternoon. The course spends a full session on writing that response plan before you need it, because nobody writes a calm, well-reasoned corrective-action procedure in the middle of an actual positive result.

## Who should take it now, not in the fall

If your creamery has never had a documented environmental monitoring program, or if the one you have was written by someone who's since left, spring is the right time to fix that — not after a positive result forces the question. The Food Safety Committee has heard from more than one member over the years who wished they'd built the program before an audit asked for it rather than during one, and rebuilding a program under pressure almost always costs more time than building it in a quiet month.

This is also a good course for anyone who inherited a monitoring plan rather than wrote one. If you can't explain why your swab points are where they are, or why the schedule runs on the cadence it does, that's usually a sign the plan was copied from somewhere else rather than built around your specific layout. The course gives you a framework for auditing an inherited plan just as much as it gives you one for building a new one.
