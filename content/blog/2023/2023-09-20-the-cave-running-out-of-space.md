Every September, the same question shows up on our member forum in some form: my cave is full and I still have six weeks of make left before the holidays. It's not a new problem, but the artisan boom of the last couple of years has made it a more common one — more members are making more cheese, and a lot of small caves were sized for a smaller operation than the one they're now running.

Our Cave Management course exists mostly to get ahead of that exact moment, and this is the time of year we point new and returning members toward it. The short version of what it covers: aging space isn't just square footage, it's a rotation plan, and a rotation plan only works if you built it before the shelves filled up.

## Space is a scheduling problem before it's a construction problem

The instinct when a cave runs tight is to think about square footage — build another shelf, rent a second room, buy a used reach-in unit. Sometimes that's the right call, and members running goat and sheep operations often find fall production genuinely does require more room, since milk changes character as animals wind down for the season and a lot of that milk becomes cheese meant for longer aging. But the course spends more time first on a cheaper fix: mapping which wheels are actually ready to move and which are sitting past their optimal window because nobody tracked the date they went in.

A tidy make sheet tells you what you made. It doesn't tell you what's still in the cave and for how long. Members who run a simple aging log — even a spreadsheet with entry date, target date, and current rind condition — consistently find they have more usable space than they thought, because a third of what's sitting on a shelf is either past prime for sale or ready earlier than assumed. Free that up and the "we need more space" problem often gets smaller before you spend a dollar on construction.

The other lesson members report finding most useful: doubling the wheels in a cave doesn't just mean managing twice as much aging, it changes the humidity and airflow math for everyone in the room. Traditional aging caves typically hold somewhere in the range of 50 to 55 degrees and 85 to 90 percent relative humidity, and a cave that held steady at that range with forty wheels can behave differently at eighty, because more surface area is releasing moisture into the same air volume. If you've added product this year without touching your climate controls, that's worth a look before winter's drier outside air starts pulling moisture out of the room in the other direction.

A member running a small farmstead cave told us she solved most of her space problem last fall not by building anything, but by moving her fastest-turning wheels — the ones sold within a few weeks of finishing — to a separate, smaller rack near the door instead of leaving them mixed in with cheeses aging six months or more. It sounds trivial, but it meant she stopped opening the whole cave door and disturbing the long-aging wheels' humidity every time a fast-mover needed pulling.

If your cave feels tight right now, the practical first step is the aging log, not a construction quote — you can start one this week with what you already have on the shelf. From there, the Cave Management course walks through humidity troubleshooting, shelf rotation systems, and the point at which renting overflow space actually pencils out versus building your own. It runs periodically through the fall; check the course catalog for the next cohort, or reach out to the Education Committee if you want to talk through your specific setup before you enroll.
