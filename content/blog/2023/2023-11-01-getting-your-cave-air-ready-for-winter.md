Every fall we get the same wave of calls, and every fall we're a little surprised how many members
are caught off guard by it: the outside air goes dry, a cave that ran fine all summer starts pulling
moisture out of its rinds faster than expected, and nobody adjusted the humidifier settings to
match. It's not a dramatic failure. It's a slow one, which is exactly why it's easy to miss until a
rind that should be developing evenly starts cracking instead.

Cold outside air holds less moisture than warm air, and when that air infiltrates a cave — through
a door seal, a vent, a wall that isn't quite as tight as the summer humidity made it feel — it drops
your cave's relative humidity even if your thermostat reading hasn't moved. Members running smaller
caves with less buffering capacity feel this first, sometimes within days of the first real cold
snap.

## What actually needs checking

Start with your humidification equipment's actual output, not its setting. A humidifier dialed to
the same number it ran at in August may be delivering less moisture into colder, denser air than it
was into warmer air, simply because the physics of evaporation shifted under it. Members who
haven't recalibrated since spring often find their real humidity running five to eight points below
the dial.

Airflow is the second thing worth a fall check. Natural convection changes as outside temperatures
drop, which can quietly speed up or slow down air movement across your shelves depending on how
your cave is vented. A rind that ages evenly in summer can start showing dry spots on one side of
the room and slower development on the other once the seasonal airflow pattern shifts, and the fix
is usually a small adjustment to fans or vents rather than a full equipment overhaul.

Most target ranges for aging cheese sit somewhere between roughly 80 and 98 percent relative
humidity depending on style, and cold, dry air is efficient at pulling a room toward the low end of
that range or below it. Members running larger caves sometimes have enough thermal mass and stored
moisture in walls and floors to buffer the shift for a few weeks before it shows up in the product.
Smaller caves, especially ones converted from a walk-in cooler or a basement room, don't have that
buffer and tend to show the drift almost immediately once outdoor humidity drops.

## Where to learn the rest

This is exactly the territory our *Humidity & Airflow Control* course covers, and fall is the
right time to take it — better to walk through the seasonal adjustments before your holiday
production ramp-up than to troubleshoot mid-crisis in December. The course is built around small
and mid-sized caves specifically, since that's where seasonal humidity swings hit hardest and
where a full climate-control retrofit usually isn't in the budget.

If you're not ready for a full course but want a gut check before the next cold snap, log your
cave's actual humidity and temperature twice a day for a week and compare it against what your
equipment display claims. The gap between the two numbers is usually where the real work starts.
Members who've caught a widening gap early tell us it's a much cheaper fix in November than it is
once a whole batch of rinds has already gone the wrong direction.

A cheaper stopgap some smaller members use while they save for better equipment: a shallow pan of
water near the intake side of the cave, or a damp towel refreshed daily, can buy you a percentage
point or two of humidity while you sort out a longer-term fix. It won't replace a properly sized
humidifier for a working cave carrying real inventory, but it's a reasonable bridge for a week or
two while a part ships or a budget gets approved. Whatever you do, don't wait until a customer
notices the rind before you notice the readout.
