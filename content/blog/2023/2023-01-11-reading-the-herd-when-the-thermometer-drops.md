If you make cheese from your own herd, this is the stretch of January where the weather starts doing some of the talking for you. A sustained cold snap changes what a cow needs to hold body condition, and it can show up in your milk before it shows up anywhere else — a shift in components, a dip in yield, a starter culture that behaves a little differently than it did in October. None of that is mysterious once you know what to watch for, but it catches farmstead producers off guard almost every winter.

The mechanism is straightforward. Once temperatures drop far enough, an animal has to divert energy toward staying warm instead of putting it into milk. Depending on wind, moisture, and how much protection the herd has, that maintenance requirement can climb noticeably above what the same animal needs on a mild fall day. If ration energy density doesn't move with it, something has to give, and it's usually components first, then volume.

## What tends to slip first

Our Milk Quality & Herd Health course spends real time on this because it's one of the more preventable seasonal problems a small operation faces. A few things worth checking now, before a bigger cold snap hits: water access, since intake drops fast if a line or trough freezes even partially, and reduced water intake pulls dry matter intake down with it; bedding depth, since a dry, thick pack does more for an animal's own insulation than most people assume; and appetite or behavior changes, since some of the more serious winter health issues start quietly, with a drop in feed interest before anything else looks wrong.

That last point matters for your make schedule specifically. A subclinical shift in a cow's health status can move component ratios in the bulk tank before it's obvious in the barn, and if you're aging on a tight schedule, a protein or fat swing you didn't account for can change how a wheel develops over the following weeks. Producers who track bulk tank components against a rolling baseline tend to catch this earlier than producers who only look at total volume.

## A practical starting point

You don't need a lab to start. A simple daily walk-through — checking water flow, watching intake at the bunk, and noting any animal that seems slower than usual to come up and eat — catches most of what matters before it becomes a milk quality problem. If you want the fuller version, with the nutritional math behind maintenance requirements and a framework for adjusting rations through a cold stretch, that's the core of the Milk Quality & Herd Health course, and it runs again this quarter.

If your cave is also fighting the cold this week, that's a related but separate problem worth its own look at your temperature and humidity logs. But start with the herd. Whatever shows up in the tank is what you'll be working with for the next several weeks of aging, and it's a lot easier to adjust a ration in January than to explain an off wheel in March.
