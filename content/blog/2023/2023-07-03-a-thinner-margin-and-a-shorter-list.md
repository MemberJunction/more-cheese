Two numbers crossed our desk this week, and neither one is the kind you can wave away over a
cracker plate. The federal supply-and-demand estimate released in mid-June trimmed the
projected all-milk price for the year again, and the federal margin-support program that
dairy farms enroll in has been running dangerously close to its lowest coverage tier all
spring. Read together with a fresh artisan-industry survey showing specialty and farmstead
makers producing fewer distinct cheeses than they were a few years ago, the three describe the
same squeeze from different directions.

The margin story is the blunter of the two. The federal margin-support program's coverage
formula has been sitting near the bottom of its range for months, which is a signal, not a
crisis on its own — but it does mean the buffer that's cushioned members through tighter years
recently is thinner than usual heading into summer. That pressure lands hardest on the members
who buy milk rather than milk their own herd, since a cut wholesale forecast doesn't
automatically show up as a cut price at your loading dock. If you've been meaning to run a
fresh break-even on your core wheel, this is the month. The federation's Pricing & Margin for
Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly that math, and July is a better time to sit with
it than October, when the holiday order sheets start arriving and there's no time to think.

## Fewer wheels, deeper roots

The survey finding is the more interesting one for the long run. After years of makers adding
varieties — a new washed-rind here, an experimental blue there — the trend has quietly
reversed. Producers are narrowing their lineup and, per the same report, leaning harder on
their own websites for sales rather than depending on a single wholesale account. Both moves
read less like retreat than like triage: fewer SKUs to manage the food-safety plan for, fewer
inventory positions tying up a cave that's already running tight through a hot summer.

We hear this from members constantly. A four-cheese lineup that you actually control end to
end — cave space, aging schedule, label runs, and now the online order queue — is proving more
resilient than a twelve-cheese lineup split across accounts you don't. It's not a glamorous
strategy. It also happens to match what the wholesale numbers are telling everyone right now:
a softer, more selective market rewards the maker who knows their own margin cold, not the one
with the longest list.

Beyond the lineup itself, this is also a good week to reread the pricing language in any
contract you sell volume under. Contracts written when milk was expensive can leave money on the table now that it isn't, and
the reverse will be true again before long. If you're weighing whether to trim your own
lineup, talk to your distributor before you decide — a Great Lakes-area member told us her
biggest customer actually wanted fewer, more consistent SKUs, not more, and she'd been
guessing wrong about that for a year.

None of this is advice about what to charge or what to make. It's a nudge to run the numbers
before the fall order cycle locks them in for you.

If you sell raw milk into the wholesale market rather than turning it into cheese yourself,
the same forecast cut matters to you in a different way: a lower projected all-milk price this
year means your milk check is likely to trend down even if your production costs don't. That's
exactly the scenario the margin-support program exists to backstop, and it's worth checking
where your own coverage election sits before the next enrollment window rather than after a
disappointing month lands. The Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded a steady stream of
questions on this from newer dairy members this year, so if the mechanics of the program are
unfamiliar, you are far from the only one asking.

The cheese side and the milk side of our membership don't always feel like they're reading the
same market report, but this month they are.
