Most members we spotlight have a decade or two behind them. This one has eighteen months, and she's spending them well. She's an affinage apprentice at a small mountain creamery, and this fall she's entering a competition for the first time — a washed-rind wheel she's been turning and watching since early summer, one she almost didn't enter at all.

"I kept finding reasons it wasn't ready," she told us. "The rind was a little uneven in one spot, or the paste seemed slightly softer than I wanted at the center. My head cheesemaker finally told me every wheel has a reason not to enter it, and the only question that actually matters is whether it's representative of what we're trying to make." That reframing is the piece she says changed her mind, and it's advice we've heard from more than one longtime judge over the years.

She joined the federation a little over a year ago, mostly for the courses, and has since worked through our sensory-foundations coursework and is partway into a judging-and-competition-prep class ahead of the entry deadline. She says the sensory course did more for her palate than she expected going in — not by teaching her new flavors to look for, but by giving her a shared vocabulary to describe what she was already tasting, so she could compare notes with cheesemakers outside her own small operation for the first time.

The harder shift, she says, has been trusting her own read of the cave over a recipe card. "Early on I wanted a number for everything — humidity, days, temperature — and the cave doesn't always cooperate with a number. My mentor kept telling me to taste the rind and trust what I was tasting, and it took months before I actually believed that was a real skill and not just a nicer way of saying 'guess.'" That tension between measurement and instinct comes up constantly in affinage, and it's part of why the federation keeps running in-person tasting workshops alongside the written curriculum — the two skills reinforce each other more than either one teaches alone.

Competition entries ask for more than a good wheel. Entrants write a short production narrative alongside their submission — style, method, aging time, and what they were aiming for — and she says drafting that narrative forced her to articulate choices she'd been making by feel for months without ever putting them into words. "Writing it down made me notice a couple of things I was doing inconsistently that I probably wouldn't have caught otherwise," she said, which is a benefit past entrants mention often and one the federation doesn't advertise as loudly as it probably should.

## What she'd tell another first-timer

Her advice for anyone else considering a first entry is blunt: enter the wheel you're proud of, not the one you think a judge wants to see. "I almost entered something safer because I thought it was what would score well. My mentor talked me out of that too. If it doesn't sound like you, it's not going to read as confident, even if it's technically fine." Judges tend to agree — technical soundness gets an entry into consideration, but a clear point of view is usually what separates a solid score from a memorable one.

If you're an apprentice sitting on a wheel you're not sure about, competition entry deadlines are posted on the events page, and the Awards & Competition Committee is a good first call if you have questions about the narrative requirement or the judging categories.
