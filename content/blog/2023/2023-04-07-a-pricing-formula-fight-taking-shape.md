There's a policy conversation building this spring that most of our members won't notice until it's further along, and we'd rather flag it early than late. Federal milk pricing formulas — the ones that set the baseline prices behind the milk that goes into your cheese — haven't been substantially revisited in roughly two decades, and pressure has been building from several directions to change that. We expect this to move from quiet stakeholder conversations to a formal review process later this year.

That timeline matters for cheesemakers specifically. Milk pricing reform conversations tend to be driven by fluid-milk and commodity interests, since that's where the volume is, and a formula rewrite aimed at solving problems in the beverage-milk market can still land hard on Class III milk — the class that prices most cheese milk — without anyone intending it to. Class III projections have already been revised more than once this year as forecasters try to catch up with a market that keeps moving, which is part of why a formula rewrite feels overdue to some producers and premature to others.

If the classes get rebalanced to fix one set of complaints, it's worth making sure it doesn't quietly create new ones for cheesemakers, who are a much smaller and quieter voice in this conversation than the fluid-milk processors who tend to drive it.

## Why we're asking members now, not later

Rulemaking on federal pricing formulas tends to run through a hearing process where the record gets built early and rarely reopens later. Once a set of proposals is on the table, the range of realistic outcomes narrows fast. We'd rather hear from a small cheesemaker about how a proposed change would actually hit their milk cost while the proposals are still taking shape, not after a hearing record has already closed around a narrower set of options.

"I don't have the staff to track a pricing hearing on top of running my cave," one member said, a small farmstead producer who buys milk under contract rather than milking her own herd. "What I need is somebody telling me in plain language when it's actually going to affect my milk check, and what to say if it's my turn to say something." That's the role we're trying to play here — translation and timing, not just a policy summary nobody has time to read.

A second member, who sits on a state dairy advisory group in addition to running her own cheese business, put it more bluntly: "Every reform conversation I've sat through in twenty years has been steered by people who never once had to think about what happens to a Class III number after it leaves the farm gate. If cheesemakers don't show up this time, we'll be reading about the outcome instead of shaping it."

## What we're asking for

Right now, we're doing two things: collecting examples from members of how their milk contracts are actually priced against the current formulas, and watching for the point where public comment opens so we can tell members exactly when and how to weigh in. If your milk contract has language tied to Class III or Class IV pricing and you're willing to walk us through it — anonymized, if you'd rather — that's genuinely useful input for us to bring into the conversation on your behalf.

You don't need to become a policy expert to have a stake in this. You need five minutes to tell us how your milk is actually priced, and a willingness to hear from us again when there's something specific to act on. Reach out through the Membership & Outreach Committee, and we'll keep this space updated as the formal process takes shape.
