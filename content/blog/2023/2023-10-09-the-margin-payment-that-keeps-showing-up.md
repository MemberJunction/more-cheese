Feed costs finally came down this year, reaching their lowest level in more than eighteen months by early fall. If you make cheese from your own herd's milk, you'd think that would be good news, and in one narrow sense it is. But the price you get paid for milk has fallen even faster than the price you pay for feed, and the gap between the two — the margin — is what actually determines whether a small dairy has a cushion going into winter.

That gap is exactly what the federal Dairy Margin Coverage program is built to track. Federal data released in early October confirmed an eighth consecutive monthly payment under the program this year, with the covered margin sitting well below the trigger level most enrolled producers selected. For members who buy their milk from someone else's herd, this isn't background noise — it's a preview of what your supplier's next price conversation is going to sound like.

## What this has meant for the cave, not just the barn

We hear from a lot of members who assume margin coverage is a farm-level program that has nothing to do with a creamery that buys milk on contract. That's not quite right. When a dairy's margin compresses for eight months running, contract renewals tend to get tighter, minimum volume commitments get firmer, and small buyers with informal handshake arrangements are usually the first ones asked to sign something more formal. If your supplier relationship has run on a phone call and a good year, this is a reasonable season to ask for it in writing.

It's also worth saying plainly what the program is not. Margin coverage is risk management, not a subsidy tied to any particular cheese style or make schedule, and it pays out on a formula — the gap between the national all-milk price and a feed-cost index — regardless of whether the milk in question ends up in a wheel of something aged eighteen months or a tub of fresh curd sold by Friday. We're not in a position to tell any member whether to enroll, adjust their coverage level, or read the payment as a signal to expand or hold steady. That's a conversation for your own accountant and, if you're the one milking, your own lender.

## The part that's easy to miss in the middle of harvest

What's easy to lose in a season this busy is that a margin recovering slightly does not mean input costs have gone back to where they were. Grain, hay, and diesel all moved up sharply over the past two years, and this fall's modest relief is coming off a much higher base than most of our members were budgeting against in 2021. A margin number that looks stable on a chart can still mean a tighter year than it did the last time the number was in the same range.
