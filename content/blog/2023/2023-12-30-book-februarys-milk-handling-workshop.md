If you milk your own herd, winter changes the job in ways that don't always show up until something's already gone wrong. Animals confined indoors longer, less airflow, more contact between udders and bedding — the contamination risks that a summer pasture handles for you without anyone thinking about it become something you have to actively manage once the herd is inside for months at a time. It's one of the quieter reasons a milk quality problem so often surfaces in February rather than July, even though the habits that caused it usually started back in November.

That's the gap our Workshop: Birch Milk Handling, scheduled for February, is built around. We're flagging it now, at the end of December, because members tell us every year that the workshops they meant to attend were the ones they found out about too late to plan travel or coverage for the creamery.

## What the workshop covers, and who it's for

This isn't a herd-health seminar in the veterinary sense — we leave that to your vet, and nothing in this session is meant to substitute for one. It's a hands-on look at the handling side: parlor sanitation routines that hold up under winter confinement, cooling and holding practices when ambient temperatures swing harder than they do in summer, and the record-keeping habits that let you show an inspector, or just yourself, that a problem got caught early rather than discovered late. Cleaning and disinfection schedules for the parlor and bulk tank get their own block of time, since those routines are the ones most likely to slip when the days get short and chores pile up before dark. For members working through the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate, the workshop content maps directly onto the hazard-analysis section most people find hardest to write from scratch.

If you buy your milk from someone else, this one probably isn't for you — look instead at the sourcing and receiving-inspection material in our food safety coursework. This workshop is aimed squarely at the members milking their own animals, whether that's a two-person farmstead operation or a herd large enough to need a rotating milking schedule. Several members who've taken it in past years said the most useful hour wasn't the formal session at all — it was the informal comparing of notes afterward, where someone finally admitted their bedding routine hadn't changed since a mild November three years ago.

## Why now, not February

Winter workshops fill a specific, small room, and small rooms fill early. If you wait until the January thaw to think about it, you'll likely be looking at a waitlist instead of a seat. Coverage for the creamery while you're away is its own planning problem, and that one gets easier the earlier you start it too. A quick call to whoever's covering your milking schedule while you're gone is worth making before the calendar fills up around you, not after.

Check the events calendar for the exact February date and location, and if you've got a specific handling question you want addressed in the session, our Education Committee is the place to route it before the agenda locks. A quiet week between holidays is, honestly, the best time all year to make that one phone call.
