If your humidity readings have been jumping around more than usual this month, you're not imagining it and you're not alone. We hear from more members about aging-room trouble in March than in almost any other month, and the reason is simple: spring is when the outdoor swing between a cold morning and a warm afternoon is at its widest, and a room that held steady all winter suddenly has to work harder to keep up.

The mechanics are worth understanding even if you've been aging cheese for years. A hard alpine-style wheel wants roughly 80 to 85 percent relative humidity; a bloomy or washed-rind wants closer to 90 to 95. Cheese itself adds moisture to a small room as it ages, which is part of why a cave that felt right in January can start drying out rinds by April once you've pulled some wheels and the room's own humidity load has shifted. A cracked rind losing weight and flavor is usually a humidity problem before it's anything else; a slick, slipping rind with mold you didn't ask for is usually the opposite problem, and both show up more in a season of transition than in the dead of summer or winter.

It's a season when a lot of dairy-processing news makes headlines too, and not just about aging rooms. Trade coverage this winter has spent real time on gentler, non-thermal ways to treat milk before it ever reaches a vat — a reminder that even the most traditional part of this craft, the milk itself, is still being rethought upstream of anything you do in your cave. None of that changes what a small creamery needs to do with a swinging spring room, but it's a useful nudge that "the way we've always done it" is worth re-examining periodically, humidity control included.

## What actually helps

Most of the fixes members tell us work are unglamorous. A cheap hardware-store hygrometer, checked daily rather than trusted from memory, catches drift before it becomes a problem you can see on the wheel. A damp cloth or a shallow bowl of salted water in a dry corner raises humidity gradually; cracking a vent for a few minutes a day keeps stagnant air from concentrating unwanted mold in a room that's running too wet. None of this requires new equipment, just a schedule for checking the room instead of trusting it.

Timing matters as much as the fix itself. Members who check their rooms first thing in the morning, before the day's temperature swing has had a chance to work on the space, tend to catch drift a full day earlier than members who check in the afternoon once the damage is already visible on a rind. If your cave sits against an exterior wall or near a loading door, expect it to be more sensitive to outdoor swings than an interior room, and check it more often during exactly this stretch of the calendar.
