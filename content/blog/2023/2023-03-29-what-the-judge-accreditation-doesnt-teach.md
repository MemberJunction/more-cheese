Every year around this time we get a run of questions about Competition Judge Accreditation,
mostly from members who've judged informally at a regional fair or a members' tasting and want
to know what the credential adds. The short answer: structure, calibration, and a defense for
your score sheet when an entrant disagrees with it. The longer answer is worth walking through,
because the credential covers less ground than people expect, and what it leaves out is where
most new judges actually struggle. It's also, trade coverage reminds us this month, arriving in
a year when export volumes and competition entries alike are climbing, so the judging bench is
under more pressure than usual to hold a consistent line.

## What the credential does cover, and what it doesn't

Accreditation is built around a shared scoring framework — the same categories, in the same
order, applied the same way regardless of which judge is holding the knife. You learn to
separate a flaw that's a defect (an off flavor, a structural fault) from a flaw that's a style
choice you personally don't prefer, which is the single most common source of inconsistent
scoring among untrained judges. You also learn to write a score sheet an entrant can actually
use — specific enough to act on, not just a number. Published quality-scoring research on
sensory evaluation describes the same split: technical defects get scored against a fixed
rubric, while character and style are supposed to be judged against what the maker was going
for, not against a judge's personal preference. Keeping those two categories separate on paper is
easier than keeping them separate after the fifteenth wheel of the day.

The Judging & Competition Preparation course pairs directly with the credential and is the
faster route if you're aiming for it this year: it runs candidates through mock flights against
a trained panel before they sit for accreditation itself, which catches most first-time judging
habits before they become graded.

## What the credential doesn't fully prepare you for

Here's the part accreditation doesn't fully prepare you for: palate fatigue. By entry twenty in
a competitive flight, your ability to distinguish a subtle difference has degraded whether you
notice it or not, and a rushed schedule makes it worse. Sensory training resources are consistent
on the fix — pace flights, use a palate cleanser, and stop between entries — but the discipline of
actually insisting on a break when the room is behind schedule is something judges tell us they
only really learn by sitting through a bad flight once.

The other thing the credential can't teach is composure under an entrant's disappointment. A
producer who's put a year into a wheel and gets a middling score wants an explanation, and wants
it to feel like more than a number. Judges who've done this a few seasons say the skill isn't
softening the score — it's being specific enough that the producer leaves with something to fix
rather than just a feeling of having been judged unfairly.

If you're considering accreditation this year, know both halves of the job going in.

Competition Judge Accreditation is open to members who've completed Sensory Evaluation
Certificate coursework or can document equivalent tasting experience. If you're weighing whether
this is your year, the Awards & Competition Committee can tell you what the current judging
calendar looks like and how many flights a new accreditee typically sits before working
independently — worth knowing before you commit a season to it.
