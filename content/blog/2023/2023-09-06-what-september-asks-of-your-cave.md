Every affineur has a month where the cave stops behaving the way the calendar says it should, and for a lot of members that month is September. Milk yield is typically at its lowest point of the year right now, but the fat and protein inside that milk are already turning the corner, climbing toward the peak they will hit in the dead of winter. That means the curd you set this week is not quite the curd you set in July, even if your recipe hasn't changed at all.

Richer milk makes a different wheel. Higher fat and protein content changes moisture retention, which changes how a rind develops and how fast a paste breaks down. Members who run the same make sheet year-round sometimes notice their cheese "acting older" earlier in the aging cycle come autumn — a washed rind that usually needs six weeks of attention suddenly wants intervention at four, or a natural rind that cracked reliably in August starts holding moisture longer than expected in September. None of this means something is wrong. It means your inputs changed and your cave needs to catch up.

## Reading the cave, not just the recipe

The instinct when a wheel behaves unexpectedly is to check the recipe. The better instinct in September is to check the cave. Most cheeses hold their character best in a fairly narrow humidity band — hard, natural-rind styles want it drier, bloomy and washed-rind styles want it wetter — and small seasonal shifts in ambient humidity outside your building can nudge an otherwise well-calibrated cave out of range before anyone notices. A simple habit that pays off this time of year: pull your hygrometer readings from the last two weeks and compare them to what you logged in July. If the swing is more than a few points, that is often enough to explain a rind that's cracking dry or turning slick.

If your cave runs warm or cool with the weather outside, a low-cost fix members have used successfully is a saturated salt solution set inside the space — a simple mix of table salt and water that settles at a stable relative humidity once it reaches equilibrium, giving you a passive buffer that doesn't need daily adjustment. It won't replace a proper humidification system in a commercial cave, but for a smaller room it is a cheap way to smooth out exactly the kind of week-to-week drift that a seasonal transition creates.

## Where to take this if you want structure

We know a lot of members figure this out by trial and error over a few seasons, which works, but costs product along the way. If you'd rather shortcut that learning curve, our Cave Management course walks through humidity and airflow control specifically for small and mid-sized rooms, including how to build a seasonal adjustment schedule instead of reacting to problems after they show up in the rind. It pairs well with Milk Quality & Herd Health if you're also trying to understand why your own herd's milk is shifting under you this month — the two courses were designed to be taken close together for exactly this reason.

One thing worth saying plainly: don't chase a September problem with a July fix. If a wheel is behaving differently, the most useful question is what changed in your inputs and your environment, not what you might have done wrong in the make. Most of what members describe to us this time of year turns out to be the ordinary cost of a seasonal transition, not a mistake — and the fix is usually a dial, not a redo. If you want a second opinion on a specific wheel or room, reach out to the Education Committee; they field exactly this kind of question every fall and can usually point you to a member who has already solved your particular version of it.
