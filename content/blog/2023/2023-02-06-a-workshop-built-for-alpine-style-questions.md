Our Willow Alpine Styles workshop runs today in Greensboro, Vermont, and if you make anything in the tomme or gruyère family, this is the session members have been asking us to schedule for a while. The question that keeps coming up isn't the recipe — most alpine-style makers already have that dialed in — it's why two cellars running what looks like the same process can turn out wheels with noticeably different rinds and paste.

The short answer is that alpine cheese takes its personality from the room it ages in as much as from the vat. A cave built into a mountain range in central Europe and a converted dairy cooler in Vermont can start with broadly similar milk and technique and still land in different places, because the resident flora on the walls, the airflow, and the exact humidity band all shape which rind cultures win out. Today's workshop walks through that in practical terms for anyone running a domestic cave rather than a mountain one.

## What the session actually covers

Alpine wheels generally want a lower humidity than a soft-ripened or washed-rind cheese — professional affinage rooms often run in the high 80s to high 90s percent humidity depending on style, and alpine sits toward the drier end of that range. Too wet and you get an unwanted surface growth; too dry and the rind cracks before the paste has had time to develop. The workshop spends real time on how to read your own cave's drift over a season and adjust before a problem shows up on a wheel you've already committed months to.

There's also a block on staged aging — moving wheels between conditions partway through the process rather than holding one setting the whole time, which several members have told us made the biggest difference once they started doing it deliberately instead of by accident. If you're three months into a batch and the rind isn't where you expected, that's exactly the kind of case the instructors want to hear about live, rather than after the wheel is already cut and the answer is academic.

The instructors are also spending time this year on a question that's come up more since the trade press started writing about a tighter retail cheese case: how do you make an alpine wheel that reads as distinctive on a crowded shelf without changing the parts of the recipe that make it alpine in the first place? A wash schedule, a rind color, a wax-versus-natural-rind choice — small, defensible variations rather than a different cheese entirely.

Alpine-style production has a rhythm to it that makes late winter a natural teaching window. Wheels made from autumn and early-winter milk are typically far enough into their aging cycle by February that a maker can already see whether a cave adjustment made last fall paid off, but not so far along that a bad call can't still be corrected. That's exactly the stage several of today's attendees are at, and it's part of why the Events Committee scheduled the session for the first full week of the month rather than waiting for spring.

## If you're not in Vermont today

We know not everyone can get to Greensboro on a Monday in February, and the session isn't recorded this time. If alpine styles are a growing part of your program, keep an eye on the events calendar — workshops like this one tend to get a virtual sibling later in the year, and the Events Committee can tell you if one's likely before the next conference cycle. In the meantime, a wheel-by-wheel humidity log is still the cheapest diagnostic tool in the cave, and it's the first thing today's instructors will ask to see.
