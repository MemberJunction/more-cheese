Industry surveys of artisan and specialty cheesemakers have tracked a steady shift over the past several years: formal, written food-safety plans went from something roughly six in ten producers had in place to something closer to nine in ten. That's not a small change. It means a documented HACCP plan has moved from "the thing the bigger operations do" to the baseline expectation across the trade, and it's a big part of why our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate gets more enrollment questions every January than almost any other credential we offer.

We get it. If your cave is full of aged raw milk wheels, food-safety compliance can feel like a moving target — testing standards for raw milk cheese have shifted before at the federal level, and cheesemakers who've been in the business long enough remember what it's like when a criterion they'd built their process around gets reopened for review. So this week we want to walk through what the certificate covers, because there's a gap between what people assume it is and what it actually teaches.

## What the certificate actually tests

The Food Safety & HACCP Certificate isn't a raw milk cheese permit, and it doesn't certify that any specific product meets any specific testing threshold. What it teaches is the hazard analysis and critical control point framework itself: identifying where contamination risk enters your process, documenting the controls that address each point, and building the records that hold up when an inspector — or your own quality manager, six months later — asks how you know your process is working.

For raw milk producers specifically, that means understanding supply-chain controls if you buy milk rather than milk your own herd, aging-and-testing documentation that supports your affinage timeline, and the record-keeping habits that let you demonstrate control even when a testing standard is under review elsewhere in the system. A written plan doesn't freeze your process in place; it gives you a record of your own reasoning, which is exactly what you want on hand if a regulatory criterion you rely on ever moves.

## Who should sign up now, and who can wait

If you're already making aged raw milk cheese and don't hold this credential, this is a reasonable month to change that — not because of any single announcement, but because the rest of the trade has quietly made a written food-safety plan the norm rather than the exception, and a credential is the fastest way to build one properly instead of assembling it piecemeal under pressure. If you're a newer member still deciding whether raw milk production is in your future, the Cheese Foundations Certificate is the better starting point; the HACCP material will make more sense once you've got a make sheet and a few batches under your belt.

Enrollment is open now through the certifications page. If you've already got a HACCP plan on file and just want a second opinion on whether it holds up against current practice, the Food Safety Committee takes plan-review questions year-round — worth a note now, before your next audit, rather than during it.
