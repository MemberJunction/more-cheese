Federal food-safety guidance on controlling Listeria monocytogenes in retail delicatessens was published earlier this month, and it landed with more relevance to our members than most delicatessen-focused documents usually do. If your creamery sells through a retail counter, ages a soft or semi-soft cheese, or ships cut-to-order wedges, the environmental monitoring practices it lays out are close enough to your own operation to be worth reading, not filing away.

Today's free webinar, Listeria Control 2023, walks through the guidance in plain terms: what a self-assessment of your slicing and cutting surfaces should look like, how often environmental swabbing needs to happen to actually catch a problem instead of missing it, and what documentation an inspector will expect to see if your operation is ever the subject of a traceback. It is not a substitute for your written food-safety plan, but it is a good gut check against one.

## Where this connects to a credential

The Food Safety & HACCP Certificate is our shortest-validity credential for a reason — food-safety practice moves faster than most of what we teach, and a certificate that never expired would stop meaning much within a few years. Holders recertify every two years, and guidance like this month's is exactly the kind of update that recertification is built to catch. If your certificate is coming up for renewal this year, today's webinar is a reasonable place to start refreshing your notes before you sit for it.

For creameries building out a fuller monitoring program rather than a one-time review, our longer-running course, Listeria Control in Small Creameries, goes deeper than a single webinar can. It walks a cohort through building an environmental monitoring plan from scratch over several weeks, with feedback on your actual sampling map rather than a generic template. One member who went through an earlier cohort put it this way: "I thought I already had a swab schedule. What I actually had was a schedule for the easy spots and nothing for the drains." That is a common enough gap that it is worth checking your own map against, whether or not you sign up for the full course.

## What to do this week

If you sell any product from a retail case, walk your own cutting and slicing surfaces this week with the new guidance's checklist in hand, even before you watch a recording of today's session. Most of what it asks for is not expensive to fix — a cutting board rotation schedule, a sanitizer log that actually gets filled in — but it is the kind of thing that is much cheaper to fix on your own timeline than on an inspector's.

If you have questions about how this guidance interacts with your existing HACCP plan, the Food Safety Committee has been fielding a steady stream of them this month and would rather hear from you before your next inspection than after.

Members who have already gone through the new guidance tell us it is more manageable than they feared. The core of it is four habits done consistently rather than a long list of new equipment to buy: clean and sanitize food-contact surfaces on a fixed schedule instead of an as-needed one, keep raw and finished product on separate paths through your space wherever your layout allows it, swab in the spots that are hardest to reach rather than the ones that are easiest to remember, and write down what you find even when the result is clean. That last habit is the one inspectors ask about most, because a monitoring log with only passing results in it reads very differently from one with an occasional failure and a documented correction.

None of this replaces judgment specific to your own space — a cave built into a converted barn has different problem spots than a purpose-built aging room, and only someone who has walked your layout can tell you where your particular risks sit. That is exactly what today's webinar and the longer course are built to help with, and it is also a fair thing to ask a Food Safety Committee volunteer to look at with you if you would rather talk it through than read another document.
