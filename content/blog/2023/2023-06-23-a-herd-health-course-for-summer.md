Researchers at a major agricultural university announced a new model this June for assessing heat stress in lactating dairy cows, built to capture something a lot of our farmstead members already feel every summer without needing a study to confirm it: once a herd's temperature-humidity index climbs past a certain threshold, milk yield drops noticeably, and so does the fat and protein content of what's still coming in. A separate review published earlier this year pulled together the growing body of research on this topic, noting that studies on thermal stress in lactating cows have increased considerably as the problem gets more attention industry-wide. Estimates cited in that research put losses from heat stress in the range of a billion and a half dollars a year nationally in reduced production and reproduction problems alone.

If you're a farmstead operation making cheese from your own herd, this isn't abstract. A make sheet built around spring milk composition can start behaving unpredictably in July without any change in your process at all, because the milk itself has changed. Longer set times, softer curd, a yield that comes up short compared to the same recipe run two months earlier — all of that can trace back to a herd under heat stress rather than anything you did wrong at the vat.

## What actually shows up at the bench

Beyond yield, the research points to shifts in milk's microbial and chemical makeup under sustained heat — more psychrotrophic bacteria, which thrive in the kind of borderline refrigeration temperatures a stressed cold chain can drift toward in summer, along with changes to fatty acid profiles that can subtly affect flavor development during aging. None of this means summer milk is unsafe or unusable. It means the variables you're used to controlling shift slightly, and a make process tuned for spring conditions may need small adjustments — a longer set time, a closer eye on whey drainage, tighter temperature control on the cold chain between the parlor and your vat room — to get consistent results through the hottest stretch of the year.

## Where our course fits

Our *Milk Quality & Herd Health* course was built for exactly this kind of seasonal variation, and this is a reasonable month to take it if you haven't already. It walks through how herd nutrition, cooling infrastructure, and even barn ventilation choices affect the milk that eventually reaches your vat — material that's useful whether you manage the herd yourself or just buy from someone who does. For members who source milk rather than milk their own herd, the course is still worth a look: knowing what heat stress does to composition helps you ask your supplier better questions when a batch behaves differently than you expect, rather than assuming the problem is on your end.

Members who've been through the course before have told us the herd-side material changed how they read a make sheet gone slightly sideways — instead of troubleshooting culture or rennet first, they check what the herd's been dealing with. If your cave or your curd has been behaving a little differently the past few weeks and you can't quite place why, that's usually where to look first.
