Most of our credentials are built around the make room — foundations, affinage, food safety. Certified Cheesemonger is the one exception, and members considering it sometimes assume it's a lighter version of those, a credential for people who couldn't get a spot in the harder programs. It isn't. It's a different discipline entirely, built for a different job, and it deserves to be judged on its own terms rather than measured against a make-room credential it was never trying to be.

Cheesemonger work is counter work: cutting a wheel cleanly without wasting the rind, storing dozens of open cheeses at different humidities without letting any of them dry out or sweat, rotating stock so nothing ages past its window on the shelf, and reading a customer well enough in the first ten seconds to know whether they want a recommendation or just want to point at something. None of that shows up in a make-sheet credential, and a head cheesemaker who's spectacular in the cave can still be genuinely bad at all four of those things behind a counter.

## What the credential actually tests

Certified Cheesemonger covers cut-and-wrap technique, storage and rotation practice, and customer guidance — building a balanced case that moves product instead of just looking good under glass. It's a four-year credential, shorter than our flagship professional certificate, which reflects how fast retail practice actually shifts: case layouts, popular styles, and customer expectations move quicker than production technique does, so the recertification window is built to match.

That shift is real and it's happening now. Cheese retailers this year have been telling us the same thing industry commentators are hearing broadly: cut-and-wrap counters are shrinking in some formats while grab-and-go and fixed-weight cheese products keep growing, which changes what "good counter judgment" even means. A monger today isn't just cutting and wrapping to order; increasingly they're also deciding what belongs pre-portioned on a shelf versus what's worth the labor of cutting fresh in front of a customer, and that's a case-management call, not a knife-skill one.

It's proven especially popular with retail members this cycle, which tracks with what we're seeing industry-wide. A recent biennial survey of artisan and specialty cheesemakers found that a large majority of producers run relatively small annual volumes, which means most of our members are selling into a crowded specialty case rather than commanding a dedicated one — the people behind the counter carry real weight in whether a wheel actually moves before it ages past its window. A monger who can explain why a particular cheese is worth the price, in the time it takes to ring up a sale, is doing real commercial work, and this credential is built to recognize that as its own skill rather than a lesser cousin of cheesemaking.

## Who should actually sign up

If your job is mostly behind a counter or building a retail case — whether you're a dedicated cheesemonger, a shop owner working the floor yourself, or a producer who also staffs your own farm stand — this is the credential built for your actual work, not a consolation prize for skipping the harder one. If your work is mostly production and you rarely touch a retail case, our foundation and affinage credentials are still the better starting point.

We hear from members most often when they're weighing this against the flagship Certified Cheese Professional credential, assuming one must be a stepping stone to the other. They aren't sequential. A member who runs both a make room and a retail counter might reasonably hold both eventually, but there's no requirement to pass through one to reach the other, and plenty of long-tenured mongers have no interest in ever taking on production work at all. Either way, the Education Committee can walk you through prerequisites and the next enrollment window before you commit.
