Every year after the competition floor closes, we get a wave of the same question: how do you become one of the people at the judging table? It's a fair thing to ask right after watching a flight of Blue Veined entries get scored down to the point, and the answer is more structured — and more perishable — than most members expect.

## The credential, plainly

Competition Judge Accreditation is our seventh and most specific credential, and it's built on top of one you may already have: you need the Sensory Evaluation Certificate first, because a judge has to be able to name what they're tasting in the same vocabulary the rest of the panel is using. On top of that, accreditation requires supervised panel experience — sitting alongside accredited judges on real entries, not a mock panel, before you're signed off to score independently.

The part that surprises people is that it lapses. Unlike some of our other credentials, Competition Judge Accreditation is valid for two years and then needs renewal, and renewal isn't a formality. It requires active judging in that window. The reasoning is straightforward even if it's a little unforgiving: palate calibration drifts, category standards shift as the field gets more competitive, and a judge who hasn't sat on a panel in three years is scoring against an older version of the category than the one currently in front of them. We'd rather have fewer, current judges than a long list of people who judged once several cycles back and never returned to the table.

## Why this matters more this year

With entries up again this year and categories like Blue Veined and Alpine Styles getting genuinely deep — several strong wheels went home without a placement at Louisville simply because the competition was that tight — we need more accredited judges, not fewer. If you've had the Sensory Evaluation Certificate for a while and have been thinking about the next step, this is a reasonable year to ask about supervised panel slots. They fill up faster than you'd think, mostly because current judges are recruiting people they've already tasted alongside, and a slot that opens in one region can close within days once word gets around.

If you're earlier in the pathway, the Sensory Evaluation Certificate coursework is the place to start, and it's useful even if you never sit on a panel. A lot of members tell us it changed how they read their own wheels before they ever thought about judging someone else's — the same vocabulary that lets a panel agree on a score also gives you a sharper read on your own cave, your own aging curve, and where a batch is drifting before it becomes a problem on the sales floor.

The supervised requirement isn't a single afternoon. It typically runs across at least one full competition cycle, sitting beside an accredited judge on actual entries in your strongest category first, then branching into others as your palate calibrates against a wider range of styles. Supervising judges are asked to sign off honestly, not generously, which is part of why the credential still means something years after a member first earns it. We've heard from a few members that the hardest part isn't the tasting itself but learning to score to the category's written standard instead of to personal preference — a distinction that only becomes intuitive with real repetitions at the table.

It's also worth saying plainly what this credential is for and isn't for. It qualifies you to judge our own competitions; it isn't a general food-safety or quality-control credential, and members occasionally ask us whether it can stand in for HACCP training on a health department form. It can't, and we'd rather correct that now than have someone find out the hard way at an inspection.

Details on both the certificate and the accreditation pathway, including how to apply for a supervised slot, are on the certifications page of the member portal. If your region doesn't currently have an accredited judge taking on supervised candidates, the Awards & Competition Committee keeps a waiting list and can usually pair you with someone at the next event within driving distance.
