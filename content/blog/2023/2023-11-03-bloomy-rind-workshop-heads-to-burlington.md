Next Wednesday, November 8, the federation's bloomy rind workshop comes to Burlington, and the
timing isn't an accident. Bloomy-rind cheeses are some of the least forgiving wheels a maker keeps
on a shelf, and the run-up to the holiday season is when most caves are pushing more bloomy
production through at once than at any other point in the year. A rind mistake made this month
shows up on a retail case in December.

The workshop is built around the part of bloomy production that's hardest to teach from a written
protocol: reading a rind's actual development day by day and adjusting instead of following a fixed
schedule. A washed-curd wheel forgives a day or two of drift. A Camembert-style rind mostly doesn't
— by the time a problem is visible on the surface, the mold has usually already made decisions
underneath it that a maker can't undo.

## What the day actually covers

Expect hands-on work with actual developing rinds at different stages, not just slides. Past
sessions have spent real time on the difference between a rind that's slightly ahead of schedule
and one that's genuinely overripening, since the two can look similar to an inexperienced eye and
call for opposite responses. There's also a block on troubleshooting the most common bloomy failure
modes — a rind that never develops evenly, one that slips prematurely, one that develops a
bitterness the maker can't trace to an ingredient.

One member who took an earlier version of this workshop told us the single most useful thing she
took home wasn't a technique — it was permission to trust her own read on a rind over a printed
timeline. "I'd been treating the schedule like a rule," she said. "It's a starting guess. The rind
tells you when it's actually ready to move to the next stage, and the workshop was the first place
someone walked me through how to actually listen to it."

## Getting there

The Burlington session still has open seats as of this week. It counts toward hours for members
working toward the Advanced Affinage Certificate, and past attendees have told us it pairs well
with our broader course on developing and washing rinds if you want the fuller arc from set curd to
finished wheel. If Burlington doesn't work for your schedule, the Education Committee runs this
workshop in rotation through several regions each year — reach out and we can tell you where it's
headed next.

Either way, don't wait until spring to take it. The lessons apply year-round, but the value of
working through them right now, before the year's heaviest bloomy production stretch, is higher
than taking the same workshop in a slower month. A maker who walks into December already comfortable
reading a rind's actual stage, rather than trusting a printed schedule, tends to lose fewer wheels
to the kind of quiet drift that only becomes obvious once it's too late to correct.

Seats are limited, and the format works best kept small since so much of the day is hands-on with
actual wheels at different points in their development. If you're on the fence, weigh it against
what a single lost batch of holiday-season bloomy wheels actually costs you in product, shelf space,
and a disappointed retail buyer waiting on an order that doesn't show up on time.
