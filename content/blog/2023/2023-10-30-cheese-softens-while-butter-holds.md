The federal dairy outlook released this month raised its 2023 milk production forecast a little,
mostly on stronger yield per cow, and split cheese and butter into two different stories. Cheese
came in lower again, roughly a third below where it sat a year ago. Butter went the other
direction, up on firmer demand. If your Class III math has felt off this fall, that split is most
of why.

We've heard from a handful of members already recalibrating fourth-quarter plans around it. A
farmstead producer in the upper Midwest told us she's been watching her component pricing more
closely than usual this month, because a milk check built on cheese solids doesn't move the way it
did last October, even with total production up.

## Why the split matters more than the average

It's tempting to look at an average milk price and call the quarter fine. The average hides the
split. If your cheese moves through a component-heavy pricing formula, softer cheese prices reach
your check faster than they reach a headline number blended with butter's strength. Members selling
primarily fluid or butterfat-forward product are having a noticeably easier fourth quarter than
members selling primarily cheese solids, even inside the same region.

Export data adds a second layer. Overall dairy export volume has held up well this year, but cheese
shipments specifically have been uneven market by market — some destinations up, others down on
tariff-related friction that's been building for over a year. A domestic cheese buyer told us the
uneven export picture is one reason wholesale cheese hasn't firmed the way butter has: more of it
is staying home, chasing the same shelf space.

International reference prices tell a similar story. Recent global dairy trading events have shown
cheddar-style cheese prices easing a few percentage points at a time, not collapsing, but drifting
in a direction that keeps pressure on the domestic number rather than relieving it. None of this
points to a single dramatic event. It's a slow grind, which is arguably harder to plan around than
a sharp move, because there's no obvious moment that tells you the adjustment is over.

## What to do with the split, not just watch it

If you haven't already, pull your own pricing formula apart by component and see how much of your
check rides on cheese solids versus butterfat versus other-solids. Members who've done this exercise
tell us it changes how they think about locking in forward contracts, because a formula that looked
diversified on paper sometimes turns out to be more cheese-weighted than the producer realized.

The Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding questions on this from smaller operations
that don't have a full-time person tracking component pricing month to month; if that's you, reach
out and we'll point you to a member who's built a simple tracking sheet worth borrowing. And if
you're heading into winter make decisions with this split in mind, our *Pricing & Margin for
Specialty Cheese* course walks through exactly this kind of component math before you commit to a
season's schedule.

None of this is a call to panic about the fourth quarter. Milk production is up, which is a healthy
underlying signal even when it puts pressure on any one class price. But a healthy supply picture
and a comfortable cheese margin are two different things, and members who've had the smoothest
close to this year are the ones who stopped assuming the two move together. Check your own
component split before you finalize any pricing decisions for the months ahead, and if the number
surprises you, that's worth a call to the committee rather than a guess.
