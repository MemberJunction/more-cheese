One of our longest-tenured members in a sales role has been with the federation for thirteen years, long enough to have watched several waves of specialty-cheese growth come and go, and he's blunt about what's kept his accounts renewing when a lot of reps' don't: it's rarely the cheese itself. "Buyers can get a good wheel from ten different reps," he told us this month. "What they can't get from ten different reps is someone who calls back when there's a problem, not just when there's a sale to make."

He's a regional sales director covering wholesale and specialty accounts, and he's seen the buyer side of the business shift meaningfully over his tenure. Specialty food sales have kept climbing as a share of the overall grocery case, and trade shows built around exactly this segment have grown busier every year — more retailers, more distributors, more competition for a buyer's attention than existed when he started. His answer to more competition hasn't been to chase more accounts. It's been to keep fewer accounts and know them better.

## What "knowing an account" actually means to him

For him, that means tracking more than order volume. He keeps notes on which of his buyers' own customers ask for a raw-milk option, which stores have cheese cases with humidity problems he's flagged before, and which buyers have burned through a rep's patience in the past and need a different rhythm of contact. "A buyer who got over-called by the last rep doesn't want a phone call every week," he said. "A buyer who got ignored during a shortage does. You have to know which one you're talking to."

That kind of account memory is hard to build quickly, which is part of why he's skeptical of advice aimed at brand-new reps that focuses mostly on pitch technique. "The pitch matters for the first order," he told us. "It's the fourth and fifth order where the relationship actually gets tested — a late shipment, a price increase, a batch that didn't turn out the way you both expected. That's when a buyer decides whether they trust you, not during the demo."

## What he'd tell someone starting out

His advice for newer members in sales roles is unglamorous: write things down, follow up even when there's nothing to report, and treat a buyer's bad week as the moment that actually builds the relationship, not a distraction from it. "The follow-up call after a problem is worth more than ten check-in calls when everything's fine," he said. "Buyers remember who showed up for the hard one." He's also candid that this approach doesn't scale infinitely — past a certain number of accounts, the notes get thinner and the follow-through slips, which is why he's turned down territory expansions that would have grown his book but thinned his attention.

If you're newer to a sales or account-management role at your creamery or distributor and want to compare notes with a member who's been doing this a while, our Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a short list of veteran members who've said they're glad to talk through what's worked for them.
