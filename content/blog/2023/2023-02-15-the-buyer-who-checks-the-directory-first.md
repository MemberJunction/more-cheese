Most of our member spotlights land on someone who makes cheese: a head cheesemaker rethinking a make sheet, an affineur watching a cave's humidity climb. This time we wanted the other side of the counter. One of our longtime members buys specialty cheese for a regional retail chain, deciding which wheels make it into the case and which don't, and she was blunt about how differently membership works when your job is sourcing rather than making.

"I don't taste for a living the way a monger does, at least not in the same volume," she told us. "My job is closer to reading a room six months out — what's going to sell in the fall case, what a distributor is going to be short on, which small producer is about to outgrow their current capacity." That forward-looking angle shapes which parts of membership she actually uses.

## What a buyer gets out of a directory

Ask a cheesemaker what membership is for and you'll usually hear about courses or credentials. Ask her, and the first thing out of her mouth is the member directory. "If I'm trying to find a producer making a specific style, in a specific region, at a scale that can actually supply a case program instead of a single farmers-market table, the directory saves me a genuinely absurd number of cold calls," she said. "I can see who's an active member, which tells me something about whether they're still operating the way they were the last time I checked."

The specialty cheese market has stayed genuinely busy through this stretch — trade coverage this year keeps describing steady demand growth for artisan and territorial styles, with new small producers entering even as a few close, which is exactly the kind of churn that makes a directory more useful than a memory. She described spending part of most weeks just confirming that the producers on her shortlist are still active and still making what she remembers them for, since a case program built around a supplier who quietly stopped renewing is a case program with a hole in it by spring.

## The parts she uses less, and why that's fine

She's attended a couple of federation events but doesn't consider herself a conference regular, and she was straightforward about why: most of the programming is written for people making or selling cheese at a counter, not sourcing it at a distance. She reads the advocacy updates closely, though, especially anything touching interstate shipment rules, since a labeling or transport change that adds friction for a small producer eventually shows up as a delay or a price change on her end. She also keeps an eye on general dairy market coverage, since a rough stretch for milk prices tends to ripple through to what small producers can afford to hold back for aging.

It's a useful reminder that membership doesn't have to look the same for every member type to be worth the dues. A retailer leans on courses and credentials. A distributor leans on the directory and advocacy notes. A buyer like her ends up somewhere in between — using the federation less as a training program and more as infrastructure for knowing who's still out there and what's about to change under them. If you're on the sourcing side of this business and haven't spent much time in the member directory, it's worth a closer look — it's built for exactly the kind of search she described.
