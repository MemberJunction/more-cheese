Every winter, we get a wave of questions from newer members about the sixty-day aging
requirement for raw-milk cheese, usually from someone who just found out a batch they were
planning to sell across state lines has to sit in the cave a little longer than they'd
budgeted for. So this week we're walking through what the rule actually says, and where the
real work happens in your process rather than just on your maturation calendar.

The federal requirement is simple to state: cheese made from unpasteurized milk that crosses
state lines has to be aged at least sixty days at a controlled temperature before it ships.
The logic behind it is that time, salt, and a dropping pH create conditions that are
inhospitable to the pathogens people actually worry about in raw milk. It is not a magic
switch that flips on day sixty — it's a proxy for a set of conditions your process needs to
be hitting well before the clock even starts. State-level permit reviews this fall, several
of them reported in the trade press, were a reminder that ongoing sampling and monitoring
matter as much as the aging clock itself; a permit can still be suspended over a testing
result even on a fully aged product.

## Where the real risk gets managed

If you only think about this as a countdown timer, you're missing most of the actual food
safety work. What regulators and, frankly, what your own liability actually depend on is
what happens upstream: verified milk quality at intake, a make sheet that documents your pH
and moisture targets and shows you hit them, and a cave environment you can prove was
holding steady temperature and humidity for the full sixty days. A logbook with gaps in it
is worth less than the sixty days themselves.

This is exactly the territory covered in *Listeria Control in Small Creameries*, one of our
most-enrolled courses this year, and it pairs well with *Maturation Planning for Small
Rooms* if your aging space is smaller than you'd like it to be. Neither course tells you
whether a specific batch is safe to sell — that's not something a course, or we, can
determine for you — but both walk through how to build a documented, defensible process
around the milestones the rule actually cares about.

## What we'd ask you to double-check

A few things members flag every year, right around now, when the holiday production push
collides with year-end recordkeeping:

- Your temperature logs for the cave, not just your ambient thermostat setting — a
  calibration drift of even a couple of degrees over sixty days is the kind of gap an
  inspector will ask about.
- Whether your supply-chain documentation for incoming milk is current, especially if
  you've changed suppliers this year.
- Whether your labeling on any interstate shipment actually states the age at time of
  sale, not just at time of packaging.
- Whether your own monthly or quarterly sampling schedule, if your state requires one,
  is actually being kept and filed, not just performed.

If any of that raises a question specific to your operation, that's a conversation for your
own food-safety consultant or your state inspector, not for a blog post. But if you want a
structured refresher before your next cohort of wheels comes off the shelf, both courses
