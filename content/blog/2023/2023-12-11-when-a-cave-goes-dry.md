It's been a strong year for artisan cheese generally — a fact we were reminded of again this fall, when an international competition's top honor went to a small producer most of that continent's own cheese buyers hadn't heard of yet. Growth like that has a quieter cost, though, and every December we hear from a handful of members whose caves have gone strange on them. It's almost never the wheels. It's the air. Cold outside air holds less moisture than warm air to begin with, and once a heating or refrigeration system pulls that dry air into an aging room, humidity can drop faster than most make schedules account for. The result shows up as cracked rinds on bloomy and washed styles, a chalky rind on cheddars that used to be smooth, and wheels quietly losing more weight to evaporation than anyone budgeted for.

Most hard cheeses want somewhere around 80 to 85 percent relative humidity while aging, and bloomy, washed-rind, and blue styles usually want more — often 90 to 95 percent. Those numbers don't change in winter. What changes is how hard your room has to work to hold them, and how much faster things go wrong if nobody's checking.

## What actually breaks first

Rind cracking is the visible symptom, but it's a lagging one. By the time a rind shows a hairline crack, the cheese underneath has usually already lost more moisture than you'd like, and a cracked rind is also an easier route in for the wrong mold entirely. Weight loss is the number worth tracking daily if you can manage it, because it moves before the rind does. A wheel that's dropping weight faster than its normal curve is telling you the room is drier than it should be, whether or not anything looks wrong yet.

The fix, once you've caught it, is usually simpler than the diagnosis. A shallow tray of salt water near the racks can lift ambient humidity meaningfully, and it's a common low-cost first step members reach for before investing in equipment. A small humidifier gets you further, faster, if the room is large or the problem severe. What doesn't work is guessing. A hygrometer that actually gets read every day is the difference between catching a dry spell in its first week and finding it after a whole rack has cracked.

Airflow matters as much as the humidity number itself. A room that's humid on average but has one cold corner near a door or a vent will still crack rinds in that corner while the rest of the rack looks fine, which is why members who've been doing this a while check several points in a room rather than trusting a single hygrometer mounted wherever it was easiest to run power.
