Cheese counters do most of their skill-building around everyday sales — the two-ounce sample, the wedge cut to order, the answer to "what pairs with a Malbec." Gift-box season asks for something different, and it is the part of the calendar where we get the most calls about waste, cracked rinds, and boxes that looked fine in the case and arrived at the customer's door in worse shape.

The core issue is that a gift wedge has to survive time and handling that a counter sale never does. It sits wrapped for days, gets shipped or carried, and often sits at room temperature for a stretch before anyone eats it. A cut that would be perfectly fine for someone walking straight home can dry out, sweat, or crack a rind within 48 hours if it was portioned the same way you would portion for the lunch rush. This year's flood of gift guides and gift-box roundups makes the point without meaning to: nearly every list singles out presentation, and presentation is a lot harder to hold onto three days after cutting than three minutes after cutting.

## What actually changes

A few adjustments matter more than people expect. First, cut thickness: a gift wedge generally wants a slightly heavier proportion of rind-to-paste than a same-size counter wedge, because rind is what protects the interior during transit — cutting it too thin for a "cleaner-looking" wedge leaves the paste exposed sooner. Second, wrap choice: wax paper works fine for same-day counter sales, but a wheel that will travel benefits from a vacuum or semi-permeable wrap suited to its moisture content, and bloomy-rind styles need breathing room a hard-paste wheel does not. Third, timing: cutting a gift order the morning it ships, rather than batching cuts a few days ahead to save time during a busy week, measurably reduces the complaints we hear about dried-out edges.

There is also a labeling piece that gets overlooked. A wedge headed straight into a customer's hand at the counter needs less on it than one going into a box that might sit under a tree for a week — a make date, a suggested use-by window, and a one-line storage note save you a return call in January from someone who forgot how long ago they bought it.

Our Cut & Wrap Fundamentals course covers all three of the cutting and wrapping adjustments directly, with hands-on practice cutting for different transit windows rather than for immediate sale. If your counter has added or trained new staff for the holiday rush, this is the course to send them to before the busiest two weeks hit, not after the first round of complaints comes in. It runs in a compact format specifically so it can fit into a December schedule, and past cohorts have found the transit-simulation exercise — cutting a wedge, wrapping it, and checking it again after 48 hours on a counter — the single most useful hour of the course.

## A smaller thing worth doing

If you have not already, walk your case today and separate your gift-box inventory from your daily-sale inventory, even if they are the same cheeses. It sounds obvious once someone says it, but a counter that treats every wedge the same way tends to lose track of which cuts have been sitting the longest, and that is exactly the batch that ends up in a holiday box.
