A member wrote to our Education Committee this month with an observation she said had been bothering her for a year before she finally did anything about it: her make sheet listed every step of her cheddar process in order, but it had never told her how long any of it actually took. She'd inherited the sheet from an earlier version of her own operation, updated the ingredients and quantities as her recipe evolved, and never once gone back to check whether the timing notes still matched reality.

So this winter, during a slower stretch, she ran three straight make days with a stopwatch and a notebook instead of just her usual rhythm. What she found surprised her: cutting the curd was taking nearly twice as long as her sheet implied, not because anything was wrong with her technique, but because her vat size had changed since the sheet was last accurate and she'd simply absorbed the extra time without noticing. Meanwhile a step she'd always budgeted a generous window for — draining before milling — was consistently finishing early, and she'd been building slack into her day around a delay that didn't actually exist anymore.

## Why this kind of drift is so easy to miss

A make sheet earns trust the same way any tool does: by working, most of the time, without complaint. That's exactly what makes quiet drift dangerous. Nothing about her cheese was failing. Her wheels were fine. But her actual production day and the day her scheduling was built around had slowly pulled apart from each other, and she only found out because she happened to check with a stopwatch instead of trusting the sheet the way she had for years.

It's a useful reminder for any small operation running lean: the tools that structure your day deserve an occasional audit even when nothing's visibly broken, because the failure mode isn't a bad batch — it's a schedule that's technically working but quietly wasting hours you didn't know you had, or worse, one that's underestimating a step until the day it finally causes a problem.

## What changed once she knew

She rebuilt her make sheet around the actual timed steps rather than the inherited ones, and picked up close to forty minutes in her production day that she'd been unconsciously padding into a step that no longer needed it. That's not a dramatic number on its own, but across a five-day production week it was enough to move a second make day for a smaller-batch cheese from "something I keep meaning to try" to something she actually scheduled this spring.

She's since told a few other members at a regional meetup that she'd recommend the same exercise to anyone whose make sheet predates a change in equipment, recipe, or batch size — which, if you think about it, describes almost every working cheesemaker's sheet eventually. Our Education Committee has folded her approach into how they talk about our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course, since a schedule that's quietly wrong tends to show up as a margin problem before anyone traces it back to timing.

**Next step:** the next time you run a make day, time one step you haven't checked in a while against what your sheet says it should take. You might be surprised which direction the gap runs.
