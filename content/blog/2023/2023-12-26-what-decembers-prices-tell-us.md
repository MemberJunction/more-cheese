The federal price reports that came out this month landed softer than November's. Forty-pound cheese blocks slipped, butter slipped further, and Class III and Class IV milk both came in below where they sat a year ago. None of that is catastrophic on its own, but it caps off a year in which the federal dairy margin safety net paid out in most months, including a stretch over the summer when the margin dropped low enough to trigger the program's catastrophic tier for the first time since it existed under any name. If your creamery buys milk rather than milks its own herd, you felt that further up the chain than the block price suggests.

We're not a trading desk, and this space isn't the place for predictions dressed up as numbers. But a few things members have been asking us about are worth laying out plainly.

## What actually moved, and what it means for you

Cheese and butter both came off their highs, and total cheese production for the year finished modestly ahead of the year before, with plenty of milk available to keep plants running steady through the holidays. That's a market that kept building supply rather than tightening it. For members who sell direct to consumers or through a specialty counter, that's mostly background noise — your case doesn't compete with commodity block cheese. For members who sell into a co-op, a wholesale contract, or anywhere the block price sets the floor, it's the number that actually shows up on the settlement sheet.

Early outlook work for 2024 has feed costs projected to ease and the milk-to-feed margin projected to sit meaningfully above where it spent parts of 2023. That's a more comfortable starting point than the one 2023 opened with. It is not a guarantee, and margin coverage exists precisely because outlooks miss.

If you're a producer whose milk price is set by a formula you don't control, the question for January is whether your feed contracts and your coverage elections match what you now expect the margin to do, not what it did last spring. Enrollment windows for the federal margin program open early in the year; if you've never walked through the coverage-level math with someone who isn't trying to sell you insurance, our Membership & Outreach Committee can point you to members who've done it and are willing to talk through their own numbers.

If you're on the finished-cheese side — a creamery selling wheels, a shop selling wedges — the milk price is an input cost, not your top line, and the more useful year-end exercise is usually a simple one: pull your 2023 cost per pound by product line and see whether your prices actually moved with it over the year, or whether a slow board update let margin erode a little at a time. Several members mentioned exactly that drift when we asked around for this post.

Softer prices at year-end, a margin program that did its job for a lot of 2023, and an outlook that's cautiously better for the year ahead. Whichever side of the milk price you sit on, the useful move this week isn't a prediction — it's checking your own numbers against what actually happened, before you build next year's budget on what you assumed would happen instead.
