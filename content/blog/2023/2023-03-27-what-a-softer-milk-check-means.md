Federal projections this year have Class III milk — the classification cheese is made from —
running several dollars per hundredweight below where it sat through most of last year. For a
member buying milk on contract, that shows up as relief. For a member who also sells finished
wheels, it raises a question we've been fielding all month: if your input cost is easing, does
your price list have to move too?

The honest answer is no, not automatically, and we want to walk through why.

## Feed costs didn't fall with milk prices

The federal forecast is a milk-price number, not a farm-economics number. Feed costs — corn,
hay, supplemental protein — stayed elevated through the winter, which means the spread between
what a dairy earns per hundredweight and what it costs to produce that milk narrowed rather than
widened for a lot of producers. If you buy milk from a single farm or a small co-op, ask what
their feed bill looks like before you assume a softer milk market means a softer bill for you.
Several members who priced this year's supply contracts back in January told us their milk cost
barely moved even as the futures market did.

That's the distinction that gets lost when "milk prices are down" turns into a headline: down
relative to what, and down for whom. A processor buying spot milk on the open market feels a
price swing almost immediately. A creamery on a fixed annual contract may not feel it until
renewal. Trade coverage this spring is also flagging tighter producer margins generally, even as
the milk-price number itself eases, which is a reminder that a softer input price doesn't
automatically mean a softer year everywhere in the supply chain.

## What this means for your wholesale sheet, and where to enroll the difference

If your cost base genuinely eased this spring, a wholesale price cut isn't required, and we'd
gently discourage a reflexive one. Buyers remember a lower number more easily than they remember
why it went back up next year when feed costs catch back up to milk prices, which most of our
members expect will happen before the year is out. A steadier approach — holding price and
quietly protecting margin, or passing along a modest, clearly explained adjustment tied to a real
cost change — tends to hold up better with retail and wholesale accounts than a price that moves
with every futures report.

If you enrolled in a margin coverage program for this production year, this is also a good month
to pull your enrollment paperwork back out. The coverage is priced against the same margin — milk
price minus feed cost — that's driving this whole conversation, and knowing your triggered
coverage level tells you more about your actual exposure this year than any single price
headline will. That's also the kind of question the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course
is built around: reading a milk-price forecast against your own cost structure rather than
reacting to the number on its own. Members who took a version of it last year told us it changed
how they talk to buyers about price, not just how they calculate it.
