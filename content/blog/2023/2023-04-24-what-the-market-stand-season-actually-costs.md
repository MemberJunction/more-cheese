Market season is opening this month across most of our footprint, and for a lot of our smaller members that means the first real test of the year isn't a make decision, it's a stand decision. Demand for local food has kept climbing, which is good news, but a folding table under a tent hides more real costs than most first-timers expect, and we hear from a new stand every spring who priced their wedges off the wholesale sheet and wondered by June why the till never quite matched the cheese that left the table.

A wholesale price and a market price are not the same number, and treating them as close cousins is the single most common mistake we see. Your wholesale price already assumes someone else handles the tasting, the conversation, and the return trip if a customer didn't love what they bought. At a market, you're doing all three yourself, which is real labor, and it belongs in the price. So does the sample loss — cut wedges you're giving away one bite at a time all afternoon — and the shrink on anything that doesn't sell and doesn't keep well enough for the following week.

## The course built for exactly this gap

Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets is our course for members making this jump, and it doesn't spend much time on cheesemaking at all. It's about the stand itself: how to build a case that reads well from six feet away, how to price for sample loss without guessing, and how to have a thirty-second conversation with someone who's never had a washed-rind cheese before without either losing them or the customer behind them. Members who've taken it tell us the biggest shift isn't tactical, it's that they stop treating the market as a smaller version of a wholesale account and start treating it as its own channel with its own math.

That math matters more this year than it did a few seasons ago. One community market network reported its farmers markets crossed a new sales record for the year, up several hundred thousand dollars over the year before, with cheese and other specialty foods now a meaningful share of the total alongside produce. That kind of growth is genuinely good for members willing to show up consistently. But a strong season for the market overall doesn't automatically mean a strong season for your particular stand — the producers who do best are usually the ones who went in with a real price built for the channel, not last year's wholesale sheet with a markup guessed on top.

## Before your first Saturday

If you haven't priced your market case yet this year, do that math before you set up, not after your third weekend of wondering where the margin went. Build in labor, sample loss, and realistic shrink, and check it against what similar stands in your region are actually charging — the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with members running comparable stands who are usually happy to compare notes. And if this is your first season selling direct rather than wholesale, the course is worth the couple of hours before you're standing behind the table figuring it out live.
