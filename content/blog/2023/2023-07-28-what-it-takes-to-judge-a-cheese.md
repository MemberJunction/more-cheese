Every year after competition wraps at the annual conference, we hear from a handful of members who watched the judging happen and thought: I could do that. Some of them can, with the right preparation, and this week seemed like the right time to walk through what Competition Judge Accreditation actually takes, since it's freshest in people's minds right after they've watched a panel work.

The short version is that it isn't a credential you can pick up on its own. Accreditation requires holding the Sensory Evaluation Certificate first, because judging depends on the same vocabulary that certificate builds - the ability to name a flaw precisely, describe a texture consistently, and score against a standard rather than a personal preference. On top of that certificate, accreditation requires supervised panel experience: time spent judging alongside an already-accredited judge, on a real panel, before you're signed off to score entries on your own.

## Why it's not a one-and-done credential

One detail that surprises members is that this accreditation lapses quickly without active judging. It isn't like some of our other credentials, where you renew every few years with continuing education and otherwise carry on. If you go too long between judging assignments, you'll need to requalify rather than simply pay a renewal fee. The reasoning is straightforward once you think about it: judging consistency is a skill that erodes fast if it isn't practiced, and a judge who hasn't scored anything in two years is judging on memory, not current calibration.

That's also why the federation tries to route newly accredited judges onto smaller regional competitions between conferences, rather than saving everyone's first real assignment for the flagship event. A member who judges a modest local competition twice a year stays sharp in a way that someone who only shows up once annually can't.

## Two roles, one score

If you watched judging happen this week, you may have noticed pairs working together rather than individuals scoring alone. That's the standard structure: one judge scores from the top down, deducting points for flaws and defects, while the other scores from the bottom up, awarding points for what the cheese does particularly well. The two scores are combined, and the cheese is judged against the standard for its category rather than against whatever else happens to be on the table that day. It's a useful mental model even if you never plan to judge yourself - it's roughly the same framework worth using when you're deciding what to enter next year.

If judging is something you've been considering, the place to start is the Sensory Evaluation Certificate track, not a direct application to the accreditation itself - contact the Awards & Competition Committee if you'd like to know when the next supervised panel opportunity is coming up.

It's worth saying plainly: judging at scale is a bigger operation than most members picture. Other national cheese competitions held this summer have reported panels of judges scoring well over a thousand entries across more than a hundred categories, all inside a compressed few days of work. Our federation's competition runs at a fraction of that scale, but the underlying discipline is identical - every judge on every panel is applying the same standard to every entry, regardless of how many wheels are left in the queue behind it.

That consistency is the entire point of requiring supervised panel time before accreditation. A judge who has only ever scored cheese on their own, without a second set of eyes checking their calibration against another accredited judge, can develop habits that feel confident and still drift from the standard over time. Pairing new judges with experienced ones - and pairing them again periodically once they're accredited - is how the federation keeps scores comparable from one competition to the next, even as the specific people on any given panel change year to year.

If you entered this year and are waiting on your scoresheet, it should reach you within a few weeks of the closing dinner, with comments from both your technical and aesthetic judge attached. Read it as a specific, usable note about this year's entry, not a verdict on your cheesemaking as a whole - even members who've placed well before have told us their most useful scoresheets were the ones where they didn't place at all.
