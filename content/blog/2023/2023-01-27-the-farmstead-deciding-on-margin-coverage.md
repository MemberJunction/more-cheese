A farmstead member in our Membership & Outreach network — she asked us not to use her creamery's name, just her situation — spent most of last week with a spreadsheet open next to her make schedule, trying to decide whether to enroll in the federal dairy margin-support program before this month's window closes on January 31. It's not a glamorous kind of member story. There's no ribbon, no competition medal. But it's the story most likely to be happening at a kitchen table near you right now.

The program pays out when the gap between milk prices and feed costs falls below a level the producer picks in advance. For a farmstead cheesemaker who milks her own herd, that gap is the whole business model in one number, and this year the feed side of it has been the harder one to predict: analysts following the program into 2023 have flagged that forecast feed costs were running near their highest point of the year in January, even as milk prices softened from where they sat twelve months earlier. That combination — cheaper milk, pricier feed — is exactly the squeeze the coverage program exists to cushion, and it's why enrollment interest has been higher than usual this cycle.

## Why we're telling you this now, not after

We don't give financial advice, and this isn't a recommendation for any coverage level — that call depends on your herd size, your feed contracts, and a risk tolerance nobody else can set for you. What we can tell you is the deadline: enrollment for this program year closes January 31, and it doesn't reopen until next year's window. If you've been meaning to run your own numbers and haven't yet, this week is the week, not next week.

She told us she ran three scenarios — no coverage, a middle tier, and the highest tier available for her herd size — before settling on the middle option, mostly because the premium for the top tier ate too far into what it was supposed to protect. Industry coverage of the program this winter has made a similar point at a bigger scale: the backstop helps most for producers who pick a coverage level that actually matches their real feed exposure, not the level that sounds the safest on paper. Overinsuring against a margin collapse that doesn't happen just turns the premium into a sunk cost; underinsuring leaves you exposed in exactly the month you needed the payout.

She also told us something that stuck with us more than the spreadsheet did: the hardest part wasn't the math, it was admitting that a program built for "farms" was actually built with her operation in mind, small as it is. A lot of our farmstead members carry a version of that same hesitation — the sense that risk-management tools are for someone else's scale of business. They aren't. The margin between what your milk costs to produce and what your cheese sells for doesn't care how many wheels you make a week, and the enrollment window doesn't care how small your herd is either.

## If you're still deciding

Talk to whoever handles your feed contracts before you pick a coverage tier — the number you need is the feed cost side, not just the milk price side, and guessing at it defeats the purpose. If you want a second set of eyes on the scenario math, several long-time farmstead members have offered to compare notes in the days before the deadline; ask in the farmstead producers group and someone will likely answer by evening. Either way, don't let January 31 pass on the assumption that a program built for bigger operations doesn't have room for yours.
