If your distributor called this month asking whether you'd hold a price instead of raising one, you're not imagining a trend. Wholesale cheddar has come down hard since March, and the softness has spread into whey and a handful of other commodity lines. For members selling mostly direct, it barely registers. For members with even a slice of business tied to a formula price, it's the first real test this year.

The short version: milk supply has been running ahead of expectations in several states this spring, and that extra volume has to go somewhere. Cheese and whey prices are usually the first place it shows up, because plants can shift output faster than farms can shift herds. None of that means your own milk check or your own case price should move in lockstep — most of our members aren't selling into that commodity market at all — but it does explain why a buyer might be pushing back on a renewal this week when they weren't in March.

## What's actually different this spring

Two things are worth separating. First, domestic supply: production is up in several dairy states this spring, and that's a normal seasonal story that plays out most years. Second, exports: cheese moving overseas has been running behind last year's pace for several months now, with heavier competition from European producers who've had more milk of their own to work with, and softer demand from a couple of major overseas buyers who are watching their own budgets. That second piece matters more for members who've built any part of their business around wholesale or export accounts, because it's not a one-month blip — it's a slower undercurrent that a single strong production month won't fix on its own.

Milk output across the broader European supply base has also been running a little lighter than originally forecast this year, which sounds like it should tighten things up, but the timing hasn't lined up with the softer demand side yet. That's the frustrating part of a market like this one: two forces pulling in opposite directions rarely cancel out cleanly, and in the meantime a buyer negotiating with you this month is looking at whichever number moved most recently, not the average of the two.

If you sell mostly at a farmers' market or through a retail case, the practical takeaway is smaller: your cost inputs — rennet, packaging, energy, the propane bill for your cave's climate control — haven't moved with the commodity market, so there's no reason to chase a price down just because a headline number did. If you do have wholesale exposure, this is a good week to actually read your contract's pricing formula rather than assume it tracks the number you saw in a newsletter. A formula tied to a national average lags reality by weeks in either direction, and a lot of members find out how their own contract is worded for the first time during a swing like this one.
