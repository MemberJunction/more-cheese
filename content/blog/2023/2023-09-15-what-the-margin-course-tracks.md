Every time milk prices move fast — up or down — enrollment in Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese ticks up. This has been one of those months: a milk price recovering off last year's low, feed costs that have not eased at the same pace, and members asking us, in different words, the same question. Is what I charge for a wheel still covering what it actually costs me to make it?

The course does not answer that question for you. What it gives you is a way to ask it correctly, because a surprising number of pricing mistakes we see in member calls come from comparing the wrong two numbers.

## The three numbers most makers aren't tracking together

The first module is about cost per pound of finished cheese, not cost per gallon of milk. Those two numbers feel related but move differently, because yield, moisture loss during aging, and trim all sit between them. A make-sheet that only tracks milk cost in and finished weight out — without accounting for how much of that weight you lose to a long affinage — will understate your real cost on anything you age past a few weeks.

The second module is about labor as a real input, not an afterthought. A lot of small operations price as if their own time is free, which works until the operation grows enough that it isn't — at which point the pricing has to change all at once instead of gradually, and that is a hard conversation to have with retail accounts used to a stable number.

The third, and the one that generated the most questions this cohort, is about how to separate a genuine cost increase from a temporary price swing before you change what you charge. Raising a price in response to a milk-cost spike that reverses in six weeks can cost you a retail account that does not want to see your number move twice in a season. The course walks through a simple test: is the cost change tied to something structural — a new feed contract, a wage increase, a packaging switch — or is it riding a commodity price that has moved before and could move back? Structural changes justify a price change. Commodity swings usually justify a temporary surcharge or just absorbing it, if your margin allows.

## Who tends to get the most out of it

We see three kinds of members sign up. Makers who have never formally priced anything and are doing it by feel. Makers who priced carefully three or four years ago and have not revisited it since, even though their cost structure has changed. And makers who sell both wholesale and direct and need two different pricing logics that still reconcile to the same underlying cost.

If any of that sounds like your creamery this month, the next Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese cohort is forming now. It runs alongside your regular schedule, not instead of it, and past participants tell us the biggest value is less the spreadsheet template and more the habit of checking these three numbers on a set schedule instead of only when a price swing makes you nervous.
