Most apprentices spend their second year getting faster and more consistent at the vat — reading curd by feel, tightening up their make sheet, learning what a slow set actually looks like instead of just reading it in a book. One of our members' apprentices, at a small farmstead creamery in the upper Midwest, has been doing all of that too, but this spring she also started pulling the weekly wholesale cheddar print and keeping her own log of it next to her cave notes.

It started, her head cheesemaker told us, because she asked why the creamery had held off on a planned equipment purchase this month. The honest answer was the market: wholesale cheese prices have been under real pressure this year, with a soft spring and a weaker global trade picture pulling international dairy values well off their 2022 highs. A small operation selling any volume into that channel feels a swing like that directly in its cash flow. Rather than leave it there, she asked to see the numbers, then kept asking every week after.

## Craft and commerce, same notebook

"She used to think pricing was somebody else's job," her cheesemaker told us. "Now she asks about it before I bring it up." That's not a small shift for someone still building her hands-on skills — we know cheesemaking apprenticeships are built around the vat and the cave for good reason, and a second-year apprentice absolutely should be spending most of her attention there. But an apprentice who also understands why a soft market might mean holding off on a new mold press, or why a strong month might loosen up room for a piece of equipment, ends up a more useful partner to the person running the business a few years sooner than one who doesn't.

Her cheesemaker isn't turning it into a formal training track, at least not yet — mostly it's a standing five minutes at the end of their Friday check-in, cheddar print pulled up next to the cave log. But she pointed to it as the kind of thing more experienced makers underestimate teaching early, and we think she has a point. Reading a market doesn't take the place of learning to read a rind or a moisture reading. It just means fewer surprises later about why a purchase got delayed, or why a price held steady when it might have moved, or why a supplier suddenly wants a longer contract.

We hear a version of this from a lot of small creameries: the person doing the make and the person doing the books are often the same one or two people, and there's rarely a natural moment to teach a junior team member the second half of that job. A five-minute weekly habit costs almost nothing and tends to pay off well past the specific numbers involved.

If you supervise an apprentice or a junior team member, this is worth trying even if you don't formalize it — pull up a price you're already tracking anyway, and talk through what it would mean for a decision you're making that week. And if you'd rather hand it a structure than build one from scratch, our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course covers similar ground more formally, aimed at exactly the kind of small operation where the apprentice and the future manager are often the same person.
