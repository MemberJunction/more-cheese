A question that comes up often enough in our Education Committee's inbox that we want to answer it in public: how do you get two people on the same production team, or a counter staff and a back-room maker, to actually mean the same thing when they say a wheel tastes "sharp" or "barnyardy" or "off"? The honest answer is that most operations never do this deliberately, and it costs them more than they realize — a quality problem gets missed, or debated for twenty minutes, because two tasters don't share a vocabulary for what they're noticing.

This is exactly the gap a structured sensory vocabulary closes, and it doesn't require a lab or a certification to start building one internally, though our Sensory Foundations course is a solid way to formalize it once you're ready.

## Start with categories before descriptors

The most common mistake teams make is jumping straight to descriptive words — fruity, grassy, nutty — without first agreeing on the broader categories those words belong to: aroma, taste, texture, and finish are the standard buckets, and separating them matters. "Sharp" could describe a taste (acidity), an aroma (pungency), or a texture sensation (a bite on the tongue), and if two tasters use the word for different categories, they'll talk past each other every time, even when they're both right about what they're perceiving.

Once your team agrees on categories, build a shared word list specific to your own cheeses rather than borrowing a generic wheel from a textbook. A washed-rind maker and a fresh chèvre maker need almost entirely different vocabularies, and forcing a one-size-fits-all list onto both wastes the exercise.

## Make it a habit, not a workshop

A single training session rarely sticks. What works better, based on what members tell our committee, is a short recurring tasting — five or ten minutes, same time each week, same small set of wheels — where everyone on the team calls out what they notice before comparing notes. The point isn't to reach agreement on every wheel. It's to notice where your team's descriptions diverge, because that divergence is exactly where a quality issue can hide undetected.

Over a few months, most teams find their vocabulary narrows and sharpens on its own — not because anyone lectured about the "right" words, but because repetition builds a shared reference point. That reference point is what lets a counter clerk flag something to a maker with real specificity instead of a vague "something's off with batch 14," and it's what lets a maker trust that flag enough to investigate immediately.

A useful trick borrowed from formal sensory panels: keep a short written reference list next to your tasting spot, pairing each descriptor your team uses with a concrete example. "Barnyardy" means something specific and repeatable, not a vague catch-all for "something's a little strong today." Writing the definitions down, even informally, does more to align a team than any amount of verbal discussion, because it forces everyone to agree on the boundary of a word rather than assume they already do.

It's also worth separating a flaw from a house characteristic early on. A washed rind is supposed to carry more pungency than a bloomy rind; a natural-rind aged cheddar is supposed to carry more depth than a young one. A shared vocabulary should help your team distinguish "this is stronger than usual for this style" from "this style is supposed to be strong" — conflating the two is one of the more common ways a good batch gets second-guessed for no reason.

**Next step:** if your team doesn't already run a short recurring tasting, start one this month with three or four wheels you make regularly, and see where your team's descriptions land in different places. Sensory Foundations is the next step if you want to build that habit out further with a structured curriculum behind it.
