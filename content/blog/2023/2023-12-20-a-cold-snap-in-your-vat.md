We've spent a fair amount of this month talking about what cold weather does to an aging room, and rightly so — but the room isn't the only part of a make that a hard freeze touches. Milk that arrives colder than usual, a make room that runs a few degrees below its normal range, or a starter culture that's been sitting in a chilly delivery truck longer than expected can all shift how a batch behaves before a wheel ever sees the cave. A handful of members have written in this week wondering why a make that's been consistent for months suddenly set slower, or why a curd felt different at cut than it has all fall. Winter is usually the answer, and it usually isn't the cave.

Starter cultures are temperature-sensitive by design — that's the whole point of them — and most mesophilic and thermophilic cultures have a fairly narrow window where they perform the way a make sheet assumes they will. Milk that comes in a few degrees colder than usual, whether because of a chilly delivery truck or a make room that's harder to hold at temperature once the building itself is fighting the cold, asks your culture to wake up and get to work from a colder starting point than it's used to. The result is often a slower set, not a failed one, which is part of why it's easy to miss: everything still works, it just takes longer, and if you're timing a make by the clock instead of by what the curd is actually doing, that gap compounds.

## Where the drift actually shows up

The most common report we hear is a set that runs behind schedule by twenty or thirty minutes without an obvious cause. The second most common is a curd that cuts a little softer or a little firmer than the maker expects, because the culture's activity curve shifted even though the recipe on paper didn't change at all. "I checked my thermometer three times because I didn't believe it," one member told us this week about a batch that set nearly forty minutes behind schedule. Neither of these is a sign anything is wrong with your culture stock. It's usually a sign that the temperature it's actually working at is a few degrees off from where it was in October, and the make sheet hasn't caught up yet.

The fix isn't complicated, but it does take a maker willing to trust a thermometer over a clock. If your milk is arriving colder, give it time to come up to temperature fully before you inoculate, rather than pitching culture into milk that's still a few degrees short and hoping the timeline holds anyway. If your make room itself runs cold in winter, a simple space heater or an insulated blanket over the vat during the ripening step can hold temperature more evenly than most makers expect. And if you're tracking set time by the clock rather than by a flocculation test or a firmness check, winter is exactly the season where that habit starts producing inconsistent wheels for reasons that have nothing to do with your skill.
