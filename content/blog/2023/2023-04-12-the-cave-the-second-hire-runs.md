Every spring we hear some version of the same story from members who run small farmstead operations: the founder can't be in the cave and the make room and the farmers' market booth all at once, so somewhere around year three or four they hire a second person, and that person quietly becomes indispensable. This week's spotlight isn't one member — it's a composite drawn from several conversations we've had this quarter with exactly that person, because the pattern is common enough in our directory that it's worth naming rather than attaching to any one member's story.

She usually starts on packing and labeling, the parts of the operation that look the most like a normal job. Within a year she's asking questions the founder stopped asking a decade ago — why does this wheel's rind color differ from the one next to it, why did the humidity dip overnight, what's actually in the wash water. That curiosity is the tell. By year two she's running her own rotation of turns and washes, and the founder has quietly stopped checking her work.

## What actually changes

The shift isn't about titles. Plenty of these members never get an official promotion; they just start being the one the founder calls from the road when a delivery is late or a batch smells off. What changes is recordkeeping. A cave that used to run on one person's memory starts running on a shared log — turn schedule, humidity readings, which wheels came from which vat — because two people can't both hold it in their heads. That log ends up being the single most useful document in the business the day anyone has to explain a batch to a food-safety inspector or a buyer.

We hear the apprentice side of this story almost as often. Small creameries that bring on a second or third set of hands tend to describe the arrangement less like a job posting and more like an apprenticeship, with a season or a year attached and a fairly steep learning curve packed into it. The people who stick past that first season are the ones who start treating the cave log as their own, not just a task someone assigned them. That's usually the moment a founder starts describing them as "the person who runs the cave" rather than "my second hire," even if payroll still calls it the same job.

There's a quieter cost to this pattern that we don't talk about enough. A one- or two-person operation that depends entirely on this second hire's judgment has a single point of failure it didn't have when the founder was the only one making decisions. It's not a reason to avoid growing the team — it's a reason to write things down sooner rather than later, so the knowledge lives in a log and not only in one person's head.

## Where we'd point this member

We'd point this member, and anyone in the same spot, toward two things. First, Cave Management is built for exactly this transition — going from "I know this cave" to "here's how I'd teach someone else to know this cave." Second, if aging and affinage decisions are becoming a real part of the job rather than a side task, the Advanced Affinage Certificate is worth a look; it's built around the judgment calls that don't show up in a textbook, the kind this member has probably already made a hundred times without a name for them.

If this sounds like you, or like the person running your own cave, we'd genuinely like to hear from you for a future spotlight — not a composite next time, your actual story. The Membership & Outreach Committee can point you to how that works, and we'd rather hear it straight from the cave than reconstruct it from a handful of similar conversations.
