More members have been asking us about interstate shipment this year than in any recent year we can remember, which tracks with how many small creameries have started selling online rather than just at the counter or the farmers' market. So this week's advocacy update is less about a new rule and more about clearing up an old one that keeps tripping people up: raw milk itself cannot legally move across a state line for sale, but raw-milk cheese can, provided it has been aged at least sixty days. Those two facts sit right next to each other in federal rule and get confused constantly, including by members who've been making cheese for years.

## Why the aging period is the whole hinge

The distinction exists because the federal food-safety regulator treats aging as a control measure, not a formality. A minimum of sixty days at cheese-making temperatures and pH levels is understood to reduce pathogen loads meaningfully, even though the scientific literature on exactly how well it works — and against which specific pathogens — has been debated for years and isn't fully settled. That's part of why the interstate milk shippers list the regulator maintains gets revisited regularly, and why a joint conference of state and federal dairy regulators met again this spring to work through dozens of proposed updates to the rules that govern grade-A milk shipment. Raw-milk cheese aging wasn't the headline item on that agenda, but it's exactly the kind of standing rule that gets touched during those periodic reviews rather than announced on its own.

For now, sixty days aged and clearly labeled as made from unpasteurized milk is what lets your cheese travel. That labeling requirement is not optional and not a place to be casual — a shipment that's aged properly but mislabeled can still get flagged, and a producer who's shipped for years told us recently that a labeling slip has cost her a delayed shipment more than once, more than an actual product problem ever has.

## What this means if you ship, or want to

If you're a small producer who's started fielding online orders from other states, treat your aging documentation the way you'd treat any other food-safety record: dated, consistent, and easy to hand over if a shipment gets questioned. Track the exact date milk went into the vat and the date a wheel is cleared to ship, and don't round in your own favor. A regulator reviewing a shipment isn't looking for perfection so much as a clear, consistent paper trail that matches what's on the label. If you're newer to raw-milk cheese specifically and haven't built this kind of record-keeping habit yet, our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate covers documentation practices that hold up under scrutiny, not just the theory behind why aging matters.

Members who want to weigh in on where this rule goes next have a real channel to do it through: every time the federal regulator or a joint state-federal body opens a comment period touching raw-milk aging or interstate shipment, our Membership & Outreach Committee circulates it to members and helps translate the regulatory language into something you can actually respond to in plain terms. Grassroots comments from people who actually run a cave, not just trade-association boilerplate, are what tends to get noticed in that process. If you've got an aging or labeling story worth telling a regulator, the committee wants to hear about it before the next docket opens, not after it closes.

Either way, the sixty-day rule isn't going anywhere this summer, and the businesses that treat it as a fixed part of their shipping process — rather than a rule to work around — are the ones we hear the fewest border-hold stories from.
