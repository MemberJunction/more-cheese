If you sell across the northern border, you've probably already heard the short version: a formal dispute settlement panel was established this month over how tariff-rate quotas for cheese and other dairy imports are allocated under the continental trade agreement that governs North American dairy. Consultations between the two governments were held in mid-January and didn't resolve the underlying disagreement, which is why the case moved to a panel instead of staying a conversation. The long version is the part that actually affects your case.

Tariff-rate quotas sound like background noise until you're the exporter who finds out, in March, that the allotment your buyer was counting on went to someone else. According to trade advisories tracking the case, this marks the second such panel request in roughly a year, since a prior ruling on the same allocation question didn't resolve the dispute. The complaint at the center of this dispute is that quota shares have been allocated using a market-share method that critics say favors a small number of large processors and retailers, while imposing conditions that make it harder for smaller specialty importers and distributors to use the allocations they do receive. For a mid-sized creamery trying to build a cross-border relationship with a single buyer, that kind of structural tilt matters more than any single year's price.

## Why this is a members issue, not a spectator sport

We've fielded calls this week from exporting members who want to know whether the outcome of a trade dispute most of them will never read in full is worth their attention. It is. This is the second time in roughly a year that this same quota allocation question has gone to a formal panel, which tells you two things: the underlying dispute hasn't actually been settled by the first ruling, and the allocation formula that eventually sticks could shape market access for the next several seasons, not just the next one.

The federation doesn't lobby on your behalf in the way a national trade office does — we don't have standing in a government-to-government dispute — but we do collect and submit member testimony when a comment period opens, and we track deadlines so you don't have to. If your business ships cheese across that border, or has considered it, this is the moment to tell us what a fair allocation would actually look like from where you sit: by volume, by years in the market, by product category. Specifics travel better than general complaints, and a panel process moves slowly enough that there's still time for member input to matter.

## What to do this week

Two concrete steps. First, if export volume is any part of your business plan for the next two years, send the Membership & Outreach Committee a short note — three sentences is plenty — describing how a quota reallocation would hit you. We're compiling those into a single submission rather than asking each member to navigate the comment process alone. Second, if you've already filed a petition signature or letter on interstate or cross-border shipment issues through the federation before, you know the format; this is the same kind of action, aimed at a different desk and a slower clock.

We'll follow up once a comment window is formally announced and once the panel's timeline firms up. In the meantime, the cave doesn't care about tariff schedules, but your export ledger does — worth ten minutes this week to make sure someone's listening on your behalf before the formula gets set for another cycle.
