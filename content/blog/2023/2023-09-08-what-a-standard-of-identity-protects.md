Most members never think about the phrase "standard of identity" until a distributor or a retail buyer asks them a question they can't quite answer: is this legally a cheese, or is it a cheese product? The distinction sounds bureaucratic, but it decides what you can put on your label, what claims your competitors can make next to your wheel in a case, and how much room there is for an imitation product to sit on the shelf wearing your category's name.

A standard of identity is, in plain terms, a legal recipe. It sets the minimum requirements — moisture content, milkfat, permitted ingredients — that a product has to meet before it can be labeled with a given cheese name. Regulators have spent much of this year working through requests to loosen or update some of those recipes, including temporary allowances that let manufacturers test cheese products that deviate from the standard while a longer review plays out. For members making traditional styles the traditional way, that review process is worth watching closely, because the outcome shapes what your label is competing against.

## Why this affects small producers more than it looks

Large processors have the compliance staff to track standards-of-identity changes as a matter of course. Most of our members do not, and the changes tend to arrive as a surprise on a shelf rather than a memo from a regulator. A farmstead creamery we talked to this summer described finding a competitor's "cheese product" sitting next to their aged cheddar at a regional grocer, priced lower, labeled in a way that a casual shopper could easily read as equivalent. Nothing about that was illegal. It was legal precisely because the standard that would have stopped it hadn't been updated in years, and a temporary allowance let the new product onto shelves while the rulemaking caught up.

This is also playing out one aisle over, in the ongoing argument over what plant-based products can call themselves. Dairy trade groups spent much of this summer pushing back on draft guidance that would let plant-based alternatives use dairy terms with only a voluntary nutrition disclaimer rather than a firmer labeling requirement. The specifics are about milk, not cheese, but the underlying fight is the same one members care about: how much a label can imply without being required to prove it. If the bar for using a dairy term gets set low in one product category, it tends not to stay high in the next one for long.

## What we're asking members to do

We are not telling anyone how to feel about any single rule change — reasonable members disagree about how much flexibility processors should get. What we are asking is that members with a stake in a specific cheese category read the current comment period materials before they close, not after. Comment periods on standards of identity changes are exactly the kind of quiet, unglamorous window where a two-paragraph submission from a working cheesemaker carries real weight, because regulators hear overwhelmingly from large manufacturers and trade groups and rarely from the people actually making the product at the standard in question.

If you want help figuring out whether a proposed change touches your category, or how to draft a comment that will actually get read, contact the Membership & Outreach Committee — they keep a running list of open comment periods relevant to specialty and artisan cheese and can walk you through the process the first time. It takes less time than most members expect, and it is one of the few places where an individual creamery's voice reaches the same desk as a national manufacturer's. We would rather members show up to that desk than find out a year from now what got decided while they weren't watching.
