A federal traceability rule for certain high-risk foods, published back in November, became effective this week. Nothing changes overnight for your operation — the actual compliance date is still years away — but the effective date starting matters, because it opens the window where the practical shape of the rule gets decided. That's exactly where the federation's advocacy work happens, and it starts now, not when the compliance deadline is finally close enough to feel urgent.

We wrote in December about this rule naming several soft and semi-soft cheese categories among the foods it designates for additional recordkeeping. This week's update is about what we're actually doing with the lead time between now and compliance: the Membership & Outreach Committee has opened a letter campaign asking for a clearer small-producer exemption path, aimed at operations that make and sell within a single state or a short radius of their own creamery.

## Why the exemption fight is worth your ten minutes

The rule as written applies a uniform recordkeeping standard regardless of scale, and a two-person farmstead tracking a few hundred wheels a month faces the same recordkeeping architecture as a plant running thousands of cases a day. That's not a hypothetical concern — it's the same gap members raised when we covered raw-milk aging thresholds last spring. A rule can be well-intentioned and still land unevenly if it doesn't distinguish between a short regional distribution footprint and a national one with multiple hand-offs between packer, distributor, and retailer.

One member who sells almost entirely through a single farmers' market and a handful of local shops put it to us plainly on a recent call: "I already know exactly where every wheel I make goes, because I sold it to someone I can call by name. Building a formal traceability system for that is real money and real hours I don't have, for a problem I don't actually have." That's precisely the kind of specific, personal account that makes a difference in a comment record — a regulator can dismiss a form letter, but not an operator describing their own supply chain in plain terms.

## What to do this week

If you want to add your name to the letter campaign, the advocacy page on the member portal has the current draft and a short guide on what makes written testimony useful. You don't need to be a policy expert to contribute — you need to describe your own operation accurately: how far your product actually travels, who buys it, and what a formal traceability system would cost you to build and maintain. Those specifics are what turn a form letter into evidence a regulator can point to when weighing an exemption.

We'll keep tracking this rule as the compliance timeline firms up over the next few years, and we'll flag any deadline that affects members directly rather than waiting for a milestone to sneak up on you. For now, the ask is simple: if this rule would touch your operation, tell us how, in your own words, before the comment window narrows further. The federation can summarize what the rule requires; it can't manufacture the specific example from your own creamery that makes an exemption case land.
