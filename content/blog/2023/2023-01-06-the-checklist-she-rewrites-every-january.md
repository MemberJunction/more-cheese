One member we spoke with this week — a quality manager at a mid-sized creamery who asked not to be named, since some of what follows touches on a specific incident with her own equipment — has a habit that sounds almost superstitious until you hear the reasoning. Every January, she throws out last year's quality checklist and writes a new one from scratch, even when almost nothing on it actually changes.

"It's not that the list gets wrong," she told us. "It's that I stop reading it. By November I know it so well I'm just checking boxes on muscle memory, and that's exactly when something gets missed." Rewriting it by hand each January forces her to actually think through every step again — cave temperature logs, swab schedule, lot tracking, the works — instead of trusting that last year's version still fits how the room runs now.

## Why the timing matters this year

This January carries a little more weight than most. A powerful cold front moved across the eastern two-thirds of the country just before Christmas, knocking out power to well over a million households and businesses at its peak, and while her own creamery wasn't directly affected, it was close enough to prompt a hard look at her cold-chain contingency plan. "If the compressor room loses power for six hours, what actually happens to a maturing wheel, and what's my documented response?" she said. "I had an answer, but it was in my head, not on paper. That's not good enough if I'm not the one who's there when it happens."

That's a useful prompt for any member heading into the colder months: a quality plan that lives entirely with one person is a single point of failure, no different from a cave with one door. Writing it down, and making sure at least one other person on staff knows where it lives, costs an afternoon and closes a real gap. It's the kind of thing that's easy to keep meaning to do and easy to keep not doing, right up until a storm makes the gap obvious.

## What she'd tell a newer quality manager

Her advice for anyone newer to the role is to resist the urge to build the perfect system on day one. "Start with what could actually hurt someone or shut you down — temperature, allergens, lot traceability — and get those three airtight before you worry about anything else," she said. She also pointed newer members toward our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate as a starting structure, not because it's a substitute for judgment, but because it gives you a checklist of what a checklist should cover in the first place.

She's also noticed more talk in the trade press lately about quality-monitoring tools that promise to catch problems automatically, and her take is measured: useful as a second check, not a replacement for someone who actually reads the checklist. "A sensor tells you a number moved. It doesn't tell you why, and it definitely doesn't write your incident response for you," she said.

She was quick to add that the annual rewrite isn't really about the paper. "The document at the end looks almost the same as last year's. The value was in the hour I spent actually reading it instead of skimming it." For a member managing a small team through a season that includes both a holiday rush and a run of hard winter weather, that hour is easy to lose and worth protecting.

If you're rewriting your own plan this month, the Food Safety Committee is a good place to ask questions — several members there have been through exactly this exercise and are generally glad to compare notes on what belongs in a cold-chain contingency section.
