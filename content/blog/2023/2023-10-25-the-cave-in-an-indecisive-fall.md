Late October is an awkward month for a cave. The days can still run warm enough to trip a cooling unit into overdrive, and the nights are cold enough that an under-insulated wall will pull heat out of a room faster than a small refrigeration system can replace it. Members with older or converted spaces — a repurposed root cellar, a walk-in that used to hold produce — tend to notice this stretch before anyone with a purpose-built cave does, because it's the weak points in the insulation and the door seals that show up first.

Most cheeses hold their best character somewhere around 50–55°F, with relative humidity anywhere from about 80% for a hard natural-rind style up to the low 90s or higher for a bloomy or washed rind. Those are wide-sounding ranges until you're actually chasing them through a week where the outside temperature moves twenty degrees between noon and midnight. A cave that's been sitting comfortably in range all summer can start drifting in either direction without anyone changing a setting, and a lot of members only notice once a wheel already looks different than it did in August.

## What actually goes wrong first

Humidity swings show up on the rind before temperature swings do, and they show up in opposite ways. Air that runs too dry cracks a rind and pulls moisture — and flavor — out of the paste faster than you want. Air that runs too wet does the reverse: a rind goes slick, a "slip skin" forms under the surface, and you start seeing mold you didn't ask for. If you've noticed a wheel behaving differently than it did in August with no change to your make, the room is usually the first place to look, not the recipe.

The fix isn't usually a bigger piece of equipment. A stable humidity source — a properly maintained brine or salt-solution reservoir, or simply more consistent airflow across the shelves — does more for a marginal cave than a stronger compressor will. Members running small or converted spaces often get more mileage out of adding a passive humidity buffer than out of chasing a thermostat that's already working as hard as it can. It's also worth checking your door seals specifically this month; a gap that didn't matter in July, when the inside and outside temperatures were close together, can pull in a surprising amount of dry air once the gap between them widens.

This is also a good week to look at where your hygrometer actually sits in the room. A reading taken near a fan or a door gets a different number than one taken in a corner where air barely moves, and members troubleshooting a "mystery" humidity problem sometimes find the room is fine and the sensor placement is the actual issue. Moving a single hygrometer to two or three spots for a day is a cheap way to rule that out before you touch anything else.

Whatever your setup, the cheapest thing you can do this week costs nothing: walk the room at two different times of day and note what the thermometer and hygrometer actually say, rather than what you assumed they'd say. A cave that surprises you in late October is a lot more forgiving to fix now than it will be once winter heating kicks on for good, and a small adjustment made this week beats a bigger one made in December once a whole batch is already committed to the shelves.
