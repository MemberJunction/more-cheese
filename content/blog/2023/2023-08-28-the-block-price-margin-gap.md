If you've been watching the wholesale exchange board this month, you already know the headline: cheddar blocks broke past two dollars a pound in August, the highest they've traded in over a year, after spending most of the second quarter stuck below a dollar forty. Barrels followed close behind, gaining anywhere from thirty-five to sixty-five cents over the same stretch. For a member selling into wholesale, that's the first genuinely good pricing news of the summer.

It's also not the whole picture, and we'd rather say that plainly than let the block number do all the talking.

## What actually moved

Federal margin support has been paying out for eight straight months this year, and August's income-over-feed margin came in a shade over six dollars a hundredweight — enough to trigger another round of coverage payments for members enrolled in the program, on top of more than a billion dollars paid out nationally so far this year. That's the context that matters more than the block price alone: feed costs have stayed stubborn even as finished-product prices climbed, so the margin between what a farm pays for hay and grain and what a cheesemaker gets for a wheel hasn't widened nearly as much as the exchange chart suggests.

For members who buy milk rather than make it from their own herd, the story is a little different but rhymes. A higher block price eventually shows up in the milk check, but there's usually a lag of a few weeks between a price move on the board and a producer actually seeing it, and an even longer lag before it changes what a distributor is willing to pay for finished product. If your August felt more stable than exciting, that's probably why — you're living in the gap between the two numbers.

We've heard from more than one member this week asking whether this is the start of a real recovery in wholesale pricing after a rough first half. We don't have a confident answer, and we're suspicious of anyone who does. Global dairy prices, tracked separately from the domestic board, actually kept sliding through August — down again month over month and well below where they sat a year ago, driven mostly by a glut of milk powder overseas. Domestic cheese demand has held up better than the export picture, which is part of why blocks and barrels have decoupled from the global trend, but that decoupling can close in either direction.

If you're deciding whether to lock in a forward contract on the strength of this month's board, our advice is the same as it's been all year: use the margin number, not the block number, to make that call. A wheel that sells for more doesn't help you if the hay behind it cost more too. The Membership & Outreach Committee can point you toward a peer in a similar operation who's already run this math for their own contracts, if you want a second set of eyes before you sign anything.

## What to watch heading into fall

Domestic demand is the piece we're keeping the closest eye on, because it's been quietly doing more work than the price chart gets credit for. Commercial cheese movement has run slightly ahead of last year for most of the summer, which is part of why blocks and barrels found a floor at all after such a weak second quarter — there simply hasn't been as much surplus fresh cheese sitting around to pressure the spot market lower. If that demand holds through the fall wholesale-buying season, it gives this month's price gain a better chance of sticking instead of reversing the way spring's brief rally did.

The other variable is milk supply itself, which has been running tighter than usual in several regions this summer. A tighter supply can support prices for a while, but it's also the kind of condition that reverses quickly once herds and pasture recover, so we'd caution against treating August's numbers as a new baseline. If you sell wholesale, this is a reasonable month to review your contract terms with fresh eyes — not to chase the board, but to make sure whatever you sign next reflects your actual feed costs rather than a headline price that may not hold past harvest season.
