If you set your own milk price because you own the herd or the goats or the sheep, you've probably already felt this. If you buy milk on a federal order or a co-op contract, you're likely starting to see it now: dairy markets opened the year with lower prices and pinched margins for farmers, and the spring numbers, while a little firmer than January's, are still well below last year's highs. This spring's numbers aren't a repeat of last spring's.

None of that is a crisis by itself. It's a correction from an unusually strong stretch, and most members we've talked to this month describe it as "back to normal" rather than "bad." But a softer wholesale milk market changes the arithmetic for anyone whose retail case pricing was set last year against last year's input costs, and it's worth walking through where that shows up first.

## Where the squeeze lands, and where it doesn't

For members who buy finished wheels from a producer rather than making their own, a softer milk price this spring should eventually show up as flatter or slightly lower wholesale cheese pricing — but with a lag, because most supply contracts don't reprice instantly and producers who locked in feed and labor costs during last year's higher-price environment aren't necessarily eager to pass savings through immediately. If your buyer is telling you pricing hasn't moved yet, that's not evasive; it's how the lag usually works.

For members who are the producer, the calculation is more direct and less comfortable. Milk production has stayed strong even as prices softened, which is part of why margins are getting squeezed from both directions this year — more milk chasing lower prices. A lower milk price generally means thinner margins on fluid sales and can shift the incentive toward value-added products — aged wheels, flavored lines, anything that captures more margin per pound of milk than a wholesale block sale does. Several members in our directory made exactly that pivot during the last downturn and never fully reversed it, which is one reason the artisan and specialty segment has kept growing even through periods when raw milk prices were flat or falling.

Producer members carrying federal margin-protection coverage have some cushion here, since payments are triggered when the gap between the milk price and feed costs narrows past a chosen threshold. That cushion matters more in a year like this one than it did during the higher-margin stretch just behind us, and it's worth a second look if you haven't revisited your coverage election recently.

## What we're watching through the summer

Two things worth tracking over the next few months: whether cheese prices hold their recent, modest recovery off the winter lows or slide again, and whether feed costs — which drove a lot of last year's producer cost pressure — ease at the same pace milk prices might. If milk prices fall further while feed doesn't, that's a worse spring for producer members than the current numbers suggest. Some analysts are also watching demand from overseas buyers, since a slowdown there earlier this year was part of what pushed prices down to begin with.

If your business plan or your loan covenants are built around last year's milk-price assumptions, this is a reasonable week to revisit them rather than wait for a surprise later in the year. The federation doesn't set price targets or give financial advice, but the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly this kind of scenario planning, and it's open for enrollment now.
