A few states began letting outdoor markets and roadside stands reopen this month, some with a simple spacing requirement and some with a longer checklist covering hand-washing stations, single-direction foot traffic, and a cap on how many shoppers can be at a booth at once. If your stall has been dark since March, or running as a pickup-only table in a parking lot, getting back to something that looks like a real market this summer is going to mean relearning a layout you thought you already knew.

This is exactly the gap our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course was built to close, and this spring's cohort has been unusually well attended, which tells us we're not the only ones thinking about it.

## What changes at the booth

The biggest shift members are reporting isn't the product, it's the flow. A stall that used to invite browsing, where a shopper could linger over three wedges before deciding, now works better as a faster transaction: pre-portioned cuts, a visible price list, and a single point of contact so you're not passing a knife or a sample tray back and forth across the table. If you cut to order, that's still possible, but plan for a dedicated cutting surface that isn't also your point-of-sale table, and think through where the sanitizer and gloves live so a helper isn't hunting for them mid-line.

Sampling is the harder question, and it's one we don't have a single answer for, because the rules vary by state and sometimes by county. Some markets are allowing pre-packaged single-bite samples handed out by a masked staffer; others have suspended sampling entirely for the season. Check with your market organizer before you build a summer plan around tastings, and don't assume this year's rules will hold into fall — several members told us their county's guidance changed twice between April and May.

The course also spends real time on signage, because a stall that used to rely on a shopper stopping to ask a question now has to answer that question from six feet away. Members who've reopened already say a clear, large-print card next to each cheese, listing milk type, age, and one sentence of flavor description, has done more for sales than any amount of chatting used to. It's a small adjustment, but it changes how you think about your table setup weeks before market day, not the morning of.

## Pricing and inventory for a different summer

The course also covers a piece that's easy to skip when you're focused on layout: how to plan inventory for a market season where foot traffic is likely to be lower but average order size might be higher, as shoppers consolidate trips instead of browsing weekly. A few members who've reopened already told us their per-transaction total is up even as headcount is down, which changes how you think about how much to bring and how you price a bundle versus a single cut. Bringing your usual Saturday volume to a market running at half its normal foot traffic just means hauling cheese back home at the end of the day, and cheese that's been sitting out in a cooler bag all afternoon isn't cheese you want to bring back out again next week.

There's also a cash-flow angle worth planning for: several members switched to contactless payment this spring and found it sped up the line more than they expected, which matters when you're also trying to keep a queue from bunching up at the booth.

If you're weighing whether this summer is the right time to bring your stall back, the course runs in a short cohort format and the next session has open seats. Reach out through the Education Committee if you want a syllabus before you commit, and if your state's rules have changed since you last checked, ask your market organizer for the current written guidance rather than relying on what a neighboring vendor tells you at setup.
