A lot of the members who called us in April to ask about food-bank donations are calling us again this month with a different problem: their farm stand or market table has grown faster than the systems behind it. That's a good problem to have, and it's also a real one. A cash box and a handshake worked fine at a fraction of the volume; it stops working somewhere around the point where you're doing three times last July's traffic on a single Saturday.

Nationally, direct sales from farms to consumers grew sharply this year, and the growth wasn't even across channels — market-stall traffic rose, but farm stores, subscription boxes, and other direct arrangements grew faster still, as shoppers who wanted to avoid crowded grocery aisles looked for a source they trusted. We've seen the same split among our members: the people who added an online order form or a standing weekly box in April are the ones telling us this month that they can't keep up.

## What actually breaks first

It's rarely the cheese. It's the paperwork behind it. Members tell us the first thing to give out is inventory tracking — knowing how many wheels of what age are actually available to sell this Saturday, as opposed to what's in the cave. The second is pricing consistency: a stand run by two or three rotating family members starts quoting different prices for the same wedge, and regular customers notice. The third, and the one with real consequences, is recordkeeping for anything sold interstate or shipped — a casual mail order that started as a favor for a former neighbor can turn into a compliance question you didn't know you'd taken on.

Our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course was built for exactly this stretch — not the theory of retail, but the specific mechanics of running a stand or a subscription program at a scale bigger than one person can hold in their head. It covers a simple inventory log you can run from a clipboard or a shared spreadheet, a pricing sheet that keeps every family member quoting the same number, and a plain-language walkthrough of what changes once you start shipping cheese across a state line.

## A caution on the shipping question specifically

We are seeing more members ask about mail-order cheese sales this year than in any prior year we can compare it to, and we want to be direct about the limits of what we can tell you in a blog post: interstate shipment of cheese, especially anything unpasteurized or aged under specific thresholds, is governed by rules that vary by product type and by the states on both ends of the shipment. The course walks through the general framework and the questions you need to be asking, but it is not a substitute for checking your specific product and route against current requirements before you ship. If you're not sure, ask before you box it up, not after.

If your stand, stall, or subscription list outgrew your system sometime this spring, you're not alone, and there's no shame in fixing the plumbing three months into a good season instead of before it started. The next Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets cohort is filling from members in exactly your position — reach out to the Education Committee to get on the list.
