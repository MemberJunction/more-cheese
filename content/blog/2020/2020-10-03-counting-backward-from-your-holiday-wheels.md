If you're planning to have finished wheels ready for a holiday case in December, October is the month the plan stops being a plan. A twelve-week aging schedule started this month lands right where you need it; started in November, it doesn't. Our Maturation Planning for Small Rooms course exists mostly to walk members through that backward math, and this year it's carrying more weight than usual because the demand it's aiming at is harder to predict than it was last October.

The basic method hasn't changed: pick your target ready-date, subtract the style's aging window, and that's your make date. What's changed is how much you can trust the demand number on the other end of that calculation. Wholesale accounts that normally place a predictable holiday order have been placing smaller ones with less notice this year, while direct-to-consumer sales — a farm stand, a subscription box, a market stall — have in some cases held steadier or grown. A room planned entirely around last year's wholesale order could end up either short on the wheels people actually want or sitting on ones nobody's ordering.

## Building slack into the shelf, not just the schedule

The course's core advice for a year like this one is to stop planning your maturation room as a single committed batch and start planning it as a small number of committed wheels plus a larger number of flexible ones. Commit early to whatever you already have firm orders for. For the rest, choose a style with a wide acceptable finishing window — something that's good at eight weeks and still good at eleven — so a shift in demand three weeks out doesn't mean cheese that's either underripe or over its window by the time an order actually shows up.

That flexibility costs you something: styles with a wide finishing window aren't always your most distinctive ones, and a shelf built for slack takes more space than a shelf built for a single committed run. The course walks through the trade-off in more detail than we can here, including a worksheet for mapping your own room's capacity against a range of demand scenarios instead of a single guess.

This week's federal dairy margin coverage report shows the cost side of the ledger holding roughly steady after a summer of sharper swings, which is a small mercy if you're trying to budget a holiday make against a milk price that isn't moving under you every few days. That steadiness is only part of the picture — federal direct-assistance payments tied to this year's production have also started factoring into how some members are deciding whether to expand a holiday run at all, on top of whatever the wholesale order book looks like. Neither number is a substitute for your own order book, but they're both worth having in front of you before you commit the room.

There's a specific mistake the course spends real time on: treating "we sold out last December" as a demand forecast rather than a data point from a very different year. If 2019's holiday season is the only number in your head, you're planning against a market that doesn't exist anymore. Better to combine that historical number with what your own order book looks like right now — how many firm commitments you actually have in hand this October compared to the same week last year — and let the gap between those two numbers tell you how much of your room should go into the flexible tier.

## Where to start

If you haven't started your October make yet, this week is still early enough to run the backward math properly. Pull your target ready-dates, check them against your style's typical finishing window, and build in the flexible tier before you commit the room to a single plan. The next Maturation Planning for Small Rooms cohort is open for enrollment now, and the Education Committee can point you to members who've run this exercise in past years if you want to see how the worksheet plays out in practice before you commit your own shelf space.
