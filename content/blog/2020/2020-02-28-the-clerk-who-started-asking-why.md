Not every member spotlight is a cheesemaker or a business owner. This one is about someone who's spent three years behind a specialty cheese counter at a mid-sized regional retailer, and who joined the federation last year for one reason: she was tired of guessing.

It's also a role the federation doesn't spotlight often enough. Most of our programming, and most of our public-facing content, skews toward the people making the cheese rather than the people selling it face to face, which is a gap given how much of a member's reputation with the public actually gets built at a counter, not in a make room.

"A customer would ask why one raw-milk cheddar cost twice what the other one did, and I'd say something about aging, which is true but isn't really an answer," she told us. "I wanted to actually know, not just sound like I knew." She started attending our webinars first, mostly the ones on affinage and milk sourcing, and signed up for the Certified Cheesemonger credential track about eight months ago. She's two exams into the four-part sequence.

## What changed behind the counter

The credential is built around four areas — production knowledge, pairing and service, sanitation and handling, and business fundamentals — and she said the production module was the one that reshaped how she talks to customers most. "Once you understand what a washed rind is actually doing, biologically, you stop reciting facts and start explaining a process. Customers can tell the difference, even if they couldn't say why." She's noticed her average transaction size creep up slightly since she started recommending pairings with more specificity instead of defaulting to "it's popular."

She's also started thinking about her counter in a wider context. Federal agricultural data published this month describes decades of steady consolidation into fewer, much larger dairy operations nationally. Her store's specialty case runs almost entirely the opposite direction — small producers, small batches, and a story behind nearly every wheel — and she thinks that contrast is part of why customers linger at her counter rather than grabbing shrink-wrapped cheddar off a shelf and moving on. "People want to know it came from somewhere specific," she said. "I can't tell them that about the shrink-wrap. I can tell them that about almost everything in my case."

The sanitation and handling module, by her account, was less glamorous but more useful day to day. "Nobody at the counter had ever explained to me why we wrap certain wheels in paper and not plastic, or why a knife needs its own board per cheese family. I'd been doing it because I was told to, not because I understood the contamination risk." She said that module alone changed how she trains newer part-time staff.

## A small credential, a specific effect

She's candid that the credential hasn't changed her title or her pay, at least not yet — that conversation is still ahead of her. What it has changed, she said, is how much she trusts her own answers on the floor, and how often a customer comes back specifically asking for her. "That's not nothing in a job where turnover is high and most training is fifteen minutes with a laminated sheet."

Retail is one of the parts of the specialty cheese business the federation hears from less often, since most of our programming skews toward producers and affineurs. If you work a counter and you're curious about the Certified Cheesemonger pathway, the certification page on the member portal has the exam schedule and a study guide for each module, and the Education Committee can put you in touch with members further along the track who've offered to answer questions.
