Our pH, Moisture & Make-Sheet Fundamentals course starts today, and this year it's opening
to a fuller room than usual — cohorts have been running heavier since March, and this one's
no exception. Some of that is people finally having the evening hours to sit down with a
course they'd been meaning to take. Some of it is newer producers who picked up a wheel
press in April and are now realizing that selling cheese and making consistent cheese are
two different skills.

Either way, the subject holds up. A make sheet isn't paperwork for its own sake. It's the
record that tells you, three weeks after a batch, whether Tuesday's vat behaved differently
from Monday's — and whether that difference was the milk, the culture, the room, or you.

## Why the number matters more than the feeling

Ask most cheesemakers how a batch went and you'll get an instinct: "that one felt slow" or
"the curd knit up faster than usual." Instincts are worth having, but they're not
diagnostic. A make sheet turns a feeling into a number you can compare — pH at set, pH at
whey-off, moisture at pressing — and numbers are what let you find the actual variable
instead of guessing at it.

This matters more than usual right now because a lot of members have changed something
about their operation this spring without quite meaning to. Maybe your milk supplier
shifted volume because a wholesale account dropped out from under them and the herd's diet
changed as a result. Maybe you're working solo shifts instead of with a second set of
hands, and your timing between steps has quietly drifted along with it. A lot of that
disruption traces back to the same source: farmers markets and small retail outlets have
been reopening on a rolling, city-by-city basis this month, each with its own new rules on
hours, capacity, and how product gets handled at the table, and every one of those changes
tugs at a schedule that used to be predictable. That's exactly the kind of pressure that
tempts a rushed shortcut on a busy Tuesday. A make sheet catches drift a memory won't,
because memory smooths things over under pressure and a logbook doesn't.

## What the course covers

Four weeks, self-paced enough to work around a farm schedule: reading pH curves instead of
just hitting a target number, understanding what moisture readings actually tell you about
shelf life and food-safety risk, and building a template you'll actually keep using after
the course ends rather than one so elaborate it gets abandoned by August. The food-safety
piece isn't optional — moisture content is one of the levers that determines which
pathogens can and can't get a foothold in your cheese, and the course walks through what
the relevant thresholds mean for your specific style, without pretending to hand you a
guarantee. That's a conversation for your own safety plan and, where it applies, your
regulator — not for a blog post.

We've also added a short module this cohort on record-keeping for cheese that's sitting
longer before it sells than it used to. If your usual seventy-two-hour turnover has
stretched into a week or two because a wholesale account paused, the assumptions your old
make sheet was built on may no longer hold, and it's worth a fresh look before your next
batch rather than after something goes wrong.

If you've been putting this one off because you "already know how to make the cheese,"
that's usually exactly the member who gets the most out of it. The course is open now
through our education page; reach out to the Education Committee if you want to know
whether it lines up with a certification you're already working toward.
