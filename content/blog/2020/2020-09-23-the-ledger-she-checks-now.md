One of our farmstead members runs a small operation in the upper Midwest — a dozen milking cows, one make a day most weeks, and until this year, a costing sheet she updated maybe four times a year. She told a Membership & Outreach volunteer this month that she now pulls it up twice a week, sometimes more, and that the habit isn't going away even if the market calms down.

It's not hard to see why. The weekly federal margin figure that determines her feed-cost coverage moved again between last week's report and this week's, continuing a pattern that's held most of the year: a number that used to shift gradually now moves enough between two consecutive Mondays to change whether a batch pencils out. For a wholesale buyer with a purchasing department, that kind of movement is an inconvenience. For someone who is her own purchasing department, her own production planner, and the person who signs every invoice, it's a different kind of problem.

## What changed in her routine

She didn't overhaul her business. She changed one habit: instead of trusting that this week would look roughly like last week, she started checking the actual margin numbers before deciding how much milk to put into cheese versus hold for fluid sale, and before setting her price at the farm stand. It's a small discipline — fifteen minutes with a spreadsheet, twice a week — but she said it's the difference between finding out she's underpriced in October and finding out in March, when there's nothing left to do about it.

She's also started asking harder questions of the numbers she used to take at face value. A weekly margin report moving a few cents doesn't mean much in the abstract; run it through her actual make and it's suddenly a real number, one she can act on instead of just noticing. That's a habit more of our smaller producers could use this year, and it's exactly the kind of practical costing work our Education Committee has been building resources around since spring.

She'd be the first to say she doesn't love the extra time it takes. What she's found, though, is that the anxiety of not knowing was worse than the fifteen minutes of finding out. A market that moves this much rewards the operators who look at it often enough to catch a shift early, not the ones who wait for a quarterly statement to tell them what already happened.

If any of this sounds familiar — a costing habit that used to be occasional and now needs to be routine — you're not behind. A lot of members have made the same adjustment this year, quietly, without announcing it. It's worth talking to each other about it anyway.

## What we'd do with this

If you haven't rebuilt your costing sheet since the spring, this is a good week to do it — not because anything specific changed, but because the weekly margin numbers underneath it have moved more than once since you last checked. The Education Committee's costing materials are built for exactly this kind of once-a-season update, and a fifteen-minute habit now can save you a much harder conversation later.
