Monday we wrote about the commodity cheese rally and the odd split it's created between retail-facing and foodservice-facing members. A few of you wrote back with a version of the same question: how do I actually know what this means for my own operation, instead of just guessing from a headline number?

That's exactly the gap our *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* course was built to close, and this feels like a good week to walk through the short version.

## The board price isn't your price

The commodity price everyone's quoting right now is a price for standardized block and barrel cheddar. If you're making a bloomy-rind triple crème or an aged alpine-style wheel, that number is a weather report, not a forecast for your own case. It tells you which direction input costs and comparable wholesale prices are probably drifting, but your actual cost per wheel depends on your milk contract, your yield, your labor hours in the cave, and your loss rate — none of which show up on that chart.

The exercise we walk through in the course is deceptively simple: take your last completed batch, and build the real cost of it backward from the milk you bought to the wheel you sold, including the affinage time you paid someone to spend turning it. Most members who do this for the first time are surprised by which line item moved the most since their last pricing review. For a lot of small operations right now, it isn't milk — it's packaging, shipping, and insurance, all of which have gotten more expensive and less predictable this year; several members have told us their liability premiums alone are up close to ten percent at renewal.

You don't need the full course to do a rough gut-check this week. Pull three numbers for your best-selling item: your milk cost per pound of finished cheese, using your actual yield rather than an industry average; your days in the cave or on the shelf, multiplied by whatever you'd pay someone by the hour to do that monitoring, even if it's you doing it unpaid; and your current loss rate — rinds you trim away, wheels that don't make grade, anything that never reaches a customer. If your wholesale price hasn't moved in the last six months and any of those three numbers has, you're not pricing to your actual cost anymore. That's true whether the market moved up, like it has this week, or down.

## When to bring this to your buyer

A price increase justified by "the market went up" is a weak argument to a distributor or retail buyer who knows the market doesn't set your cost structure. A price increase justified by "my packaging and insurance costs are up, and my loss rate improved four points, so here's the net change" is a conversation most buyers will take seriously, because it's specific and it's honest about the parts that got better too.
