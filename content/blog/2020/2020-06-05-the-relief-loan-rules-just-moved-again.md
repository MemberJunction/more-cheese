We told members back in April to read the fine print on the emergency small-business loan program before spending a dollar of it. This week the fine print changed under everyone's feet, and mostly in members' favor. Federal legislation signed this week loosens several of the terms that our shop, creamery, and distributor members had been struggling to hit.

The original program gave borrowers eight weeks to spend loan proceeds, with at least three-quarters of that spending required to go to payroll, in order to qualify for forgiveness. For a lot of our members — especially ones who'd furloughed staff in March and were only now bringing people back as farmers markets and reduced-capacity retail reopened — eight weeks was nowhere near enough time to rebuild a payroll that matched what forgiveness required.

## What actually loosened

The new rules stretch that spending window to twenty-four weeks, or through the end of the year, whichever comes first, for anyone who hadn't already gotten their loan before this week. Borrowers who'd already received funds under the old eight-week terms can choose to keep that shorter window if it works better for their books, or switch to the longer one. The payroll-spending threshold for forgiveness also drops from seventy-five percent to sixty percent, which matters a great deal to members carrying real non-payroll costs right now — packaging, cold storage, the kind of expenses that don't show up as wages but that a creamery or cheese shop can't operate without.

There's also new language addressing something several members raised with our advocacy committee directly: what happens if you can't rebuild your headcount because local health guidance won't let you. The new rules say forgiveness won't be reduced for failing to restore staffing levels if a business can document that it couldn't return to the same level of operation because of sanitation or distancing requirements tied to the public-health response. That's a real answer to a real question we heard from more than one shop member who reopened at reduced hours and reduced staff because that's all the space allowed.

## What to do this week

If you took a loan under the original terms, talk to whoever holds it about which covered period actually serves you better — the math depends on your specific payroll timeline, and it's not automatic. Members who took their loan early, back when the program first opened, are the ones most likely to have already burned through an eight-week window without hitting the old payroll threshold. For them, the choice of covered period is the single most consequential decision in this update, and it's worth a call to your lender before you assume the answer is obvious.

If you haven't yet spent down your loan, the new sixty percent threshold and twenty-four week window give you meaningfully more room to plan a return to staffing that matches your actual reopening, rather than a forced sprint to hit an eight-week deadline that never fit a creamery's real recovery curve. That also means you have more room to spend deliberately on the non-payroll costs — packaging, cold storage, the sanitation supplies a lot of members have been buying since March — that count differently now that the payroll share has dropped.

One more detail worth flagging: loans issued from this week forward carry a five-year term instead of the original two years, which changes the monthly math even on the portion that doesn't end up forgiven. If your creamery or shop is carrying other debt against the same cash flow, that longer term is worth factoring into whatever you're planning for the rest of the year.

We flagged this fight in April and we'll keep flagging what comes next. If your business is running into forgiveness terms that still don't reflect how a small creamery or shop actually operates, tell our advocacy committee. That's exactly the kind of detail that got fixed this week because members spoke up about it in the first place.
