If you glanced at a dairy futures screen this morning, you probably did a double take. The broader financial markets opened sharply lower today, dragged down by a widening respiratory illness outbreak and a separate jolt in oil prices, and dairy contracts moved with them, adding volatility to a milk-price picture that had already softened over the past few weeks. Members who watch Class III and Class IV futures as a pricing signal are telling us the board has been unusually hard to read since late February, with wide daily swings replacing the slow drift we'd gotten used to.

That's a genuinely uncomfortable place to sit if you're finalizing spring contracts or setting wholesale prices for the next quarter. A few members on our Membership & Outreach Committee calls this week described holding off on locking in supply agreements they'd normally have signed by now, simply because neither side wants to commit to a number that might look wrong in three weeks. One producer member put it bluntly on the call: he'd rather sign a shorter contract at a slightly worse rate than lock in twelve months against a board that's still finding its footing.

## What we're watching, and what we're not predicting

We want to be careful here: we're a trade association, not a trading desk, and nobody on our staff is going to tell you where the futures board lands next month. What we can tell you is what members are reporting from the ground, which is a split story. Retail cheese and butter sales have actually picked up in some regions as households stock pantries, while food-service and restaurant-channel orders — a meaningful share of business for members who sell to wholesale accounts — have started to soften in the areas seeing the earliest illness clusters. If your business straddles both channels, you may be feeling pulled in two directions at once this month, and that's not a sign you're doing anything wrong.

Livestock and feeder markets outside dairy have already seen a sharp run-up in volatility over the past several weeks, and analysts covering that side of agriculture have been urging producers to stay disciplined rather than react to any single day's print. We think the same advice holds for dairy: a board that swings five percent in a session is telling you it's uncertain, not that it's found a new fair price.

For members with milk supply contracts coming up for renewal, our general advice is the same as it always is in a volatile stretch: build in a shorter review window if your buyer will agree to one, and don't treat a single week's futures print as the number you'll be living with all season. A twelve-month average smooths out a lot of noise that a single Tuesday close does not, and it gives both sides of a contract a fairer basis to negotiate from than whatever the screen happens to show this morning.

## Where to take questions

If your creamery or shop is trying to figure out how much of this volatility to build into near-term pricing, that's exactly the kind of question our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course is built to walk through — not to tell you a number, but to give you a framework for stress-testing one against a range of outcomes rather than a single forecast. We'll also be watching the export lane closely, since a stronger dollar tends to show up there before it shows up anywhere else, and we'll flag anything material in next week's update.

In the meantime, if your region is seeing a channel shift — more retail, less food-service, or the reverse — tell your Membership & Outreach Committee contact. We're collecting exactly that kind of on-the-ground read right now, and it's more useful to us this month than it usually is.
