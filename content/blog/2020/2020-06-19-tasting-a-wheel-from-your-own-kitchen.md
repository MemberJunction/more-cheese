Sensory evaluation has always assumed a room: controlled lighting, neutral surfaces, a facilitator handing out identical samples to everyone at the same moment, no outside smells or noise to skew a palate. Almost none of that has been available to most members since March, and the credential we built around it — the Sensory Evaluation Certificate — has had to figure out fairly quickly what still holds up when the room disappears.

The short answer is: more than we expected, with real caveats we're not going to pretend away.

## What's transferring to a kitchen table

The analytical framework — how to describe texture, how to separate aroma from flavor, how to score consistently rather than just impressionistically — travels fine outside a lab. Members working through the coursework at home can still learn to identify a fault, name a specific off-flavor, and build the vocabulary that makes a tasting note useful to someone who wasn't in the room. That part of the certificate hasn't changed, and if anything, some instructors have told us that members without the built-in structure of a group session are being forced to be more disciplined about their own note-taking, which isn't a bad habit to build.

What doesn't transfer as cleanly is anything that depends on identical samples reaching every candidate at the same time in the same condition. A shared reference set, evaluated together, catches individual palate drift in a way that mailing samples to different kitchens across different climates and different shipping timelines simply can't replicate exactly. We're not going to claim a home-tasting exam component is equivalent to a proctored group session, because it isn't, and members who've completed both formats tell us they notice the difference.

## What we're actually doing about it

For now, we've split the certificate's practical component into two paths. Members near an approved regional tasting site can still sit for the traditional group-proctored evaluation, distanced and scheduled in smaller cohorts than before. Members without reasonable access to a site can complete a remote evaluation, with reference samples shipped ahead of time, a live video-proctored session, and a slightly wider acceptable scoring range that accounts for the reality of a kitchen counter instead of a controlled tasting room. Neither path is labeled as lesser on the credential itself; we made a judgment call that access mattered more than forcing a false equivalence, and we're being upfront with members about the tradeoff we made.

We'll be watching completion data from both paths this year, and if the remote format turns out to produce meaningfully different outcomes, we'll say so and adjust rather than quietly leaving it in place. If you're partway through the certificate and unsure which path makes sense for you, the Education Committee can walk through what each format actually involves before you commit. And if you've already sat for a remote evaluation this spring, we'd like to hear how it went — your experience is part of how we decide whether this stays a temporary accommodation or becomes a permanent second track.
