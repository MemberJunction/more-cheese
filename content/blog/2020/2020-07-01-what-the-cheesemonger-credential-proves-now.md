We've had more calls about the Certified Cheesemonger credential in the last six
weeks than in the six months before that. Part of it is timing: shops that scaled
their counter staff down in April are hiring back now, sometimes with people who've
never worked a cheese case before. A credential that used to be a nice-to-have on a
résumé has turned into something owners are asking about directly during interviews.

So it's worth being precise about what it does and doesn't cover.

## What the credential verifies

Certified Cheesemonger is built around the working knowledge a counter needs day to
day: identifying and describing cheese by family and style, correct storage and
cutting practice, pairing and recommendation skills, and basic sanitation at the
point of sale. It is not a food-safety management credential — that's what our Food
Safety & HACCP Certificate covers, and the two are meant to be complementary rather
than substitutes for each other. A shop that's rebuilding its floor team after a slow
spring often wants both, in that order: safety fundamentals first, then the
merchandising and product knowledge that make a counter person genuinely useful to a
customer.

A few members running smaller retail operations, including The Quiet Curd and
Orchardmere Cheese & Provisions, told us they're now treating the credential as a
90-day goal for new counter hires rather than something reserved for veteran staff.
That's a shift from how the program was used in past years, when it skewed toward
people five or more years into a cheesemongering career.

The written and practical components have both moved to formats that don't require
travel, and enrollment this spring ran well above what we'd normally see for early
summer. Members tell us the shorter, spaced-out session format has actually made it
easier to fit around a shift schedule than the old single-day intensive was, which
matters when your floor team is already stretched thin.

Part of why the credential feels more urgent right now is that a counter person's
job has gotten harder without one of its best tools. Sampling has been paused at
most shops since March, and as of late June only a few larger retailers had started
cautiously bringing it back, in modified form, behind a shield rather than left out
for self-service. That leaves a counter person selling an unfamiliar wedge on
description alone — no taste to fall back on when a customer hesitates. That's
exactly the muscle the credential trains: naming what a cheese is going to taste and
feel like before anyone bites into it, and reading a customer well enough to know
which wedge to reach for in the first place. A few members have also picked up new
counter staff this spring through unrelated channels, including one or two people
who came off short-term work supporting a regional dairy purchase program before it
wound down; the credential has been useful precisely because it doesn't assume any
prior cheese-specific experience.

If your shop is short-staffed, we understand that sending someone through a
multi-week credential program feels like a stretch. Members who've done it anyway
say the return shows up fast — fewer wrong cuts, faster case turnover, and a
noticeably shorter runway before a new hire can handle a walk-in customer's questions
without pulling in a manager. It also gives an owner something concrete to point to
when a customer asks why they should trust the person behind the case, which matters
more this year than it used to, when a lot of familiar faces have rotated out of
retail entirely.

## Getting started

If you're weighing whether now is the right time, our read is simple: if you're
hiring at all this summer, it's a good time. Reach out to the Education Committee for
the current course calendar and prerequisite details, and if cost is the barrier,
ask about the member-rate cohorts before you rule it out.
