We talk to a lot of members who've been in this industry for decades, so it's worth pausing this month for someone at the other end of the timeline: a second-year apprentice at a small goat dairy in the upper Midwest, still building the habits that will carry the rest of her career.

She joined the federation last year, mostly because her head cheesemaker insisted, and has since become one of the more active names on our course rosters — two completed already, a third in progress. When we asked what's actually made the difference in her first two years on the vat, her answer wasn't a technique. It was a notebook. Two of them, actually.

## The make sheet, and the other one

Every apprentice learns to keep a make sheet: milk temperature, culture addition time, rennet dose, cut size, cook temperature, the whole sequence a food-safety plan and a quality program both depend on. Apprenticeship listings across the industry treat that kind of record keeping — logging recipes, ingredients, quantities, and test results — as a baseline skill, not an advanced one, and that matches what she was taught in her first month. What she does differently is keep a second notebook for the makes that didn't go the way the sheet predicted.

"The make sheet tells you what you did," she told us. "It doesn't tell you why the wheel came out wrong anyway." Her second notebook is where she writes down her own guesses — a slower-than-usual flush that changed the milk's protein balance, a cave door propped open too long during a delivery, a batch of culture that seemed sluggish from the start. Some of her guesses turn out to be wrong. She keeps those entries too, crossed out but legible, because she says the wrong guesses are how she's learning to ask better questions.

## Small dairy, close feedback loop

Working at a small operation has its own advantage here: she's often the one who both makes the cheese and stands behind the counter selling it a few weeks later, so she hears directly when a batch tastes different than the one before it. That loop — make it, sell it, hear about it, write it down — is compressed in a way it wouldn't be at a larger operation with more people between the vat and the customer. Formal apprenticeship programs at small dairies tend to build in exactly that kind of rotation, moving a trainee between milking, make room, affinage, and the sales counter rather than keeping them on one station, and she credits that structure with forcing the habit early rather than letting her specialize before she'd seen the whole chain.

She's not chasing a specific certification yet, though she's been asking around about a next step now that she has a season of goat's-milk cheesemaking behind her. For now, her goal is simpler: get through her second full production year with a set of notebooks detailed enough that she could hand them to the next apprentice and save that person a year of guessing. She's already started a third notebook, a slimmer one, that's nothing but questions she hasn't answered yet — a running list she says she expects to be adding to for a while longer.

If you're a small operation training someone new this year, her approach costs nothing but a second notebook and the discipline to actually write in it. It might be worth borrowing, and if you want a broader look at how a rotation-based apprenticeship structure could work for your own operation, our Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a short list of member creameries willing to talk through what their programs actually look like day to day.
