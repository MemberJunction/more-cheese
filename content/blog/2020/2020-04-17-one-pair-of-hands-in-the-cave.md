A member wrote to us last week to explain, almost apologetically, why she hadn't returned a form on time: she's the only one going into the aging room right now. Her farmstead creamery normally rotates three people through wheel-turning and rind care across the week. As of this month, it's just her, twice a day, alone.

She's not unusual this spring. With wholesale orders down sharply across the industry and at least one member telling us their restaurant accounts had all but disappeared overnight, several small producers have cut shifts to the owner and one or two others rather than lay anyone off outright. The aging room is often the first place that shows the strain, because it's the part of the operation that can't be paused, sped up, or done from a laptop. Milk still comes in on the same schedule the cows and goats set, whether or not there's anywhere to sell what's made from it.

## What actually changes with one person instead of three

She walked us through it plainly when we asked. The physical work is the same — brine washes, turning, checking for unwanted mold, watching humidity — but the pace and the judgment calls change in ways that took her by surprise:

- **Rounds take longer**, which means the wheels at the end of a rotation sit slightly longer between turns than they used to. She's adjusted by reordering the room so the most sensitive wheels — younger, softer-rind styles — get handled first each pass, and the sturdier aged wheels wait a little longer without much risk.
- **There's no second opinion.** Normally if she wasn't sure about a rind, she'd ask whoever else was in the room that day. Now she photographs anything that looks borderline and keeps a running log instead, so she has something concrete to compare against a week later rather than relying on memory alone.
- **She's simplified the rotation schedule itself**, moving from a complex multi-style calendar to fewer, larger batches that are easier to track solo. It's not how she'd choose to run the cave in a normal year, and she's the first to say a more varied case sells better, but it's held quality steady through a genuinely hard stretch.

## The part she says surprised her

What she didn't expect was how much the log-keeping habit has taught her. Writing down her own uncertainty in real time — "rind looks slightly tacky, watching, not treating yet" — turned out to be useful information she'd never captured when there were three sets of eyes to rely on informally in the room. Nobody had ever asked her to write any of it down before; it was tribal knowledge that lived in conversation, not on paper. She told us she's planning to keep some version of that log even after staffing is back to normal, because she can already see gaps in her own memory that the written record would have caught.

She was quick to add that she doesn't think of this as a silver lining. Fewer hands in the cave is a hardship, not an efficiency gain, and she'd trade the tidier log for her old rotation back in a heartbeat if the wholesale orders came back tomorrow. But she also didn't want the habit to disappear the moment things return to normal, and that seems like the right instinct to hold onto.

If you're managing a cave, a make room, or a retail counter with fewer hands than you're used to this spring, a simple written log of what you're watching and why is one of the cheapest changes you can make, and it keeps paying off later even once normal staffing returns.
