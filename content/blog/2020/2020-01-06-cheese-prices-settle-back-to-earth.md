Every member who has been through a holiday season knows the pattern: cheese and butter move fast in November and December, and then the market takes a breath in January. This year the breath is a little deeper than most of us expected.

Cheddar barrels, which were trading above $1.60 a pound for parts of the fourth quarter, have slid back toward the middle of a $1.47 to $1.64 range as the new year opens. Class III futures, which touched a strong high late last year, are pointing to a print several dollars below where December finished. If your co-op statement or your buyer's opening offer looks softer than you expected this week, you are not imagining it — the whole class is repricing at once.

## What's actually moving, and what isn't

The one bright spot in an otherwise quiet market is dry whey, which has firmed modestly since the start of the year. For members running combination operations — vat cheese plus a whey stream, even a modest one sold to a processor — that firmness is worth watching. It won't offset a soft Class III number on its own, but it's the one line on the sheet moving in your favor right now.

Butter, for what it's worth, is holding up better than cheese. That's cold comfort if your business is built entirely around the cheese case, but it's a useful data point if you're negotiating a component contract that blends multiple classes.

None of this is a crisis. January softness after a strong holiday season is closer to the historical norm than the stronger finish we had in November was. But it is a reminder that the fourth-quarter numbers on your year-end statement are a peak, not a baseline, and your February and March cash flow planning should be built on the softer figure, not the one that made your December look great.

We heard from a few members over the holidays who were surprised by how quickly the mood on a sales call can shift once the calendar turns. A buyer who was pushing hard for volume in the second week of December can be a very different, much more price-sensitive conversation partner by the second week of January. That isn't anyone acting in bad faith — it's the same seasonal pattern showing up on their side of the desk as it is on yours. Knowing that going in tends to make the January renegotiation a little less personal and a little more mechanical.

## What to do with this in your own numbers

A few members have already written in asking whether it's worth locking in forward pricing on any February or March volume. That's a conversation for your own buyer and your own risk tolerance — we don't give trading advice, and a wrong guess on futures can cost you more than the softness itself. What we can tell you is that the spread between barrel and block cheddar has been unusually wide the past few weeks, which tends to signal a market that hasn't found its footing yet. If you're negotiating a contract this month, ask which reference price it's actually tied to before you sign.

If you want the raw numbers behind this rather than our summary of them, the federal dairy market reports are public and free, and several state farm-extension offices publish a plain-language monthly outlook that's a faster read than the full federal release. We'll keep flagging anything that looks like more than seasonal noise as the quarter goes on.
