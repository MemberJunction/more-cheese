Earlier this year, the federal comment window on interstate cheese shipment rules reopened, and it's been sitting quietly in the background of a very loud year. It's not quiet for much longer — the window is closing soon, and we want to make sure every member who has a stake in it has actually weighed in before it does.

The core of it is familiar to anyone who's shipped a raw-milk wheel across a state line: the 60-day aging requirement, the labeling that has to travel with the cheese, and the enforcement discretion that varies more by inspector than any of us would like. This round of comments is specifically about how those rules apply to smaller producers who ship occasionally rather than as a core part of their business — a category that describes a lot of our farmstead members, and one the current rule text doesn't address especially clearly.

## What members have been telling us

Our advocacy volunteers have been busy. Between letter campaigns, individual testimony, and one memorable coalition call that ran forty minutes over its slot, we've logged well over a hundred distinct actions from members this cycle on this single topic — more than any other issue we track right now. A few threads keep coming up. Producers who age in shared cave space with other makers want clarity on how the labeling requirement applies when wheels from different sources aren't cut and packaged until the aging clock has already run. Smaller shippers want a straight answer on whether an occasional interstate sale — a single pallet twice a year — triggers the same paperwork as a full-time wholesale account.

One member, a longtime farmstead producer who's been filing testimony on raw-milk rules for years, put it to us plainly on a call last week: the rule as written seems to have been drafted with a mid-size regional creamery in mind, and it doesn't flex well for the kitchen-table operation that ships four times a season. That's exactly the kind of specific, first-hand account that carries weight in a federal comment file — general objections don't move a rule, concrete examples of how it plays out on a real production schedule do.

## How to add your voice

If you ship raw-milk cheese across state lines even occasionally, this affects you whether or not you've ever thought of yourself as someone who does advocacy. You don't need a lawyer or a polished letter. A short account of your own aging and labeling practice, and where the current rule text creates a genuine ambiguity for you, is more useful than a general statement of support or opposition.

The Membership & Outreach Committee has a template letter and the submission link on the advocacy page, along with a plain-language summary of what the rule currently says versus what's proposed. If you want a second set of eyes before you submit, the committee is holding office hours this week specifically for this — reach out and they'll find you a slot before the window closes.

Comment periods on this rule have opened and closed before without much changing, and we understand if some members feel like this is a familiar exercise that rarely moves anywhere. This round has a narrower, more specific question attached to it than past versions did, which is actually an advantage — a rule that's being reconsidered for a defined category of small, occasional shippers is easier to influence with concrete examples than a rule being reconsidered in the abstract. Regulators respond to a stack of comments that all describe the same practical friction more than they respond to a stack of comments that each object to the rule on different grounds.

That's also why we're asking members to be specific rather than passionate. A letter that says "this rule hurts small producers" gets read and filed. A letter that says "I age wheels in a shared cave with two other makers, our cheese isn't cut and labeled until the aging clock has run, and the current text doesn't say clearly whose responsibility the interstate label is at that point" gets quoted. We've seen it happen in past rulemakings on other topics, and it's the difference that matters here.

Comments don't disappear into a void — federal rulemaking requires the agency to respond to the substance of what's raised, even if the response is eventually just "no change." We'll be watching the docket after the window closes and will let members know what comes back, whether that's a revised rule, a clarifying guidance document, or nothing at all. In the meantime, the office hours slots the committee has open this week are the fastest way to get a letter in front of us before you submit it.
