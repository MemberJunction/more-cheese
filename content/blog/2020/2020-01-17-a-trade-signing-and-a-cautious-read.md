A new trade agreement between the United States and a major overseas trading partner was signed this week, and it includes language specifically aimed at dairy and infant-formula access — a market segment that had seen a sharp, multi-year decline in exports under a round of retaliatory tariffs. For a federation membership with meaningful export exposure, that's the kind of headline worth reading past the summary paragraph.

## What actually changed

The agreement addresses two separate things members sometimes conflate: tariff rates and nontariff barriers. On tariffs, the retaliatory duties that had made US dairy products uncompetitive in that market largely remain in place for now — the agreement is a step, not a resolution, on that front. Where it does more is on the regulatory side: it commits the importing country to recognizing facility inspections already performed by domestic food-safety authorities, rather than requiring a separate, duplicative approval process before a shipment can clear customs. For a mid-sized exporter, that second piece can matter more day-to-day than a tariff percentage, because it's the paperwork delay, not the duty rate, that most often kills a shipment window.

## Why the federation's read is cautious rather than celebratory

We'd rather be straightforward with members than run a victory lap the trade press itself isn't running. Export volumes to that market had fallen sharply over the prior year, and this agreement does not undo that on its own — the underlying tariff structure that drove the decline is still largely intact. What it does is remove some of the friction on the compliance side, which mostly benefits members who already have export relationships and infrastructure in place, less so members who are just starting to explore that market. If you don't currently export, this week's news doesn't change your calculus much. If you do, it's worth a conversation with your customs broker about what the facility-recognition language means for your specific paperwork, because the practical effect varies by product category and by which agency handled your original inspection.

Export data takes a few months to reflect a policy change like this one, so we won't have a clean read on actual shipment volume until later in the spring at the earliest. In the meantime, the federation's export documentation course remains the most direct resource if your operation is trying to figure out whether this market is now worth pursuing — it walks through the current certification and labeling requirements as they stand today, not as they might look once the fuller effects of this agreement settle out.

Domestically, cheese prices have also been soft coming into the new year, which is a separate story from the trade news but one that compounds it for members weighing whether export markets are worth the added complexity right now. We'll keep members posted through the bulletin as clearer trade data comes in, and the Membership & Outreach Committee is collecting questions from members with active export operations if you want a specific concern flagged for our next check-in with the customs-facing side of this.
