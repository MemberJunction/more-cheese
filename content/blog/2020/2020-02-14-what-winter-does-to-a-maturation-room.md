Ask a handful of members which season is hardest on their cave, and you'll get more "winter" than "summer" than you'd probably expect. Heat gets the reputation, but a small maturation room built into a converted basement or a repurposed walk-in has to fight a different set of problems once the furnace kicks on for the season, and those problems are exactly what our Maturation Planning for Small Rooms course spends its first sessions on. With coverage heading into this year describing a specialty and artisan segment that keeps expanding, more of our members are running fuller caves than they were a few winters ago, which means less room for a batch to go sideways because a room wasn't holding what it should.

The short version: cold winter air is dry air, and dry air pulled into a heated building gets drier still. A room that held steady at 90 percent relative humidity in October can slide into the 70s by January if nobody's adjusting for it, and most rind development — the washing, the brining, the slow build of the microbial community that actually makes a washed-rind or a natural-rind wheel taste like itself — depends on humidity staying in a fairly narrow band. Drop too far and you get a rind that cracks or dries out before it's had time to develop properly. That's not a subtle problem once it happens, but the drift that causes it can be gradual enough that you don't catch it until you're already looking at a compromised batch.

## Three things worth checking this week

First, if your humidification system runs on a set-it-and-forget-it schedule, this is the week to walk over and actually check the reading against what the dial says it should be. Sensors drift, ultrasonic humidifiers scale up with mineral deposits, and a unit that was calibrated correctly in September can be quietly underperforming by February without throwing any kind of alarm.

Second, think about airflow, not just humidity. Furnace heat rising through a building changes the air currents moving through an attached or basement cave in ways that aren't always intuitive, and a cave that was well-balanced in warmer months can develop a dry spot or a cold spot once the heating season is in full swing. Walking the room with a handheld hygrometer at a few different points, rather than trusting a single fixed sensor, catches this kind of unevenness that a single reading misses.

Third, revisit your turning and washing schedule rather than assuming it still fits. A rind that's drying out faster than usual often needs a shorter interval between washes or a lighter hand when you do turn a wheel, and a schedule built around October's humidity can quietly overwork or underwork a rind once the room has shifted underneath it. A few members told us they simply hadn't recalculated the interval since building the room — worth a look before you assume a slow-developing rind is a recipe problem rather than a room problem.

None of this is exotic. It's mostly attention and a working hygrometer. But we hear from members every winter who assumed their cave would behave the same way it did the rest of the year, and the room told them otherwise a few weeks in. If you want a structured walk through building and maintaining a small maturation room across all four seasons, not just how to survive this one, the current cohort of Maturation Planning for Small Rooms is a good place to start, and we're always happy to point you toward the right course for where your operation actually is.
