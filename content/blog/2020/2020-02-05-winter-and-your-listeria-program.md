Most environmental monitoring programs are built and validated in a warmer, drier month, which means February is exactly when the gap between the plan on paper and the plant on the ground tends to show up. Listeria monocytogenes doesn't take winter off, and a few habits that are harmless in July become real risk factors once the weather turns.

## Where winter changes the picture

Guidance from federal food-safety resources on Listeria control keeps coming back to the same idea: the pathogen persists in wet, cool environments and travels on equipment, footwear, and standing water more than it grows fresh in the product itself. Winter creates more of exactly those conditions. Snow and salt brine tracked in on boots carry organisms straight into zones you've worked hard to keep clean. Propped doors — held open longer than usual while someone hauls in deliveries or firewood for a space heater — let in cold air that condenses on nearby cold surfaces the moment it meets a warmer room, and that condensation is exactly the standing moisture the guidance flags as a harborage risk. Floor drains that ran fine in warmer months can back up more easily when meltwater volume spikes, leaving pooled water in a zone that's supposed to stay dry between cleanings.

None of this means your program is wrong. It means a program calibrated for one set of conditions needs a second look when the conditions change, and most small creameries don't have a formal seasonal-adjustment step built into their monitoring plan. A raw-milk dairy in another state was ordered to stop sales last month after routine testing turned up Listeria, a reminder that winter testing can't be treated as a formality even on an operation with a clean recent history.

A few places worth an extra look this month, on top of your normal swabbing schedule: boot-wash stations at every entrance that actually gets used in winter traffic, not just the main one; floor drains checked for backup after a thaw, not only on the regular cleaning schedule; door seals and propped-door habits during deliveries, especially if your loading area sits close to a Zone 1 or Zone 2 surface; and any space heater or supplemental heat source that's new for the season, since heat sources placed near cold, damp equipment can create the kind of condensation cycle that guidance specifically calls out as a growth-friendly niche.

If your swab results already trend clean, this isn't about assuming a problem exists — it's about not letting a seasonal blind spot go unchecked simply because nothing has gone wrong yet. Environmental monitoring is most useful precisely when it catches a drift before a result does.
