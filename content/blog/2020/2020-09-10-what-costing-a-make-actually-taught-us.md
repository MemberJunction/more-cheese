Tuesday's webinar, Costing a Make 2020, drew close to three times the sign-ups we planned for when we scheduled it back in July. We'd guess that has less to do with the topic being new and more to do with the timing: a summer of a soft block market followed by feed costs that a lot of members are still trying to pin down for fall, on top of a federal margin-coverage report this week that gave a mixed read on where feed and milk prices actually stand relative to each other right now.

The session itself was the one we've run in some form for a few years — walking through a worksheet that turns a make sheet into a real per-pound cost, milk through packaging, including the pieces people usually forget like whey disposal, culture and rennet, and a share of the electricity bill for the cave. But the questions afterward told us more about where members are right now than the slides did.

## What people actually asked

Two questions came up over and over. The first was about how to treat a spike in feed or trucking cost that you expect to be temporary — do you rebuild your price around it, or absorb it and wait it out? We don't have a universal answer, but the presenter's rule of thumb was useful: if you can't say with a straight face that the cost is coming back down within two make cycles, price as if it isn't going to. Waiting on a cost that doesn't come back down just means the loss compounds for another quarter.

The second question was about wholesale accounts that are slow to accept a price increase even when your input costs are documented and dated. There, the advice was blunter: bring the worksheet to the buyer, not just the new number. A price increase with a page of real cost behind it moves faster through a buyer's approval process than one that shows up as an unexplained line-item change on an invoice. A couple of attendees pushed back that even a documented increase gets stonewalled by an account that's squeezed on its own end this year, and the presenter didn't have a clean answer for that — sometimes the honest response is that a buyer relationship needs to change, not just the number on it.

We also had a good number of questions from members who are newer to formal costing altogether, several of whom mentioned they'd priced by instinct for years and only started tracking a real cost per pound this year, once the summer's swings made instinct an expensive way to run a small creamery.

## If you missed it

The worksheet and a recording are available to members through the education portal — look for Costing a Make 2020 under this year's webinars. If you work through the worksheet and get stuck on where a shared cost like rent, a second employee, or refrigeration belongs, the Education Committee has office hours most weeks and will happily look at your numbers with you before you commit to a new price.

We're already collecting topics for the next round of free webinars this fall. If margin, labeling, or a specific credential question is what's keeping you up at night, tell us — that's usually where the next session comes from, and this one is a good example of why that works.
