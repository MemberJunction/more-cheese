This week's spotlight isn't a member with a milestone to celebrate — it's one who spent the week making a call most of us have never had to make. A farmstead creamery in southern Oregon has been inside a level 2 evacuation zone since Tuesday, close enough to an active wildfire nearby that the owner kept a loaded truck parked in the driveway the entire time, and far enough from certainty that she couldn't tell us, when we spoke Thursday, whether Saturday's cave check would happen at all.

The fire itself never reached the property. The smoke did. For four days the air quality outside sat somewhere between "close the windows" and "don't go out," and the harder problem turned out to be indoors: a cave built to hold a steady temperature and humidity doesn't have an easy way to filter what comes in through the vents, and running the exchange less often to keep smoke out meant watching the humidity climb past where she wanted it, on wheels that were already three weeks into an affinage schedule she didn't want to restart from day one.

## What she prioritized, in order

She told us the choice came down to a short list, decided in the first hour of the warning: get the animals somewhere safer first, get four cheeses she'd need to re-culture from scratch if lost labeled and ready to grab, and accept that everything else in the cave would either make it or it wouldn't. You can't evacuate a cave, she said — you can only decide what you're willing to lose and stop thinking about the rest until it's over. That's a harder discipline than it sounds, especially on a second or third night of watching an air-quality map instead of sleeping.

What she didn't do was ignore the risk to the cheese that stayed behind. She adjusted her turning and brushing schedule to the minimum that would keep mold in check without requiring her to open the cave door any more than necessary, logged the humidity swings so she'd know exactly what she was dealing with once the smoke cleared, and made peace with the idea that a batch or two might need to be reclassified as seconds or written off entirely. Neighboring producers in the region have described similar weeks this fire season — homes and herds prioritized first, product decisions made after, often over days rather than hours. As of this week, the evacuation warning near her has downgraded and the worst of the smoke has cleared; she's still waiting to see what four days of elevated humidity did to the wheels closest to the door.
