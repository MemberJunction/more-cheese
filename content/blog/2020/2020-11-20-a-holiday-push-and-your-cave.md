With the commodity cheese price holding its recent gains and retail demand still solid heading into the holidays, several members told us this week they're pushing more wheels into maturation than they have all year. A dairy-commodity update published earlier this month pointed to the same pull: retail cheese demand has stayed strong even as foodservice wavers under renewed restrictions, and that's translating into fuller vats and fuller caves across the industry. That's a good problem to have, but it's also a reminder that a cave behaves differently when it's fuller. Every wheel in the room is respiring, shedding moisture, and shifting the air around it, and a room that held steady at forty wheels doesn't necessarily hold steady at seventy.

The complaint we hear most this time of year isn't a dramatic mold bloom or a cracked rind — it's a slow drift. Humidity readings creep up half a percent a week, airflow that used to reach the back shelf stops getting there, and by the time anyone notices, three weeks of product have matured a little differently than planned. None of that is usually a food-safety problem. It's a consistency problem, and consistency is what a holiday order depends on.

## Reading the room, not just the gauge

A single humidity reading near the door tells you less than members think it does once a cave is packed tighter than usual. Product stacked close together blocks airflow between rows even when the room's overall reading looks fine, which is why a few members walk their cave with a handheld probe during a busy stretch rather than trusting the wall unit alone. If you're adding shelving or reorganizing to fit more wheels this month, it's worth checking readings at a few points, not just the one your equipment was calibrated against.

Turning schedules are the other thing that quietly breaks under volume. A rotation built for forty wheels a week can slip fast once there are seventy to get through, and a missed turn here and there adds up differently depending on the style. Members working with washed-rind or bloomy styles this season have told us they're the ones feeling it first, since those rinds are least forgiving of an inconsistent turn.

Cold air is also lazier than warm air, and a lot of members are running their caves at the coolest point of the year right now, which slows the natural convection that normally helps even out a room. Add extra product blocking the vents and a room that used to self-correct over a day or two can drift for a week before anyone notices the shelf in the back corner is a few points drier than the rest. A small circulating fan, pointed away from the product rather than at it, is a low-cost fix several members have added this month specifically to compensate for a fuller room.

## Where to go before it becomes a pattern

If your cave is behaving in a way you can't quite explain this month, the Cave Management course covers exactly this kind of seasonal load problem — how airflow and humidity respond to product density, and how to adjust a turning schedule without adding hours you don't have during the busiest weeks of your year.
