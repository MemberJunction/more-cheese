We're not naming the member this post is about, at her request — she's still in the middle of the cleanup and didn't want a spotlight on her business while the insurance adjuster hasn't been out yet. But she agreed to let us tell the story, because she thinks other members in the storm's path could use it this week.

She runs a small farmstead creamery in eastern Iowa, a few dozen wheels a week, mostly washed-rind and a young alpine-style that's been building a following at two farmers' markets. Monday's storm took the roof off her machine shed and knocked out power to the whole property. The cave — a converted root cellar she's spent three years dialing in — has no natural humidity control worth trusting on its own in August. Without refrigeration, she had roughly a day before the temperature swing would put weeks of aging cheese at risk.

She told us the strangest part of the first hour wasn't the damage, it was the quiet — no compressor hum, no fans, nothing but wind moving through a barn that no longer had a roof over half of it. She said her first instinct was to open the cave door and check the wheels by hand, the way she used to before she trusted any equipment at all.

## The first 48 hours

Here's what she did, in her words, condensed: she called two other members within twenty minutes of finding a shed roof in the pasture — one who'd offered spare generator capacity back when the federation first put out a call for mutual aid earlier this spring, and one a county over whose cave still had power and room. The first member had a generator big enough to run the cave's compressor but not enough fuel on hand for more than a day; a third member, hearing about it secondhand, showed up that evening with a truck and thirty gallons of diesel he'd been keeping for his own equipment. The cave held at temperature the whole time. Power came back on day nine.

None of that was a federation program. It was three members who know each other from committee calls and an annual conference finding out what everyone had and moving it to where it was needed, in under a day, without anyone asking us to coordinate it. She said the generator ran loud enough that she could hear it from the house, and for nine days that sound meant the difference between a normal fall inventory and a total loss.

By day four, she'd worked out a rhythm: check fuel levels at dawn, check the cave temperature log at noon, and spend the evenings on the phone with her insurance company and with the two members who'd lent her equipment, trading updates on what each of them still needed. She said the hardest part wasn't the mechanics of it, it was not knowing how long "temporary" was going to last.

## What she'd tell you to do first

Her advice, if a similar storm ever comes through your area: know your cave's thermal buffer before you need it — how many hours you actually have before a power loss becomes a spoilage event, not the number on the equipment spec sheet. Keep a written note of it somewhere your family can find without you. And put your name on the outreach list now, not in the middle of a crisis; she'd never used it before Monday and didn't know two of the three members who ended up helping her.

If you came through the derecho's path this week and could use backup cold storage, a generator, or just someone else's hands, let the Membership & Outreach Committee know. That's the list she used, and it's still open.
