Two named storms are sitting in the Gulf of Mexico as we write this, and forecasters are watching them closely enough that a few of our members with distribution contracts along the coast have already started calling around. If you ship or receive product through that corridor this week, this post is for you. If you don't, it's still worth ten minutes, because coastal disruption reaches inland faster than most people expect, and this hasn't been a year short on reminders of that.

## What actually breaks first

It's rarely the cheese itself. Reefer trucks can hold temperature for a while even parked, and most carriers running this corridor have weathered storm season before. What breaks first is the schedule: a distribution center loses power and can't cross-dock, a port closes to inbound freight, a driver who was supposed to make your Thursday delivery gets rerouted around road closures and shows up Saturday with a product that's now three days closer to its sell-by date than your retail partner expected. None of that is anyone's fault, and all of it is avoidable friction if you know it's coming and plan around it instead of being surprised by it midweek.

If you have a standing wholesale account that routes anywhere near the Gulf Coast, this is the week to call your account contact and ask directly whether their fulfillment center sits in a watch or warning area. Don't wait for them to call you — during active storm response, outbound communication from a distribution partner is usually the last thing to get done, not the first, and for good reason. Their people are busy keeping the lights on, not updating vendors.

There's also a human layer worth naming plainly: several coastal counties are under evacuation orders this week, and any member with employees or family in an affected area should treat checking in on people as the first priority, well before checking in on inventory.

## The market backdrop hasn't calmed down either

Separately from the weather, block cheddar has kept sliding since its record run in mid-July, and Class III futures for the rest of the year are pointed lower rather than higher. That's relevant background if you're negotiating a fall wholesale contract right now: a buyer who watched the block price fall through August has more leverage at the table than they did a month ago, and it's worth going in with current numbers rather than July's headlines still ringing in everyone's ears.

We know this is a lot of moving pieces at once — a volatile commodity market layered under storm risk layered under everything else this year has already asked of small producers. The one piece of advice that holds regardless of what happens with either storm: document your shipment dates, temperatures, and any delay communications this week. If a claim needs to be filed with a carrier or an insurer later, the members who kept simple contemporaneous notes — a dated log, timestamped photos of a load before it leaves the dock — are always the ones who get reimbursed faster and with less back-and-forth.

If your operation or your buyers sit in an affected region and you need a hand thinking through contract language or documentation, reach out to the Membership & Outreach Committee. They've fielded these calls before, this year more than most, and can point you to what's actually worked for other members rather than what sounds good in theory.
