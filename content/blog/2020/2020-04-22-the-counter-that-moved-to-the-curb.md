For six years, the whole point of a small cheese shop we work with has been the counter. You come in, you ask a question, someone hands you a sliver on a toothpick and tells you a story about the farm it came from, and you leave with something you didn't plan to buy. That's the model. It doesn't work when the door has to stay shut.

The owner told us she spent the first week of closures fairly convinced the shop wouldn't make it through spring. Wholesale accounts — the two small restaurants she'd been supplying since her second year in business — had gone quiet along with everyone else's. What changed her mind was watching how many regular customers started emailing to ask if she was still open in any form at all. People wanted cheese. They just couldn't come stand at the case for twenty minutes debating between two wheels the way they used to.

## Building a counter you can't stand at

She converted her Saturday tasting flight into a weekly online order form within about ten days, built around three or four fixed selections instead of an open case, because she didn't have the staff to field individual substitution requests on top of everything else. Orders get bagged and set out on a folding table by the door at pickup windows, twenty minutes apart, no browsing. It is a worse version of the experience she spent years building, and she said so plainly when we talked to her. It is also, right now, most of her revenue.

The part she didn't expect: a fair number of her regulars started asking more questions by email than they ever had in person, wanting the same story about the farm and the affinage that used to come with the toothpick. She's been answering those questions in the order confirmation email instead, one paragraph on the week's selections, written the way she'd talk if someone were standing in front of her. Members running counters of their own have told us versions of the same thing — the story-selling didn't disappear with the shop's open sign, it just moved into writing.

## What she'd tell another counter operator

Her advice for members weighing whether to try the same thing: keep the selection small on purpose. She tried a longer list in her second week, and the extra choice slowed down pickup and confused the volunteers helping bag orders. Four options, changed weekly, turned out to move faster and generate fewer questions at the folding table than a wider menu did.

She's also candid that this isn't a plan for growth, it's a plan for surviving a season she didn't choose. Whether any of it sticks once people can browse a case again, she doesn't know, and neither do we. What we can say from what members are reporting is that a fixed weekly selection with real writing behind it is outperforming an open online catalog for shops trying this for the first time — worth knowing if you're deciding where to start.

If you're building something similar and want to compare notes, the Membership & Outreach Committee has started a members-only thread specifically for retail pivots. It's a good place to ask what worked and what didn't before you commit staff time to a system that might not fit your shop.
