Most weeks we spotlight someone whose story involves a stage, a committee, or a competition ribbon. This week's member doesn't have any of those, at least not yet — she's two years into a small goat dairy in the upper Midwest, and until March her entire retail plan was a folding table at two farmers markets a week. We're not naming her creamery here, because her town's market guidelines are still in flux and she asked us to keep the details general, but her situation will be familiar to a lot of you.

When her markets announced they'd reopen for the season but with real changes — no sampling, no browsing, one-way foot traffic, vendors expected to pre-bag orders — her first reaction was that it sounded like more work for less money. Her second reaction, a few days later, was to build a text-order system anyway. Customers send a message the night before, she pre-weighs and wraps their usual wheels and chèvre logs, and pickup on market day takes about ninety seconds a customer instead of the five-minute conversation she used to have at the table.

## What she'd keep and what she wouldn't

She told us the ninety-second pickup line is the part she didn't expect to like. It moves more product per hour than her old setup did, and pre-orders mean she cuts less waste, because she isn't guessing how many logs to bring. What she misses is the conversation — the customer who wanted to talk about rind development, the one who asked what to pair with a fresh chèvre for a dinner party. That's the part of a market table that actually builds repeat customers, and a pre-order pickup line doesn't replace it on its own, so she's started adding a card with each order: a tasting note, a pairing suggestion, sometimes a line about what's different in this batch. It's a small thing, and she says it's the closest she can get to that market-table conversation right now.

The harder adjustment has been cash flow. A markets-only business collects money in person, often the same day; a pre-order system means she's committing ingredients and labor before she knows for certain who's picking up. She's started asking for a deposit on standing weekly orders, something she'd never have considered charging regulars before this year, and reports that nobody's pushed back.

## The part we want other members to hear

If you're running a small direct-to-consumer operation and you've built something similar — a pre-order system, a delivery route, a subscription box — we'd like to hear about it. The Membership & Outreach Committee is putting together a short practical guide for members navigating this kind of retail pivot, and real examples from working members are more useful to us than anything we could write from a desk. If your local market rules have changed and you're not sure what's actually required versus what's a market's own house policy, that's a good question to bring to your local health department directly rather than guess — we can point you toward general resources, but market operating rules vary by jurisdiction and we won't get those details wrong on your behalf.

Two years in, a folding table, and a text-order system that didn't exist eight weeks ago. It's not the retail plan she wrote when she started the creamery, but she says it's the one that's keeping the lights on this spring.
