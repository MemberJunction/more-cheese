Every retail member we've talked to this month has said some version of the same thing: January is quiet. Holiday case turnover is behind them, gift boxes are cleared out, and the walk-in traffic that carried December has thinned to a trickle on weekday afternoons. We spent some time this week with one of our retail members — a counter manager at a mid-sized cheese shop who asked not to be named but was happy to talk shop — about what a slow January actually looks like from behind the case.

"The mistake people make is treating January like a problem to survive," she told us. "It's actually the one stretch of the year where you have time to fix things you never get to fix in November." For her shop, that means recutting the case layout, rewriting shelf-talkers that have gone stale, and working through a backlog of supplier tastings that got pushed aside during the holiday rush.

## Where the real traffic shows up

The slow afternoons aren't where her shop makes its January money — the evening classes are. Her counter runs a monthly tasting class, and she said the surprising thing wasn't that people showed up in January, it was when they bought the most cheese: after the class ends, not during it. "Eight to nine at night is our busiest hour some weeks, which sounds backwards until you've watched it happen. People taste six cheeses, decide they liked three of them, and buy all three to take home."

That pattern tracks with something we've heard from more than one retail member: classes rarely turn a profit on their own, especially early on, but they build a different kind of customer — one who comes back monthly instead of showing up twice a year for a gift box. A few members have told us they priced early classes below cost just to get people in the door, treating the loss as a marketing expense rather than a line item that needs to pencil out immediately.

## What she's doing differently this year

This January, she's also spending more time than usual on supplier relationships, in part because the wholesale side of the business has been unusually active even while retail traffic is soft. National specialty food sales grew to nearly $149 billion over the most recent reporting period, outpacing the broader food and beverage market by a wide margin, and she says that growth shows up as steadier wholesale ordering even in a month when foot traffic at the counter doesn't reflect it. "You can't let the slow afternoons fool you into thinking demand is soft," she said. "It's just moved to a different part of the week, and honestly a different part of the business. People are buying cheese to cook with in January more than they're buying it to gift."

That distinction has changed what she stocks for the month. Gift-forward items — the wrapped wedges, the seasonal pairings — get pulled back from the front case in favor of cooking cheeses: a grating parmesan-style wheel, a melting cheese for winter gratins, the kind of block that doesn't need a story attached to sell. It's a smaller, plainer case for a few weeks, and she's fine with that. "The case doesn't have to work as hard in January. It just has to be honest about what people actually need right now."
