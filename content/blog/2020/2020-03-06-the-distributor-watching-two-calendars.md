Marta Vellinger runs logistics for Prairie Wind Refrigerated Transit, a mid-sized carrier that moves cheese and other perishables between about a dozen creameries and their wholesale accounts across three states. Most weeks, her job is a fairly predictable puzzle of routes, temperature logs, and delivery windows. This spring she's found herself watching two calendars at once: the normal one, full of spring wholesale orders building toward summer farmers'-market season, and a newer one, tracking which of her retail accounts are starting to ask cautious questions about supply.

"Nobody's cancelling anything," she told us. "But I've had three buyers this month ask me some version of 'what happens if a driver gets sick,' which is not a question anyone asked me in January." She's quick to say she doesn't think the sky is falling — milk is still moving, trucks are still running on schedule, and her creameries haven't reported any disruption to production. But she's started doing something she calls "boring on purpose": overcommunicating basic status updates to accounts who haven't asked for them, so that if something does change later, nobody's finding out for the first time in a moment of stress.

## What a distributor actually sees first

Small producers sometimes assume a distributor's main value is the truck. Vellinger says the more useful thing Prairie Wind offers right now is visibility across a dozen operations at once — if one creamery's delivery slips, she often knows about a supply hiccup before the retailer does, and she can flag it early instead of letting a buyer find out when a shipment simply doesn't show. "A distributor who calls you before you notice a problem is worth more than one who explains after," she said. "That's true in a normal year. It matters more when everyone's a little jumpy."

Her practical advice to the small producers she works with hasn't changed much: keep your own inventory and cold-chain records current, tell your distributor early if a batch is running behind schedule rather than late, and resist the urge to overcorrect based on headlines rather than what's actually happening at your own plant. "Your cave doesn't know what's on the news," she said. "Keep making good cheese and keep your paperwork current. That's the same advice in March as it is every other month — it just matters a little more when people are anxious."

## A note for members shipping wholesale this spring

If you distribute through a third-party carrier and haven't talked recently about contingency plans — what happens if a driver is out, what a delayed pickup does to a delivery window — this is a reasonable month to have that conversation, not because anything is wrong yet, but because a five-minute call now is easier than a scramble later. The Membership & Outreach Committee has a short template for a producer-distributor check-in on the member portal if you want a starting point.

Vellinger's read on the broader market backs up her instinct to stay calm and specific. The latest federal dairy outlook update had already trimmed the 2020 milk price forecast even before this week, with dairy futures softening on trade uncertainty rather than anything happening in a cave or a truck — not a crisis picture, just a tight one. "Margins were already thin walking into spring," she said. "That's a milk-price problem, not a virus problem, and it's been true since before anybody was asking me about drivers. I'd rather members worry about the thing that's actually pressuring their margin than the thing that's just loud in the news this week."

Her other habit worth borrowing: she keeps a one-page status note for each account, updated weekly, that says plainly what's normal and what isn't. "It takes ten minutes and it means when someone calls me anxious, I have an answer instead of a guess," she said. "Most of what people are actually afraid of is not knowing. You can't fix the outbreak. You can fix not knowing."
