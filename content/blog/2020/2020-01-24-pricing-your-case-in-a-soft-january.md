Every January brings a dip in milk prices after the holiday selling season ends, but this year's drop has been sharper than usual. Class III futures came in well over two dollars below December's level, with the decline traced mostly to softer cheese prices on the wholesale market. For members whose cost base runs off milk price rather than a fixed ingredient contract, that's the kind of swing that changes a margin conversation overnight.

The instinct in a soft month is to hold retail and wholesale prices steady and wait it out, on the theory that customers notice a price change more than they notice a quiet stretch of stable pricing. That's often the right call for a short dip. It's a worse call when the dip follows a pattern — and January-to-February softness after a strong holiday season has shown up often enough in recent years that it's worth planning for rather than reacting to each time it happens.

## What actually moves in your cost structure

For a cheesemaker selling direct or through a small distributor, the milk-price swing usually shows up fastest in wholesale contracts that reprice on a monthly or quarterly formula. If your pricing to distributors is tied to a published milk price index, you may already be seeing the adjustment flow through automatically. If it isn't tied to an index — if your wholesale price was set once and hasn't moved in a while — this is a reasonable month to look at whether it should.

Retail pricing is a different conversation, and a slower one. A retail case price that moves with every milk-price swing reads as erratic to a customer, and most members we've talked to hold retail steady through routine seasonal dips, reserving a price change for a sustained shift that looks likely to last more than a quarter. The distinction that matters is between noise and signal — and telling the two apart is harder than it sounds when you're the one living inside the numbers every week.

## Where to go deeper

This is exactly the territory the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course covers, and a new cohort is opening for enrollment now. It walks through building a real cost model — milk, labor, aging loss, packaging, and distribution — and then applying it to both wholesale and retail pricing decisions, including how to decide when a cost shift is worth passing through and when it's cheaper to absorb it for a season. Past participants have told us the most useful part isn't the formulas so much as the discipline of tracking the inputs consistently enough that a month like this one doesn't feel like a surprise.

Either way, a soft January is a better time to check your assumptions than a strong one — the math is more forgiving when the pressure is already showing up somewhere in the numbers.
