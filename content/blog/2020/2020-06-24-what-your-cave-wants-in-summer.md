Most aged cheese wants somewhere in the range of 50 to 55 degrees and high humidity, usually 80 to 95 percent depending on style, held steady week to week. That's a manageable ask in October. It's a much harder one in late June, when a small maturation room built into a converted basement or a repurposed walk-in is fighting an outside temperature that's twenty or thirty degrees higher than what's inside, all day, for weeks at a stretch.

This is the time of year we hear from members whose cave has started drifting — a degree or two warmer than usual, humidity swinging more than it should between refrigeration cycles, condensation showing up somewhere it didn't in the spring. None of that is unusual for a small system working harder than it's sized for, but small drifts compound. A cave running a couple of degrees warm all summer doesn't ruin a batch outright; it just quietly speeds up ripening and can shift a rind's development away from what you're used to seeing, which is its own kind of problem if you're aging for a specific style profile or a specific delivery date.

## What actually helps at small scale

The most useful fix is usually the least exciting one: reduce how often the door opens and how long it stays open, since every open door is a heat and humidity swing your system then has to correct. If your cave sees a lot of daily traffic for turning and monitoring, batching that into fewer, faster visits instead of frequent quick ones can measurably steady the numbers. Checking your door seals and insulation is worth doing before you assume the compressor needs attention — a system that's cycling constantly to fight a slow leak around the door frame will struggle no matter how well-sized the unit is.

Humidity is usually the harder half of the problem in summer, because a cooling system that's working overtime tends to pull moisture out of the air along with heat, leaving a cave that's cold enough but drier than a washed-rind or bloomy style wants. A humidifier sized for the space, or something as simple as damp cloths or a shallow water tray depending on your scale, can close that gap — but it needs monitoring, since a cave that's both cold and over-humidified in an attempt to compensate invites its own mold problems.

## Where the course comes in

This is exactly the kind of judgment call our Affinage Fundamentals course spends real time on: not just what the target numbers are, but how to read your own space's specific quirks and respond to a slow drift before it becomes a pattern across a whole batch. If you're maturing anything beyond a handful of wheels at a time and haven't taken it yet, summer is a reasonable moment to — a lot of what the course covers is easier to absorb when you're actively watching your own numbers move.
