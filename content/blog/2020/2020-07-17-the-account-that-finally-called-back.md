Every membership organization has a version of this account: the dues clear every year, the renewal is automatic, and nobody on staff can quite remember the last time someone from the company actually called. We have a few of those too. This spring, one of them called us.

The membership belongs to a mid-size distributor that's carried an ICF corporate membership for close to a decade, mostly as a line item their purchasing team renews without much thought. We don't say that as a complaint — quiet, reliable dues are still dues, and we're glad to have them. But when their regional account manager reached out in April, it wasn't a routine question. She wanted to know if the federation was coordinating anything around moving surplus cheese to food banks, because her company had a warehouse of product built for a restaurant channel that had mostly stopped ordering.

## What changed

We'd been fielding versions of that question all spring from smaller producers, but this was the first corporate member to ask it, and the scale was different — pallets, not wheels. We connected her with two of the regional food-bank distribution efforts our members had already been running informally, and within about three weeks her company had moved several truckloads of product that would otherwise have sat in cold storage depreciating toward a total write-off.

What struck our membership team wasn't the volume, though that mattered. It was that nobody at the company had asked for anything back — no press mention, no discount on next year's dues, no request that we feature them by name. The account manager told us she'd been meaning to get more involved with the federation for a while and just hadn't had a reason that felt urgent enough. This, unfortunately, qualified.

We're telling this story without naming the company, because that's how they asked us to handle it, and because the shape of the story matters more than the name attached to it. A lot of corporate memberships in every trade association look identical from the outside — steady, low-contact, easy to take for granted on both sides. This spring reminded us that the contact information behind that renewal is a real person who might pick up the phone if the reason is good enough.

If your organization holds a corporate membership and has capacity sitting idle — warehouse space, surplus product, a logistics team with slack in its schedule — the Membership & Outreach Committee is still coordinating matches between supply and need. You don't have to wait for us to call first.

## The broader pattern

This account isn't alone in what it ran into this spring. Across the dairy supply chain, the same mismatch showed up at every scale: product built for restaurants and school cafeterias sitting in cold storage while food-bank lines grew in the same regions. Trucking and distribution partners around the country moved hundreds of thousands of gallons of milk and thousands of pounds of cheese into donation channels over the same stretch of months, mostly through exactly the kind of informal, phone-call-first coordination our member described. None of it was elegant. Most of it depended on someone picking up the phone before the product aged out of usefulness.

We think that's worth naming plainly, because it says something about what a trade association is actually for in a year like this one. The federation doesn't own warehouses or trucks. What we have is a membership list and a habit of knowing who has capacity and who has need in the same week. That's a smaller thing than it sounds, and this spring it turned out to matter more than we would have guessed in January.

The surge in cases across the South and West this month makes it likely that restaurant and food-service ordering stays depressed longer than anyone hoped back in May. If your account, corporate or otherwise, is sitting on inventory that isn't moving through its usual channel, say something sooner rather than later. The account manager who called us in April told us her only regret was not calling in March. We'd rather hear from you a month early than not at all.
