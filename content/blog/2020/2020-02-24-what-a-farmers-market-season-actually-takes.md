If you sell at a farmers' market from May through October, February is when the season actually gets decided. Market applications close soon in a lot of regions, stall fees get set, and the producers who wait until April to think about staffing usually spend the whole summer catching up. We've had enough members ask us the same handful of questions this month that it seemed worth writing down.

Recent research into specialty-cheese distribution backs up what our members tell us anecdotally: a large majority of small producers sell through farmers' markets alongside restaurants and wholesale accounts, and for many farmstead operations it's the channel that actually pays for the rest of the business. That's a good reason to plan it properly instead of treating it as a folding table and whatever didn't sell wholesale that week.

## The three decisions that actually matter

Our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course is built around three planning questions, and members who skip the first two tend to regret it by July. First: who is staffing the table, and are they briefed to talk about the cheese, not just make change. A market customer asking about a washed-rind wheel wants a sentence or two about the cave it came out of, not a shrug. Second: how is market pricing set relative to your wholesale accounts. Undercutting your own retail partners at a market ten minutes from their store creates a problem you'll be explaining to a buyer later, and it's one of the most common mistakes we see from members in their first season or two.

Third, and the one people plan for least: what happens to the product that doesn't sell. A market table in July heat is not a controlled environment, and a wheel that's been sitting out for six hours needs a plan before it goes back in the cooler, not after. The course spends real time on this because it's a food-safety question wearing a merchandising costume, and it's exactly the kind of thing that gets missed when a market booth is staffed by whoever was free that Saturday.

## Building a season, not a table

Members who've run a successful market program for a few years describe it less as "showing up on Saturday" and more as a small seasonal business with its own margin math. That means knowing your per-market breakeven before the season starts, not backing into it in September. It also means treating the relationships you build at the table — a regular customer, a chef who buys off your stall instead of calling a distributor — as worth cultivating deliberately, since they tend to be the accounts that stick around longest.

For members newer to direct sales, the course also covers the permitting differences between a produce stand and a table selling perishable dairy, which vary more by jurisdiction than people expect and are worth confirming well before your first Saturday, not the week of.

If you've been meaning to formalize a market program that's been running on instinct, or you're planning your first season this year, the course runs through the member portal, and past sessions are archived there for anyone who can't make a live date. The Membership & Outreach Committee can also connect you with members in your region who've already worked out the local permitting questions specific to your state.
