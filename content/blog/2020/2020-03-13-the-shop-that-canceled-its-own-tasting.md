Most weeks, this feature covers a maker or a longtime buyer. This week it's a decision — one small retail member made this Tuesday, before any local order required it, and one we suspect a lot of shop owners are quietly weighing right now.

The counter manager at a member-owned specialty cheese shop, who asked us not to use her name or her storefront in this post, called her monthly evening tasting off three days before it was scheduled. It's a standing event: a dozen or so regulars, four or five wheels open on the counter, a light pour of something to go with them. "It's the smallest possible version of a public gathering," she told us, "and this was the week it stopped feeling small." She'd watched two much larger trade events get pulled from the calendar entirely over the past several days, and decided her own event didn't need to wait for a mandate to make the same call.

She's not alone. Members across our retail rolls have told us this week that they're rethinking anything on the calendar that brings a crowd into a small space, even when nothing in their state requires it yet. A few are postponing rather than canceling outright, hoping a spring date six or eight weeks out will feel different than this one does. Others are converting an in-person event into something smaller and quieter — an appointment-only tasting, a curbside pickup window — rather than dropping the idea entirely.

## What the shop lost, and what it didn't

The immediate cost was real. A tasting like hers typically moves a case or two of the featured wheels that wouldn't otherwise turn that fast, plus whatever regulars pick up on their way out the door. Cutting it cold, with three days' notice, meant eating that bump for the month. She also had to refund the handful of people who'd pre-paid for a seat, which her point-of-sale system doesn't make especially graceful.

What she didn't lose was the relationship. She emailed the list herself instead of letting the cancellation email do the talking, told regulars exactly why she'd made the call, and offered a version of the same wheels at a discount for pickup instead. About half the RSVP list came in over the following two days to buy anyway. "People didn't want the party," she said. "They still wanted the cheese, and they wanted to feel like someone was being straight with them about why the party wasn't happening."

## What we'd tell other members weighing the same call

If you run a retail counter or host any kind of public event — a tasting, a class, an open house — and you're wondering whether it's premature to cancel, her experience suggests the reputational cost of canceling early is a lot smaller than the cost of being the shop that didn't. Communicate the reason plainly, keep the retail offer open even without the gathering, and don't be surprised if more of your list shows up for the transaction than you expected once the pressure of a public event is off the table.

We don't know yet whether this becomes the shape of the next few months or a one-week blip. Either way, if your shop makes a similar call, tell us about it — these are exactly the kinds of small, practical decisions our Membership & Outreach Committee wants to hear about while they're happening, not after the fact.
