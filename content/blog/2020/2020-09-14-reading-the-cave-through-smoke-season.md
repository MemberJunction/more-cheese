A member who runs a small farmstead creamery in the foothills wrote to us last week with a subject
line that just said "still here." Her cave had gone through a planned utility power shutoff timed
to a wind event and a stretch of smoke-hazed skies, and every wheel in the room came through fine.
We asked her how, and the answer had less to do with luck than with a habit she built years before
any of this started.

This has been the roughest wildfire season on record across the West, and utilities have been
cutting power ahead of high-wind conditions as a precaution rather than waiting to react to a fire
already burning. For a cave, a planned outage and an unplanned one cost the same thing: hours
without active cooling. Members in fire-prone territory have spent September watching two numbers
at once — the air quality index outside and the humidity gauge on the cave wall — and only one of
those is something a member can actually manage.

## What she checked before the lights went out

Her creamery had already lived through one shorter outage earlier in the year, and she used that
as a dry run rather than a scare to forget about. Before this month's longer shutoff, she had a
generator sized to hold the cave — not the whole building, just the cave — at its target
temperature and humidity for as long as the outage might reasonably run. She also pulled her
oldest, most humidity-sensitive wheels to the coolest shelf ahead of time, on the theory that if
anything drifted, it should drift on the cheeses that could take it best.

"You don't want to be doing math on the fly when the power's already out," she told us. "I wanted
to know the number before I needed it." That's a smaller lesson than it sounds like: know your
cave's thermal buffer — how many hours it holds a safe range with no active cooling — before
you're relying on it in the dark.

## The part smoke actually touches

Smoke's real effect on a farmstead operation this season hasn't been the cave; it's been the
pasture and the herd. Members with grazing animals have had to bring cows onto stored feed earlier
than planned when smoke sat low over pasture for days at a time, which shows up later as a milk
supply question, not a cheese-quality one. That's a separate problem from the cave-during-outage
one, and it deserves its own planning rather than getting folded into "the smoke was bad this
year" as a single memory.

She's back to a normal make schedule now, and the wheels that rode out the outage are aging on
schedule. Her advice to other members in fire country wasn't complicated: write down the generator
math, the fuel supply you'd need, and the shelf order you'd move things in, before the first watch
notice arrives, not during it. If your own contingency plan is still a mental note rather than a
written one, this is a good week to put it on paper while the last outage is still fresh enough to
learn from.

We're glad to have her wheels — and her — back on a normal schedule, and we'll keep checking in
with members in the path of this season as it runs its course.
