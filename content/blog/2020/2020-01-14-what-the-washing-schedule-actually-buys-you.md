Washed-Curd & Dutch Styles wrapped its Willow cohort last week, five weeks after it started back in December, and a few members from the class have already stopped by the office to talk about what stuck. The recurring theme wasn't the recipe itself — most people taking a course like this already know roughly what a Gouda or Edam is supposed to taste like. It was the washing schedule, and how much a small change to it shifts the finished wheel.

## Two different kinds of washing, two different jobs

It's worth separating the two things people mean when they say "washing," because members regularly conflate them. Curd washing happens during the make: replacing some of the whey with warm water partway through the process, which pulls lactose out of the curd and lowers the acidity of the final cheese. That's what gives washed-curd styles their characteristically sweet, mild flavor and dense, springy paste. Rind washing happens weeks later, during aging: a brine or cultured wash applied to the surface of the wheel on a regular interval, which shapes the rind's color, moisture, and the bacterial community that develops on it. Get the two confused and you'll troubleshoot the wrong step when a batch doesn't come out right.

## Consistency matters more than any single ratio

Ask five experienced Dutch-style makers what water-to-curd ratio they use for curd washing and you'll get five different answers, all defensible. What the course spent more time on, deliberately, is the discipline of doing the same wash on the same schedule every time — roughly every two to three days during the active rind-washing period, using a consistent brine strength around the low single digits by weight. Members who'd been inconsistent about timing, sometimes stretching four days between washes when the week got busy, reported the most noticeable improvement once they moved to a fixed calendar reminder instead of doing it "when there's time." A rind that gets washed on an irregular schedule dries out between treatments and then gets soaked again, and that stop-start cycle is a common, under-diagnosed cause of cracking and uneven color members had been blaming on their milk or their cave.

The instructor also pushed back on a habit a few members admitted to: reusing the same brine solution for months without checking or refreshing it. A working brine picks up whey proteins and fat from every wheel it touches, and over time that buildup changes how it behaves against the rind, sometimes encouraging surface growth nobody intended. The course's rule of thumb was to test the brine's salt concentration periodically with a simple hydrometer and to replace or top it up on a fixed schedule rather than waiting until a wheel comes out looking wrong. It's a small habit, but several members said it was the piece of advice from the whole five weeks they were most likely to actually change once they got home.

None of this is to say the make-day ratio is irrelevant — how much whey you replace with water, and at what temperature, still sets the baseline sweetness and moisture of the finished paste, and that part is genuinely style-specific. The course's point was narrower: most members troubleshooting an inconsistent batch were chasing the wrong variable. They'd adjust the water ratio on the next make when the actual problem was that the rind treatment two weeks later had drifted off schedule. Separating "what happens in the vat" from "what happens on the shelf" turned out to be the single clearest organizing idea of the five weeks, according to more than one member who came by to talk about it afterward.

If you make washed-curd or Dutch-style cheese and missed this cohort, the course runs again later in the year, and the syllabus is on the education page in the meantime. For members who want to keep the conversation going before the next session, a few Willow cohort graduates have said they're happy to compare notes — ask the Education Committee for an introduction if that's useful to you, and if your own washing schedule has drifted over a busy season, this is as good a week as any to write a new one down and stick it somewhere you'll actually see it.
