We've had a steady run of questions about the Certified Cheese Professional (CCP) credential this month, mostly from members who've been in the industry a few years and are trying to decide whether it's worth the study time. Short answer: for the right role, yes. Longer answer follows.

CCP is the federation's broadest credential, and it's built that way on purpose. Where the Cheese Foundations Certificate covers entry-level basics and the Advanced Affinage Certificate goes deep on one specialty, CCP is meant to test whether someone can move across the whole industry — retail counter, wholesale account, distribution, and production — and still speak the same language as everyone else in the room. It draws questions from production and make process, from affinage and storage, from pairing and service, and from the business side: labeling rules, shelf life, and how a specialty case actually turns a profit.

## Who it's actually for

The credential tends to matter most for people whose job already spans more than one part of the business — a counter manager who also does purchasing, a distributor rep who trains retail accounts, an aspiring shop owner building credibility before opening. If your day-to-day is narrow and deep — you make one style of cheese, in one cave, for one label — the Advanced Affinage Certificate or a course tied directly to your craft will usually serve you better than the broader CCP track. Neither is more prestigious than the other. They're built for different jobs.

Growth in the specialty and artisan segment over the last several years has been part of what's driving interest. As the category has expanded, the businesses in it have gotten more varied — bigger case sets, more direct-to-consumer sales, more staff who need a shared vocabulary fast — and a broad, portable credential does real work in that environment. It's also become a common line item on the resumes we see for retail management roles, which is worth knowing if you're the one hiring.

## How to actually prepare

The most common mistake we see is treating CCP prep like a memorization exercise — flashcards on regions and styles, without touching the production and business sections that make up a real share of the material. The exam draws from all of it, and the sections candidates skip studying are usually the ones that sink them. If you've been in retail your whole career, budget real study time for the make process and food-safety material. If you came up on the production side, do the same for pairing, service, and case management.

The food-safety section is worth taking especially seriously right now. A study published this month on how pathogens behave in an unpasteurized aged cheese during storage was a reminder that "food safety" isn't a box to check once and forget — it's a moving body of knowledge, and a counter manager who can explain why a facility's testing and aging practices matter is a better advocate for the products on their shelf than one who can only recite a region and a milk type.

Cohorts for the underlying prep coursework run a few times a year, and instructors report that candidates who study in a small group — trading practice questions, talking through the sections they're each weakest on — tend to do noticeably better than solo studiers. If you're weighing whether to sign up, that's worth factoring in as much as the material itself. Reach out to the Education Committee if you want to be matched with a study group before the next testing window opens.
