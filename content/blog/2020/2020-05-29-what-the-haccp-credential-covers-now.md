Our Education Committee flagged something this week worth passing along: enrollment in the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate is running well above where it usually sits for late spring, and it's not close. Some of that is members who had a conference-season credential on their calendar for the summer and shifted to finishing it online instead. Some of it is newer members using a quieter production stretch — federal dairy market reports have been tracking soft food-service order books straight through spring — to get a credential they'd been putting off. Either way, we've had enough questions about what the certificate actually covers that it's worth spelling out plainly.

## What HACCP is and isn't

HACCP, hazard analysis and critical control points, is a framework for identifying the specific points in your make and aging process where something could go wrong biologically, chemically, or physically, and putting a documented control in place at each one. It is not a food-safety plan you buy off a shelf and drop your name into. The certificate walks you through building your own hazard analysis from your actual process, your actual cave, and your actual equipment, because a control point for a bloomy-rind maker with an open-air cave looks nothing like one for a washed-curd operation running enclosed aging rooms.

The certificate covers five pieces: identifying hazards specific to your process, setting critical limits you can actually measure, building a monitoring routine your team will realistically follow, writing corrective actions for when a limit is missed, and keeping records that would hold up if a regulator or an auditor asked to see them. None of it is abstract. The coursework has you build the plan for your own operation as you go, so what you finish with is a working document, not a certificate to frame.

A question we've fielded a few times this spring: does finishing the certificate mean a facility is automatically compliant with every applicable food-safety rule? No. The certificate teaches you the framework and helps you document a plan specific to your operation; it isn't a substitute for whatever inspection or licensing your state or your product category requires. Members sometimes assume the two are the same thing, and we'd rather correct that early than have someone find out otherwise during an audit.

## Finishing it without leaving home

The certificate has always had a self-paced coursework component, but the final piece, a plan review with an instructor, used to happen in person at a workshop or the annual conference. This spring we moved that review online, over video, with the same instructors going through the same checklist they'd use in a room. Members who've been through both formats tell us the online review actually runs a little longer, because there's less pressure to keep a room on schedule, and a few said they got more detailed feedback on their monitoring routine as a result.

The other change worth mentioning is scheduling. In a normal year, the plan review slots fill up around our spring and summer events, since a lot of members like to combine the review with a trip they're already making. Without that travel this year, reviews have spread out more evenly across the calendar, which has actually shortened the wait for some members. If you'd been putting off registering because the review slot you wanted was months out, it's worth checking again — a few instructors have added evening sessions to keep up with the enrollment jump, and availability is better than it's been in a while.

If you started this certificate earlier in the year and stalled out when your production calendar got busy, or if you're a newer member weighing whether now is the time, the Education Committee can walk you through where you'd pick up and what the remote review actually involves before you commit the hours.
