A member who runs a small farmstead operation in the upper Midwest told us this week that her farm stand looked nothing like it did in March, and nothing like it did in July either. What started this spring as a folding table with a cash box has become a weekly pickup route with a text-message order list, a second chest freezer, and about sixty households on a rotating schedule. "I stopped thinking of it as an emergency measure sometime around September," she said. "It's just how we sell cheese now."

Her restaurant accounts, which made up nearly half her revenue at the start of the year, have been unreliable since spring and are thinning out again as dining rooms cut capacity or close outright in her state. Rather than wait out a recovery that keeps stalling, she's spent the fall building the direct list instead — asking regular customers to refer neighbors, adding a second pickup evening, and finally investing in the freezer capacity to hold a bigger buffer of aged wheels between pickups.

## What changed after the first freezer filled up

The turning point, she said, wasn't a single big order — it was realizing her direct customers were more predictable than her wholesale ones had ever been. A grocery account might cut an order with a week's notice if foot traffic dropped; her direct list rarely does, because people are ordering cheese for their own table, not a case they're hoping to sell through. That predictability let her plan her make schedule around known demand for the first time since she started the business, rather than guessing at a wholesale order that might change.

She's also more deliberate now about which wheels go where. Anything destined for the direct list gets cut and wrapped to a size that makes sense for a household, while anything still going wholesale stays in bigger formats for the accounts that can still move it. It's more labor at the cutting board, she admits, but it means fewer wheels sitting half-sold in either direction.

She's watched other channels shift around her, too. A federal program that pays for boxes of food, including dairy, for distribution through food banks and pantries opened a new purchase round at the end of October, with deliveries running into the end of the year. She's not selling into that program directly — her volume is too small for the kind of contract it requires — but she's noticed a couple of larger producers nearby step up production to fill it, which has occasionally freed up cave and cold-storage space she's been able to rent short-term when her own freezers run tight before a big pickup week.

## What she'd tell a member starting from scratch

Her advice to members considering the same move: start smaller than you think you need to, and be honest with customers about what a pickup route can and can't do. She turned down expanding into full home delivery this fall, deciding the labor cost didn't pencil out against the marginal households it would add. Members exploring a similar shift can find a structured version of what she built through the Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course, which walks through order-list logistics, pickup scheduling, and the pricing questions that come with selling smaller cuts direct rather than wholesale by the wheel.

She's not against wholesale returning to what it was. She just isn't planning around it anymore, and after this week's headlines about another round of dining restrictions, she doesn't sound like she's in a hurry to.

What strikes us most about her story is how ordinary it's become. A year ago, a sixty-household pickup route with a waiting list would have been an unusual arrangement for a small farmstead producer to describe to us. This fall, it's one of several similar accounts we've heard from members in different states, each built a little differently but landing on the same idea: sell direct, hold a buffer, and stop waiting for wholesale to decide your production schedule for you. If your own direct list is somewhere between a folding table and a full route, the Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course is a reasonable next stop, and we're always glad to hear how it's going for you.
