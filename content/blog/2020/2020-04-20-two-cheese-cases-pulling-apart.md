Two things are true this month, and they don't agree with each other. Farm milk prices have fallen to levels most members have never priced against, some producers report figures below fourteen dollars per hundredweight. And at the same time, cheese and butter are piling up in coolers with nowhere to go, because the accounts that used to move that inventory — restaurants, school cafeterias, hotel breakfast lines — mostly closed at once.

That's the split members have been describing to us for three weeks running: retail cheese counters are busier than they've been in years, while the wholesale and food-service side of the same supply chain has fallen off a cliff. A creamery selling into both channels can watch one line of its business boom and the other collapse in the same quarter, and neither number tells you the whole story on its own.

## Why the cooler is full

Cheese doesn't spoil the way fresh milk does, so when demand disappears faster than a plant can shift its output, the milk goes into cheese and the cheese goes into cold storage rather than getting dumped. That's the mechanical reason members are hearing about elevated cheese and butter stocks even as farm prices are historically weak — production kept running on contracts and habits built for a food-service world that stopped ordering almost overnight. Some processors have asked farms to pull back volume by ten percent or more, which is not a request any of our members enjoy making or receiving.

For a small creamery selling wheels directly off the farm, this shows up differently: your own case might be fuller than usual because the tasting room or the farmers market crowd increased, but your wholesale accounts have gone quiet, and your distributor is telling you they're overstocked on categories you don't even sell into. Both halves are part of the same national picture.

What relief funding is actually meant to touch

Federal disaster relief passed at the end of March includes a dedicated pool for producers hurt by the pandemic, and dairy is explicitly named in it. The mechanics are still being worked out at the department level as of this week, and we don't have confirmed payment formulas to share yet — when we do, it'll come through the Advocacy Committee's channel, not a guess from us. What we can say is that the relief is aimed at farm-level and producer-level losses tied to the collapse in food-service demand, not at retail-side businesses that are currently busier than usual. If your operation sells wholesale and lost that channel, this is worth watching closely. If your operation sells mostly direct and is having a decent April, it's probably not for you, and that's fine.

What we'd tell a member asking "should I worry"

If you're a small producer with a strong local or online direct channel right now, keep doing what's working and don't assume it will last at this pace once retail habits normalize. If you're a producer or distributor whose business runs mostly through food service or wholesale, this is the moment to talk to your buyers about what a partial reopening might look like for them, because nobody has a clean answer yet and the ones who ask early will get better terms than the ones who wait.

We'll keep tracking both sides of this split as the month goes on. If your numbers don't match what we're describing here — better or worse — tell us. The picture we're building for the Advocacy Committee's federal comments is only as good as what members actually report.
