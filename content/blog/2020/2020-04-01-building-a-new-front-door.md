A member wrote in this week describing a scene a lot of you will recognize: a walk-in that's
fuller than it's ever been, a wholesale account that stopped answering emails, and a farmers'
market that just got canceled for the second Saturday running. Selling direct to the household
that would normally buy your cheese at a restaurant or a market stall has gone, in the space of
three weeks, from a nice-to-have side channel to the whole plan for some of you. It is not a
seamless swap, and it's worth being honest about what it actually takes.

## The parts that are harder than they look

Curbside and mail order both sound simple until you're doing them. A curbside pickup line needs a
posted schedule, a way to take payment without a lot of hand-to-hand contact, and a system so an
order placed by phone or email actually matches what gets handed out the window — mixing up two
family orders on your busiest day does more damage to a new customer relationship than a slow
line does. Shipping cheese is its own craft: insulated packaging, ice packs sized to your
transit time, and a real answer for what happens if a box sits on a porch in an 80-degree
afternoon. None of that is hard to learn. It is hard to improvise for the first time under
pressure, which is exactly the position a lot of members are in right now.

## Pricing the channel, not just the cheese

Direct sales usually carry a better margin per pound than wholesale, but they also carry costs
wholesale never charged you — packaging, shipping, and the labor of running a pickup line or
packing a mail order box. A price that worked at the farmers' market table doesn't automatically
work once you've added a $9 insulated box and next-day shipping. Members who've made this switch
well tend to treat it as pricing a new product, not moving an old one to a new counter.

Our **Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets** course covers the practical build: setting up an
order system that won't fall over on your first busy weekend, packaging that survives a porch
delivery, and a pricing worksheet that accounts for the costs a wholesale account used to absorb
for you. If you've never sold direct before, start there rather than guessing your way through a
first week of mail orders.

If direct sales are new to your operation, keep the first version simple. A weekly order window,
a short fixed menu, and pickup at a set time beats a sprawling online store you don't have the
staff to run. You can always add complexity once the basics are holding. Several members who
already had some direct channel in place before this month told us the same thing: the version
that worked was the boring, reliable one, not the ambitious one.
