Federal health officials closed out a separate listeria investigation this week tied to ready-to-eat deli meat, a reminder that environmental monitoring gaps show up across ready-to-eat categories, not just cheese, whenever a facility's volume outruns its sampling plan. Listeria Control in Small Creameries started its Willow cohort earlier this month, and the timing wasn't an accident on our end. Holiday production is the highest-volume stretch of the year for most of our members, which means more batches moving through the same drains, the same coolers, and the same aging rooms in a shorter window than any other time of year. That's exactly the condition under which a small, manageable environmental monitoring program starts to slip if nobody's watching it closely.

The course itself is built around a plain premise: most listeria problems in small creameries aren't caused by a single bad batch, they're caused by an environment that was never mapped in the first place, so nobody knows where to swab when volume goes up and time gets short. Members in the current cohort have spent the first two weeks building an actual floor plan of their own facility — drains, floor drains near aging racks, condensation points, the mat by the back door that never quite dries in winter — before touching a single test result.

## Why the holiday crunch matters here

A few members who took this course in an earlier cohort told us the real value showed up months later, during exactly this kind of volume spike. One put it this way: "I didn't think about my swab map differently until we were running double batches through the same room in November, and suddenly the corner I'd been treating as low-risk all year was getting foot traffic three times a day." Her fix wasn't dramatic — she added it to her rotating swab schedule and moved a rack six inches — but she wouldn't have caught it without a map built ahead of the rush rather than during it.

If you're not enrolled in the current cohort, you can still take the same starting exercise this week on your own: walk your facility at your busiest hour of the day, not your quietest, and note every spot where moisture collects, product sits, or people and carts pass close to aging or packaging areas. Holiday volume is exactly the stress test that reveals whether your existing environmental monitoring plan matches how your space is actually being used right now, versus how it was used in a slower month.

## What the course covers if you're weighing the next cohort

Beyond the mapping exercise, the course walks through building a rotating swab schedule sized to a small facility's actual budget and lab-turnaround reality, reading environmental results in context rather than reacting to a single positive in isolation, and documenting corrective actions in a way that holds up if a buyer or auditor asks to see your program. None of it assumes a large-plant budget or a dedicated quality role — it's built for the member wearing the food safety hat alongside three or four others.

The Willow cohort is partway through, but the course runs again with new intake windows through the year. If holiday volume has you rethinking your own environmental program, the Food Safety Committee can point you toward the next opening, and toward members like the one quoted above who are glad to talk through what changed for them.
