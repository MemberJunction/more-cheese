If your bulk tank has been sampled twice in the last month instead of once, you are not
imagining it. The national milk-supply surveillance program that started rolling out state
by state late last year has kept expanding, and this week several more milk-producing states
moved from voluntary to required testing. For members running their own herd, or buying raw
milk from someone who does, this is worth five minutes with your recordkeeping before it
becomes a scramble.

The testing itself is simple: a sample from the bulk tank or silo, screened before
pasteurization, with results shared back to the herd owner and the state. It is not a
judgment on your operation and it does not slow your make schedule. What it does change is
paperwork. If your farm holds records under a food-safety plan already — and if you carry our
Food Safety & HACCP Certificate, you do — add one line to your monitoring log noting when a
sample was drawn and by whom. Auditors like a paper trail more than they like an explanation.

The rollout has been uneven by design rather than by accident. States with larger dairy herds
and denser processing infrastructure were brought in first, on the theory that a positive
signal there would be caught faster and would matter to more milk. Smaller dairy states have
followed on a rolling basis, which is exactly why two neighboring farms can be on different
schedules this month. If you supply a cooperative that spans more than one state, don't assume
your obligations match your neighbor's — ask your cooperative's compliance contact directly
which tier your specific plant falls under.

## What we are hearing from members

A few farmstead producers in the upper Midwest told us their bigger worry isn't the testing
itself, it's uneven rollout — a neighboring state fully enrolled, their own state a few weeks
behind, and a transporter who has to keep two sets of expectations straight. We don't have a
fix for that beyond saying it plainly to your inspector and your milk hauler: ask what your
state's enrollment status is this week, not last month's, because it has been changing fast.

Others have asked us whether a positive bulk-tank result would show up publicly in a way that
could affect a farm's reputation with buyers. It's a fair question and not one we can answer on
your behalf — that depends on your state's public-records rules and your own supply
agreements — but it's worth reading your buyer contracts now, before a result exists, rather
than after.

Meanwhile the broader milk market is doing what late winter usually does — production edging
up, all-milk prices easing off their fall highs, and margins staying comfortable mostly because
feed costs have behaved themselves. None of that is dramatic, but paired with a farmstead sector
that's already watching every line item this year, a quiet month is a good month. If your
margin coverage elections are due for a look before spring, now is the moment, not April,
since enrollment windows for that program close well before the season gets busy.

## If you make from raw milk

For members doing raw-milk aging under state exemption, the testing order doesn't change your
underlying legal requirements — those are set at the state level and vary widely, so we are not
going to tell you what you may or may not do here. What we can tell you is what the rule
requires procedurally: if a request for a bulk sample comes to your farm, cooperate with it and
log it the same way you'd log a routine inspection. Treat it as one more thing a recall
investigation would ask for later, and you'll never regret having it on paper.

If you want a second set of eyes on your monitoring log before your next inspection, the Food
Safety Committee takes informal questions through the member portal, and our Food Safety &
HACCP Certificate course walks through recall-readiness documentation from the ground up.
