Thursday's workshop, Cave Farm Shop Retail, has quietly become one of the more requested sessions on this year's calendar, and the waitlist tells us why: a lot of members are sitting on unused square footage next to the make room and wondering whether it should be a store.

The pull makes sense. Direct-to-consumer and agritourism sales have been growing faster than wholesale for most of the small producers we talk to, and the appeal is obvious: no distributor cut, no case-fee negotiation, and a customer standing in front of your cave instead of a line item on someone else's truck. National farm-sales data backs up what members are telling us anecdotally — direct sales and on-site visits have grown into a meaningful line of revenue for small producers over the past few years, not a side hustle, and the trend keeps compounding rather than leveling off.

## It starts with what you can't sell there

The workshop opens with the boring part on purpose: a farm shop is still a retail food establishment, and that means a different inspection category, different labeling requirements for anything repackaged on-site, and in some states a separate license from your production permit. Facilitators will walk through the most common mistake they see, which is treating the shop as an extension of the cave rather than as its own regulated space with its own paperwork. Get that part wrong and the shop becomes the thing that flags an otherwise clean inspection — a frustrating outcome for a member whose actual cheesemaking has never had a finding against it.

From there it moves into the parts members came for: case layout for a room that's open maybe twelve hours a week instead of a supermarket's eighty, pricing when your only comparison is your own wholesale sheet, and how to handle the tasting counter without turning it into an unlicensed sampling operation. Specialty and artisan varieties have kept outperforming plainer cheese categories at retail this year, which is exactly the kind of case a small farm shop is positioned to build. There's a short section on seasonal traffic, aimed squarely at members whose shop only really earns its keep from a fall market bump through the holidays, which is most of the room.

Facilitators are also setting aside time for a walkthrough of what happens when a farm shop starts pulling foot traffic beyond what the cave's parking and refrigeration were designed for — a good problem, but still a problem, and one several members have already run into without planning for it.

## If you're still deciding whether to go

You don't need to have broken ground on a storefront to get something out of Thursday's session. Several members registered are still at the "is this worth it" stage, and the facilitators are planning to spend real time on the numbers: what square footage, staffing and insurance a small shop actually costs before it sells a single wedge, set against what direct sales tend to return once it's running.

The workshop is open through Thursday morning registration if you haven't signed up yet. If you can't make the live session, recordings go to the Events Committee's library within a week, and the accompanying checklist gets posted alongside it for anyone weighing the decision on their own timeline.
