A farmstead member in the upper Midwest, running twenty-two milking cows and a small aging room she built herself six years ago, told us this week that her September ledger looks "confusing in a way I didn't expect." Her milk check finally moved up after a rough stretch. Her grain bill also moved up, later and faster than she'd planned for. She's not sure yet whether that nets out to relief or just a different flavor of worry.

That's a fair place to sit. This has been a hard stretch for small operations — feed and utility costs have squeezed farmstead creameries all through the closures we've been tracking this year, and more than a few members have told us they're making decisions month to month rather than season to season. So when one number finally moves in the right direction, it's tempting to treat it as the turnaround. She's not doing that, and it's worth explaining why.

## Reading the ledger sideways, not just down

Her approach this fall has been to track the two numbers separately instead of netting them into one relief figure. The milk-price recovery is real, and she's grateful for it, but she watched a feed quote she'd been counting on get revised upward twice in three weeks after a regional crop reassessment moved grain prices. "I almost treated the better milk check as money I already had," she told us. "Then the feed quote came back higher than the one I'd budgeted from, and I realized the two things had basically canceled each other out. I'm not further behind, but I'm not further ahead either."

Instead of committing the milk-price gain to anything new, she's holding her retail pricing steady through the fall and putting any actual surplus toward a repair on her aging room's humidity system that's been on a list since spring — a fix, not a raise, and one she can afford only because she didn't spend the recovery twice.

## What she's telling other members

Her advice to other farmstead operators watching the same two lines: don't let one good number talk you out of caution on the other, and don't lock in a feed contract off a quote that's more than a couple of weeks old. She's also started keeping a simple two-column note in her ledger, feed cost and milk price, side by side, specifically so she doesn't average them into a feeling instead of a decision. "If I only look at the checkbook balance," she said, "I can't tell which of the two moved. I need to see them separately to know what actually changed."

She's also started asking her feed supplier for a written quote with a short hold period rather than a verbal number, after being caught by a price that moved between the phone call and the delivery. It's a small procedural change, but it's the kind of thing that only becomes obvious after a season like this one.

If you're weighing a similar call this fall, the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with other farmstead members working through the same math, and it's worth a look at your own dairy risk coverage before you commit any gain to something you can't undo.
