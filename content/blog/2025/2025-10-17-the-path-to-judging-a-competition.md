If you caught Wednesday's Judging Standards webinar, or heard about it secondhand from a colleague who did, you may have come away with one clear takeaway: judging a federation competition is not something you sign up for the week before entries close. It's a credential path with a real prerequisite, and it's worth laying out plainly for anyone who's curious rather than leaving it as something people only piece together after they've already applied and gotten confused.

The destination is Competition Judge Accreditation, which authorizes a member to sit on a judging panel for federation competitions. It's a two-year credential, which surprises people who assume accreditation is a one-time achievement you earn and then keep forever. It isn't. Panels change, styles trend, and a judge who hasn't been active on a panel recently is asked to requalify rather than coast on a scorecard from a couple of competition cycles back.

## The route runs through sensory evaluation

You don't start at accreditation, and there's no shortcut around the step before it. The usual on-ramp is the Sensory Evaluation Certificate, which covers structured tasting, defect identification, and scoring against style standards — the shared vocabulary and discipline that make one judge's scores comparable to another's instead of just a collection of personal opinions that happen to sit on the same table. It's a three-year credential in its own right, and useful well beyond judging: plenty of buyers and quality managers hold it purely to standardize how they evaluate incoming product before it ever reaches a competition table.

From there, accreditation adds supervised panel experience on top of the certificate. That's the part people consistently underestimate. Reading about off-flavors in a course module and identifying them correctly under time pressure, seated next to two or three other judges whose scores you don't get to compare against until the round is over, are genuinely different skills. The supervised hours exist because that gap is real, not because the federation enjoys paperwork for its own sake.

## Why this matters more this year

Interest in judging has been climbing alongside the broader specialty cheese market, which has kept growing even as some conventional categories have softened. Deli specialty cheese sales are up meaningfully this year by most industry tracking, and Hispanic and Italian cheese styles in particular have been standouts, alongside continued strength in goat's milk offerings — helped along by a fall milk supply that's been running seasonally strong, which gives more makers more finished wheels to actually enter. More entries at competitions, across more categories, means more demand for qualified panels — and a credential that lapses without active use means the pool of eligible judges doesn't grow on its own just because entries do.

If you sat through Wednesday's webinar and want to move toward accreditation, the Sensory Evaluation Certificate is where that starts, and enrollment windows for it open a few times a year. If you already hold the certificate and haven't judged in a while, now is a reasonable time to ask the Awards & Competition Committee what supervised slots are coming up before the next competition cycle fills them — those slots tend to go faster than members expect once entry season gets closer.
