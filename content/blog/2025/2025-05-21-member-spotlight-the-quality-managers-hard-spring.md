One of our members has been quality manager at Fernholt Creamery for several years, a job that in a normal season means make records, sanitation logs, and a running argument with whichever cave is running warm. This spring her job has meant something a little different: sitting in on cost conversations she used to be handed the outcome of, not asked into.

Fernholt is a farmstead operation, milking its own small herd and turning most of it into cheese on site, which means feed and energy costs hit the creamery from two directions at once — the barn and the make room both got more expensive at the same time. She says the ownership started looping her into pricing discussions this year in a way they hadn't before. "I used to just be told what the new price point was and figure out how to hit it," she told us. "Now I'm in the room when they're deciding whether there's a price point that still works at all."

## What changes when quality has a seat at that table

It's not a comfortable seat. She says the instinct in a tight year is to look for savings on the production side first — trim a testing frequency, stretch a cleaning cycle, skip a step that's never once caught a problem. She's held the line against most of that, not because the savings wouldn't help, but because she doesn't think a farmstead creamery this size survives a bad batch it can trace back to a corner it cut under pressure. "The math on cutting quality never actually works out the way people hope it will," she said. "It just moves the cost to later and makes it bigger."

Where she has found room to help is less dramatic: renegotiating a testing lab's turnaround schedule to reduce a courier fee, batching sanitation supply orders with a neighboring creamery to hit a volume discount neither could reach alone, and adjusting the make schedule to reduce a utility peak-rate window that was costing more than anyone had noticed. None of it closes the gap the feed bill opened. All of it helps, and she says the habit of hunting for small savings has stuck even on the weeks when the numbers finally look a little steadier.

## What she'd tell another quality manager right now

Asked what she'd say to a peer at another small creamery watching the same numbers, she didn't offer a pep talk. "I'd say get into the pricing conversation before you're asked, because by the time you're asked it's usually already decided," she said. "And write down every place you found a saving that wasn't a quality shortcut — you'll need that list again."

If your own creamery is weighing where costs can move without moving where quality standards sit, our Cave Management and Milk Quality & Herd Health coursework both touch on operating efficiently without cutting the corners that come back to bite you later. And if you've found your own version of the supply-batching trick above, the Membership & Outreach Committee would like to hear about it — small efficiencies are worth more shared than kept quiet this year.
