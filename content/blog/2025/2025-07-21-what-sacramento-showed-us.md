Six days out from the ICF Annual Conference in Sacramento, and the badges have finally come off. If you were there, thank you for showing up in what has been, by most accounts, a hard year to leave the farm or the shop for three days. If you weren't, here's the version of the week we think is worth carrying home even secondhand.

The tone was different from last year, and most members we talked to noticed it without needing us to point it out. Two years of an artisan boom gave the conference a certain buoyancy — new entrants, bigger booths, a competition floor that kept expanding. This year felt steadier and more careful. Attendance held up better than some of us expected given how many small operations are watching every travel dollar right now, but the conversations in the hallway leaned practical: margin, not menu. People wanted to talk about what's actually working, not what's next.

## The sessions members kept coming back to

The best-attended breakout wasn't the flashy one. It was a working session on reading your own cost structure against this year's milk price swings — Class III and Class IV prices have been drifting toward each other for months, which changes the math for anyone whose contract leans on the spread between them. A room that was supposed to hold sixty people ended up standing three deep along the back wall, which tells you something about where members' attention actually is right now.

The competition floor was, as always, the emotional center of the event. This year's entries leaned harder into washed-curd and blue styles than we've seen in a while, and the judges' table had more than one quiet argument about a wheel that was almost, but not quite, there. If you entered and didn't place, several judges asked us to pass along that the notes matter more than the ribbon — read them, don't just file them.

Heat was a running theme even away from the herd-health sessions. A number of members mentioned adjusting their own travel and shipping plans around this summer's stretch of extreme temperatures and the strain it's put on regional power grids in a few parts of the country — cooling and refrigeration costs came up in more casual conversations than we expected for a conference that's nominally about cheese, not electricity bills.

A handful of members flagged that the conference schedule keeps landing in the same mid-July window every year, which is exactly when several regions are dealing with their worst heat and their tightest cash flow. We don't set next year's dates lightly, and we don't have an answer yet, but the Events Committee has heard this from enough members now that it's on their list for real discussion, not just a note in a file.

We also heard, more than once, some version of "I almost didn't come." Travel costs, coverage at home, a summer that's already stretched thin — this wasn't a complaint so much as an honest accounting of what it took to show up. If that's your story too, we'd genuinely like to hear it; it shapes how we plan. If you picked up a session handout you haven't had time to read, most of this year's materials are now posted to the member portal under the conference archive. And if Sacramento reminded you that your own cost tracking could use an overhaul, the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course is exactly the follow-up several attendees asked us for — a short, practical companion to the working session that packed the room.
