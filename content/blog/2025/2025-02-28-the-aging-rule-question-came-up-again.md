Raw-milk aging rules are one of those topics that never fully closes. Every few years a handful
of states revisit how they handle unpasteurized cheese — minimum aging periods, testing
frequency, labeling — and this year is shaping up to be one of those years. One state legislature
advanced a bill this month broadening farm-direct raw milk sales, with a governor's signature on
similar bills following soon after, and more states are expected to take up their own versions
before sessions end.

For members who age under the standard sixty-day rule, none of this changes your compliance
picture today. But state-level movement tends to shift the national conversation, and it's worth
understanding where things stand before it does. The activity so far this year has mostly been at
the state level, not federal — legislatures updating who can sell raw milk directly to consumers,
under what testing regime, and with what labeling. That's a different question from aging rules
for raw-milk cheese, but the two get tangled together in public conversation more often than they
should. A member selling raw-milk cheddar aged past the federal minimum is operating under a
settled rule; a state loosening farm-direct raw fluid milk sales doesn't touch that rule at all.
We've fielded enough confused questions on this from members and from local reporters covering the
topic that it seemed worth spelling out plainly.

Where it does matter to producers: state agriculture departments in a few places have signaled
they're reviewing testing and recordkeeping requirements alongside the sales-law changes, and a
requirement written for a farm selling fluid milk can sometimes get copied, awkwardly, into
language that also touches cheese. That's the piece the Food Safety Committee is watching most
closely — not because the aging standard itself is at risk, but because adjacent rulemaking has a
way of picking up unintended riders.

## What ICF is doing

The Food Safety Committee has been compiling member accounts of how state-level reviews are
playing out on the ground — which is exactly the kind of detail that's useful when a rulemaking
comment period opens and needs specifics, not generalities. If you've had a conversation with a
state inspector this year that touched on aging, testing frequency, or raw-milk labeling, the
committee wants to hear about it, plain description, no need to interpret it for us.

We're also keeping the advocacy tracker on the member portal current with open comment periods as
they appear. Testimony and letter campaigns on raw-milk aging rules have moved policy before —
not by volume alone, but because a producer describing their actual testing routine carries more
weight with a regulator than a form letter does.

## If you want to weigh in

Two ways to get involved without much lift: submit a short account of your own testing and
labeling practice to the Food Safety Committee for its files, or watch the advocacy tracker for a
comment period in your state and file testimony directly when one opens. Neither commits you to
anything beyond describing what you already do. If you're not sure whether a specific state
proposal touches your operation, the committee can help you read it — that's a faster path than
guessing from a news summary.
