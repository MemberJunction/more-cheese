One of our members has run the cheese counter at a mid-sized specialty grocer for six years, and until this spring she'd never bothered with a formal credential. She knew her case cold — what paired with what, which wheels needed a week's rest before cutting, which regulars wanted a recommendation and which just wanted their usual pound of the same cheddar. Then a new store director asked, not unkindly, whether anyone behind the counter held a certification the shop could put on its website. She signed up for the Certified Cheesemonger track that week.

She's now about two months out from her exam, and she told us the studying has been harder than she expected — not because the material is unfamiliar, but because finding an hour a day for it has been close to impossible. Specialty cheese has kept growing as a share of what her store sells this year, which is good for the business and good for her case, but it's also meant more special orders, more detailed customer questions on the floor, and less of the quiet stretch she'd planned to spend with her study notes after close.

## What the exam actually asks for

The Certified Cheesemonger credential leans harder on structured knowledge than most people expect going in — production methods across categories, proper storage and handling temperatures, allergen and labeling basics, and a tasting component that asks candidates to identify defects, not just describe flavor. She said the labeling section surprised her the most. "I know how to talk about a cheese," she told us. "I didn't necessarily know the rule behind why the label has to say what it says." That's a fair gap for the credential to close, and it's exactly the kind of thing a customer never asks about until there's a problem.

There's also more paperwork behind a cheese counter than there used to be. Recordkeeping expectations tied to broader food-traceability rules have been phasing in gradually across the supply chain, and even where a specific requirement doesn't land squarely on a retail counter, the habit of knowing where a wheel came from and how it got there is becoming part of the job description rather than a specialty skill. She said the credential coursework was the first time anyone walked her through why that record matters, not just that she's supposed to keep it.

Her approach, for what it's worth, has been to fold studying into her shift rather than compete with it — running through a category's defects during a slow hour instead of saving everything for the evening, and using real questions from customers that day as her review material. It's not a method the course requires. It's just the one that fit around a job that doesn't slow down enough to leave a clean hour free.

## If you're on your own credential timeline

If you're working toward the Certified Cheesemonger or any of our other credentials this fall, you're not the only one squeezing it around a full shift — that's the norm, not the exception, for most of our members. The certification team can talk through pacing if the standard timeline doesn't fit your schedule, and several regional chapters run informal study meetups before the exam windows open. Ask your rep whether one's happening near you, and bring a genuinely difficult wheel to the next one. It makes for better practice than flashcards alone.
