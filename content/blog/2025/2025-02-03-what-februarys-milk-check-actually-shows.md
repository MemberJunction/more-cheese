Mailbox milk prices for February came in a little softer than January's, and the national dairy margin-coverage numbers are getting talked about again on the calls we've been fielding this week. Neither of those sentences is unusual for this time of year. What's worth your attention is the gap underneath them, because the national averages are telling a smoother story than a lot of our farmstead members are actually living.

## The number on the milk check

Federal order mailbox prices for reporting areas averaged a bit lower this February than they did in January, though still comfortably ahead of where they sat a year ago. Cheese prices have actually firmed up some this month on thinner stockpiles, even as butter and a few other dairy products softened — a split that's easy to miss if you're only watching one line of the report. If you sell most of your milk fluid or into a co-op pool, this is background noise. If your creamery is buying milk from a neighbor, or pricing your own herd's output against it, it's the number everyone in the barn is watching.

Global analysis heading into this year has flagged the same tension from the other direction: producers across the major exporting regions are expecting production gains this year on decent profitability, but whether global demand actually absorbs that growth without pushing prices down is an open question, with softer Chinese import demand cited as one reason it might not. That's not a crisis for domestic producers by itself, but it's one more sign that supply isn't the tight side of this market right now. The pressure most of our members are describing isn't a shortage story. It's a margin story.

The national feed-cost outlook for the year is genuinely better than it's been — corn and soybean meal projections are down from where they sat twelve months ago, and some of the federal dairy forecasting leans on that to call 2025 a steadier year for margins overall. That's real, and it matters. It's also a national average, and the program built to smooth out margin swings for smaller operations measures national feed costs, not the cost of the hay and corn silage you actually grew or bought locally this winter. A farm running mostly on homegrown feed doesn't see that national number move the way a market analyst does, and several of you have told us exactly that over the past month.

## Why this is the era it is

We've been careful all year about not overstating how widespread creamery distress actually is — most of our members are managing, and plenty are doing well. But the federal government's downward revision to its own milk production forecast this winter, driven by the smallest replacement-heifer inventory in decades, is a real signal of tighter supply ahead. It's consistent with what several farmstead members have told us directly: herd expansion doesn't look attractive right now, even with softer feed costs on the table, because the capital and labor it takes are harder to justify than they were three years ago. That's not a crisis. It's a slower, more cautious posture, and it's the posture we'd encourage anyone weighing a herd or cave expansion this spring to take seriously before committing to it.

There's also a quieter structural point worth naming: proposed changes to how federal milk marketing orders calculate class prices are still working their way through the system, and even modest adjustments there can move the number on a mailbox check by real cents per hundredweight. None of that is dramatic on its own. Stacked together — softer milk prices, a tighter herd outlook, and pricing formulas in flux — it's enough to explain why so many farmstead conversations this winter sound more careful than they did two years ago, even with the feed-cost relief working in members' favor.

If you're deciding whether this is a year to expand, hold steady, or scale back, the Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding a steady stream of these questions and can point you to members who've made each of those calls recently. You don't have to guess alone from a national average that may not describe your operation at all.
