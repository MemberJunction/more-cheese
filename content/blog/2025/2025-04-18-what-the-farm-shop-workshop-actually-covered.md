Wednesday's workshop on farm shop retail filled its room in Saint-Hyacinthe, and the crowd wasn't quite who we expected. We'd pictured mostly members already running a farm store wanting to sharpen it. Instead, close to half the room was still deciding whether to open one at all — a question that's landed differently this year than it might have two years ago, with feed and energy costs pushing more small producers to look hard at every revenue line they aren't currently using.

## The math nobody wants to do out loud

The session's most useful stretch wasn't about merchandising or signage. It was a blunt exercise in what a farm store actually costs to staff, once you count the hours it pulls away from the cave or the herd rather than just the hours it takes to ring up a sale. The instructor walked the room through a simple framing: a farm store is a second small business bolted onto your first one, and it needs its own honest accounting rather than a rounding error folded into the creamery's books.

For members already running a shop, that reframing landed as validation — several told us afterward that separating the two sets of books for the first time last year was the single most clarifying thing they'd done. For members still on the fence, the instructor's case for even considering a store came back to the same pressure driving the rest of this year's programming: milk price forecasts have been revised down this spring and feed costs still have room to move against a farmstead's margin, so a second, controllable revenue line looks more attractive than it did when wholesale alone covered the bills.

Nobody left with a clean yes or no. Most left with a better list of questions to bring home, and a clearer sense of which of those questions only their own books can answer.

## What to do with a farm store you already have

For the half of the room already running a storefront, the second half of the session covered seasonal case planning and the specific challenge of a farm store that's a destination rather than a walk-by: how to build repeat traffic when your customer has to decide to drive to you rather than pass you on a sidewalk. The consensus in the room, more than any single tactic, was that a farm store's real advantage over a retail cheese shop in town is the story it can tell in person — the cave, the herd, the maker standing behind the counter — and that the stores doing best lean into that rather than trying to compete on convenience or price.

We don't currently have plans to run this session virtually, but the Events Committee tracks which in-person workshops draw enough repeat interest to justify a second offering, and farm retail has come up before. If you'd have wanted a seat and didn't get one, let the committee know — that's exactly the signal that turns into next year's calendar. The next stop on the spring workshop circuit lands later this month, covering costing for prairie-region producers, for members who want the numbers side without the retail layer on top.
