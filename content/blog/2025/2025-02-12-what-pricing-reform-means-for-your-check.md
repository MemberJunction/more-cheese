Most weeks we're writing about your case or your cave. This week we're writing about the number
on your milk check, because it's about to move for reasons that have nothing to do with your
herd or your make schedule.

A round of changes to the federal formulas that set milk-pricing classes takes effect this year,
and the early modeling isn't gentle. Forecasters have been trimming their all-milk price
projections since January, with more of the revision landing on Class III than on the rest of
the pool. If your creamery buys milk under a Class III-priced contract — and most cheesemaking
members do — that's the number to watch on your supplier invoices this spring, not the headline
all-milk figure in the trade press.

The spot market gives you a sense of the direction, even if your own contract doesn't price off
it directly. Cheese held roughly steady through the first week of this month before climbing on
this week's session, while butter has been drifting lower and milk futures have edged up despite
the cost pressure producers are describing. None of that is a forecast for your specific
contract. It's a reminder that the market underneath the formula is moving in more than one
direction at once, which is exactly the kind of week where a documented before-and-after matters
more than a gut sense of "prices feel higher."

## Why we're asking you to write this down

Advocacy under "Dairy pricing reform" has been one of the busier lines in our member action log
for two years running, and it usually spikes after a formula change actually lands rather than
before. That's backwards. The comment record on the *next* round of adjustments is built from
what members can show happened this time — a documented before-and-after on your milk cost per
hundredweight is worth more to a rulemaking file than a general complaint that pricing feels
unfair.

If you buy raw milk, pull your last twelve invoices now, before this quarter's changes are fully
baked into the number, and set them aside with a note of your class and component pricing. If you
ship milk, do the same with your settlement statements. We're not asking anyone to become a
policy analyst overnight — we're asking you to keep the receipts, because six months from now
nobody will remember the exact number without them.

Feed costs are easing a little this year, which is real relief and worth saying plainly. It
doesn't offset a pricing-formula shift for members already running thin, and it's exactly the
kind of "good news, but" year where the two effects get confused in casual conversation. Keep
them separate in your own numbers.

## What we're doing with it

The federation doesn't lobby on your behalf in the sense of picking a side in the formula debate
— that's not our role, and members hold genuinely different positions depending on whether they
buy or ship milk. What we do is aggregate the documented impact members send us and make sure it
reaches the comment record when a window opens, properly anonymized and organized by region and
operation size. That's only useful if the underlying numbers are good.

If you want a hand setting up a simple tracking sheet for your milk cost or your component
pricing, email the federation office and we'll send you the template our advocacy volunteers use.
It takes about twenty minutes to fill in for the first time and five minutes a month after that.

We'll also be flagging the next open comment window here as soon as we have a date, along with a
plain-language explainer of what's actually changing in the formula — not the trade-press
shorthand version, the version that tells you which line on your settlement statement to watch.
Until then, the most useful thing any member can do is keep this month's invoices somewhere you
can find them in six months, whether or not you plan to write a word of testimony yourself.
