She asked us not to use her name or her company's, but she was glad to talk through the decision: a regional cheese distributor covering a three-state territory, a federation member for nine years, who merged two of her delivery routes into one this spring after running the numbers on fuel and refrigerated-truck costs one too many times.

"I'd been putting it off for a year," she told us. "Two drivers, two trucks, two sets of accounts that each thought they had a dedicated route. Combining them meant some of my longest-standing customers had to move to a different delivery day, and I dreaded those calls more than almost anything else I've done this year."

## Why the math finally won

Running two half-full trucks instead of one full one had made sense when her territory was smaller and fuel was cheaper. This year, with diesel and refrigeration costs both running high enough to eat into margins she used to count on, the inefficiency stopped being something she could absorb quietly. She spent a month mapping every stop on both routes by mileage and delivery window before deciding which accounts could shift days without disrupting their own operations.

"I didn't want to just draw a line down the middle of the map," she said. "A shop that gets a wholesale delivery on Tuesday because that's when they restock their case can't suddenly get it on Thursday without their own plan changing. I called every account before I moved them, not after."

That legwork mattered. Of the roughly thirty accounts affected by the merge, she says only two pushed back hard enough that she adjusted the new route to keep their original day. Everyone else, she believes, appreciated hearing about the change from her directly instead of finding out when a truck showed up on the wrong afternoon.

The timing added one more wrinkle she hadn't planned for: a federal labeling-claim rule was set to take a new effective date at the end of the month, and a couple of her accounts asked, in the same call where she explained the route change, whether their case cards would need updating too. She didn't have a firm answer on the spot, which she says bothered her more than the scheduling questions did. "I'm supposed to be the one who knows these things before my accounts ask," she said. "I called our Standards Committee contact the same afternoon so I wouldn't be caught flat next time."

## What she'd tell another distributor weighing the same call

Her advice to anyone else eyeing a similar consolidation this year is blunt: do the mileage math before you do anything else, and don't assume your regulars will be upset by less frequent service if the alternative is less reliable service.

"A lot of my accounts were more worried about whether I'd still show up on time than about which day I showed up," she said. "Once I could promise the new route was actually more dependable than the old one — because one full truck breaks down less often than two half-empty ones, if I'm honest — most of the resistance went away."

She's kept every account she had before the merge, and says the combined route has actually improved her on-time delivery rate, a number she now tracks closely enough to bring up unprompted. She's also started reviewing her fuel and refrigeration costs monthly instead of quarterly, a habit she picked up out of necessity this year and expects to keep even if prices ease. "I used to check those numbers when I had time," she said. "Now I make time, because the quarter I skip is probably the quarter something moved enough to matter."

If you're a member weighing a similar consolidation, our Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with distributors who've been through the same recalculation this year. And if fuel or refrigeration costs are the piece of your budget you're least sure how to plan around, that's a conversation worth having before your next route runs itself into the red.
