This week's spotlight is a composite rather than one member, because a version of the same story has landed with more than one of our members this year: a founding cheesemaker retires or steps back, and the person who's spent years as the assistant is suddenly the one making every call in the cave.

One member we talked to had six seasons behind her as an assistant before her creamery's founder retired this spring, earlier than the plan they'd sketched out together a year ago. "We had a three-year handoff mapped out," she said. "Then her knee gave her no choice, and we did it in four months instead."

## What actually transfers, and what doesn't

The industry conversation around succession tends to focus on the financial mechanics — who buys whom out, over what timeline, at what terms — and that part matters enormously for the members going through an ownership change. But the members we spoke with this year who inherited a make program rather than a business talked less about money and more about a different kind of transfer: the accumulated judgment calls a longtime maker carries and rarely writes down, because after twenty years she doesn't need to.

"She could tell a batch was off by smell before the pH meter confirmed it," one assistant-turned-head-cheesemaker told us. "I can't do that yet. I'm doing it by the numbers this year, which is slower and occasionally wrong in ways she never was. I'm trying to be honest with myself about the gap instead of pretending I've already closed it."

What did transfer cleanly, more than one member said, was the relationship side of the job: knowing which distributor rep to call first when a wholesale account has a problem, which regular customers care about consistency above all else, and which experiments the business can actually afford to run. Those habits, learned by watching rather than by doing, turned out to be more portable than the sensory judgment that takes years to build independently.

Money still matters underneath all of this, even for members who inherited a role rather than a mortgage. A wholesale buyer doesn't automatically extend a new head cheesemaker the same pricing patience a founder earned over twenty years, and more than one member told us their first hard conversation in the new role was renegotiating a term that had gone unquestioned for a decade. "Nobody hands you the goodwill along with the title," one said. "You rebuild it wheel by wheel, account by account."

## The part nobody warns you about

More than one member described an adjustment they hadn't expected: being the one whose name is now attached to every wheel, after years of a founder's reputation absorbing both credit and blame. "Customers ask for her by name still," one said. "I don't take it personally. But it means every wheel that goes out with my hands on it is also quietly asking people to trust someone new, and I feel that every single day in a way I didn't expect to."

If you're navigating a similar transition — stepping into a role a mentor is leaving, on any timeline, planned or not — the Education Committee maintains guidance on documenting institutional knowledge before it walks out the door, and several members who've been through this recently have told us they'd be glad to talk it through with someone earlier in the process than they got to.
