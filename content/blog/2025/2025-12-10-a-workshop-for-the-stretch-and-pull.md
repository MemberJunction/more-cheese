Today's stretched-curd workshop is running in person, and the timing is better than we planned it. Burrata was up almost 20 percent in volume this year, mozzarella and Parmesan both grew too, and Italian-style varieties are named as one of the two categories actually driving deli specialty cheese growth, alongside a strong run for Hispanic cheese styles like queso fresco and cotija. If you've been meaning to add a stretched-curd line to your own case, this was the year the data started backing that instinct up.

It's also been a year when food-safety scrutiny across the dairy world has been unusually heavy — raw-milk research alone has kept several of our committees busy since spring. None of that touches pasteurized stretched-curd cheese directly, but it's part of why we built today's session around careful, hands-on technique rather than a quick demo. A new product line launched under pressure to move fast is exactly the kind of thing that skips a step it shouldn't.

Pasta filata is a deceptively simple name for a technique that punishes shortcuts. The curd has to hit the right pH and moisture before it goes into the hot water, and the stretch itself is more about feel than a stopwatch — too cool and it tears instead of gathering, too hot and you cook the structure out of it before you've shaped anything. Today's session is built around that judgment call: get your hands on curd at three different acidification points and feel the difference before you're doing it alone on your own make day.

## Why this keeps coming up

We hear from members every year who've watched a neighboring shop's mozzarella case move faster than expected and wondered whether it's worth the equipment and the learning curve. The honest answer is that fresh pasta filata rewards a shop that can sell it within days of making it — it's not a style built for a slow-moving case or a long shipping route. If your distribution runs short and local, or you're selling direct at a counter or a market stand, the math works better than it does for a maker shipping wheels across the country.

The workshop doesn't try to talk anyone into a new product line. It's structured to let you leave with an honest read on whether the technique fits your operation, your equipment, and the volume you can realistically move before the cheese loses the texture that makes it worth eating fresh. A few attendees each year decide it's not for them, and that's a useful outcome too — better to learn that in a training kitchen than after a batch of curd that never quite comes together.

If you couldn't make today's session, pasta filata technique also shows up as a module inside several of our production-track courses, and we run this workshop again periodically as interest holds up. Reach out to the Education Committee if you'd like to be notified the next time it's scheduled, or if your region has enough interested members to make the case for hosting one closer to home.

Either way, keep an eye on your holiday case planning over the next few weeks. If the growth numbers hold through the season, the makers who already have a stretched-curd cheese on the shelf are going to be glad they built the skill before the demand showed up rather than after.

We'll say the same thing we say every time this technique comes up: don't buy the equipment before you've done the workshop. A steam kettle and a paddle are a real investment for a small operation, and it's a lot easier to decide that after you've stretched curd with your own hands than after you've read about it. Several members who took an earlier session told us the biggest surprise wasn't the technique itself but how much the milk's own chemistry mattered going in — a make that starts with the wrong acidification never really recovers, no matter how good you get at the stretch.
