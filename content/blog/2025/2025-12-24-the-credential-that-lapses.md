Most of the credentials the federation issues sit quietly on a member's profile once earned. Competition Judge Accreditation doesn't work that way. It's valid for two years, but it's also built to lapse fast if the person holding it stops actually judging — and the quiet week between Christmas and New Year's is exactly when it's easiest to lose track of where you stand.

If you earned your accreditation coming out of a Sensory Evaluation Certificate course, you probably remember how much supervised panel time it took to get there. What's easy to forget is that the accreditation doesn't reward that one push forever. It's built around ongoing practice — judging real entries on real panels, on a rolling basis — because a palate that hasn't scored a wheel in two years isn't the instrument it was when it was first tested. Judging panels for our competitions typically pair a technical read, which deducts points for defects, with an aesthetic read, which credits a wheel for what it does well. Staying sharp at either takes repetition, not a certificate on a wall.

## Why this matters more this year

Interest in specialty and artisan cheese has held up even as membership overall has felt the pressure of a hard year for small creameries, with deli specialty categories posting real growth through 2025. That's good news for competition entry volume, but it means panels need judges who are current, not judges who let their accreditation quietly expire between competition seasons. A judge who shows up to volunteer for spring panels only to learn their credential lapsed back in November has cost the Awards & Competition Committee a seat it was counting on, and left a category short-handed at exactly the wrong moment.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: check your own status now, while the office is quiet and before entry season ramps back up. If you can't remember the last panel you sat on, that's your answer.

## What to do this week

If your accreditation is close to its two-year mark, the fastest path back to current standing is signing up for an upcoming judging session before the next cycle of entries closes. Panels run smaller in winter, which paradoxically makes them easier to get a seat on — there's less competition for slots than during the crush of late-summer competition prep, when every experienced judge in the region is already booked.

If you've let your Sensory Evaluation Certificate itself lapse rather than just the accreditation built on top of it, that's a longer road back, but it starts the same way: reach out to the Education Committee and ask where the next cohort opens. Either way, this is the week to find out where you actually stand rather than assume last year's badge still means what it used to. A quiet holiday week is a strange time to think about competition season, but it's the best time to fix a credential gap before it becomes a real one — no entries waiting, no panel schedule to work around, just an honest look at your own record.

If your creamery is also weighing whether to send someone new through the Sensory Evaluation Certificate for the first time, now is a good moment to ask: the next cohort typically fills before spring, and a new judge in training benefits from watching a full winter panel cycle before they're asked to score one alone.
