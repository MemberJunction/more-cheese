Registration is open for the Cedar Export Readiness workshop, a one-day, in-person session in Brattleboro on February 4. If you've been circling the idea of shipping product overseas for a season or two without quite starting the paperwork, this is the workshop built for exactly that stall.

We put this one on the calendar every year because the gap between "wanting to export" and "actually exporting" is almost never about demand. It's about documentation, and documentation takes longer to assemble than anyone expects the first time through.

## What actually eats the time

Export documentation for cheese touches more agencies and more forms than most members expect walking in — health certificates, product composition statements, and country-specific labeling requirements that vary by destination market. Some markets require the export certificate itself to be dated close to the actual shipping date rather than issued weeks in advance, which means the certificate step can't simply be front-loaded and forgotten; it has to be sequenced against your actual shipping schedule. Add plant eligibility checks and importing-country listing requirements on top of that, and the realistic lead time for a first shipment runs into months, not weeks.

Members who've been through the process once describe the same lesson: start the paperwork before you have a confirmed buyer, not after, because the buyer's timeline will not wait for your documents to catch up. This workshop walks through the actual sequence — what to request first, which agencies respond slowest, how to sequence a time-sensitive certificate correctly, and how to build a checklist you can reuse for every shipment after the first one, instead of relearning the process from scratch each time.

## Why now, and how to get there

February is a realistic timeline for a first export attempt tied to spring production. If the workshop confirms exporting is the right move for your creamery, you'd have roughly two to three months to gather documentation before a summer shipment window, which is tight but workable if you start now rather than after the workshop ends. A federal farm bill extension at the end of last year kept the broader agricultural policy framework, including export-adjacent support programs, running without a gap into this year, so the planning runway you'd be building on is stable even if it isn't new.

Seats are limited and this workshop has historically sold out by mid-January in past years, so don't wait until the week before to register if you're seriously considering it. Contact your regional membership representative for lodging recommendations near Brattleboro if you're traveling in, and bring whatever documentation you already have on hand — even a partial file gives the instructors something concrete to work from during the session.

Brattleboro in early February is genuinely cold, and the workshop runs a full day indoors, so pack for a long sit rather than a scenic trip. Members who've attended in past years say the most useful part isn't the morning lecture but the afternoon block, when instructors walk the room reviewing whatever paperwork attendees actually brought with them. Show up with real documents, even incomplete ones, and you'll leave with a specific list of what's missing rather than a general sense that exporting sounded complicated.
