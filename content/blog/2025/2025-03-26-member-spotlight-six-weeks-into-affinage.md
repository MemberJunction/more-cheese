Most of our member spotlights land on someone with a decade or two in the trade. This one is the opposite. Priya Natarajan joined the federation on February 10, six weeks ago as of this writing, and she's already spending most of her working hours somewhere most new members don't get near for years: inside the aging cave at Larkhollow Creamery, as an affinage apprentice.

Natarajan didn't come up through a culinary program or a family creamery. Before this, she worked a desk job unrelated to food, and the career change was less a plan than a decision she made faster than she expected to. "I'd been going to the farmers market every Saturday for years and always ending up at the same cheese stand," she told us. "At some point I asked the person behind the table how you get into this, mostly as a joke, and she gave me an actual answer. I didn't expect that."

## What the first six weeks actually look like

Apprenticeship in a working cave is not, by Natarajan's account, romantic in the way outsiders sometimes picture it. "Nobody told me how much of this job is humidity logs," she said. "I thought I'd be flipping wheels and learning to read a rind. I do that too, but mostly I'm checking a hygrometer, writing numbers down, and learning why the numbers matter before anyone lets me make a call based on them." That's a fairly common arc for new affinage apprentices — the sensory judgment comes later, once you've internalized what "normal" looks like for a given cave and season.

She's also been candid about the parts that don't come naturally yet. Telling a rind that's developing correctly from one that's drifting toward a problem is, she says, mostly pattern recognition built through repetition, and six weeks isn't enough repetition to trust her own read over a supervisor's. "I ask a lot of questions I'm sure sound basic," she said. "Everyone's been fine with that so far. I'm told that changes around month four, when you're supposed to start having your own opinions."

## Joining in a hard year for the trade

Natarajan joined the federation in a year when a lot of the public conversation around small creameries has been about feed costs and margin pressure rather than growth. She's aware of that context, and it hasn't dampened her enthusiasm so much as sharpened it. "I came in with my eyes open," she said. "Nobody's pretending this is an easy year to start a career in cheese. But the people I work with are still doing careful, exacting work under real pressure, and that's actually what made me want in — not in spite of the hard year, kind of because of it."

For members who are earlier in a similar transition, or thinking about one, the federation's Cheese Foundations Certificate is the usual starting point — Natarajan hasn't enrolled yet but says it's next on her list once she's steadier on her feet at the cave. If you're a member with your own late-start story, the Membership & Outreach Committee is always looking for a few more voices for a future spotlight; a note through the member portal reaches them directly.
