Listeria Control in Small Creameries isn't a course anyone takes for fun, and we don't pretend otherwise. It exists because soft and semi-soft cheeses carry a real environmental risk that scales with humidity, foot traffic, and the number of hands touching product between the vat and the wrapper, and small operations often don't have a dedicated food-safety staff member the way a large plant does. The next cohort opens this fall, and the timing isn't an accident: finishing before the holiday selling season starts means your environmental monitoring plan is already running, not something you're improvising in December while also filling triple the usual orders.

The course is built around a plain premise: you cannot inspect your way to safety after the fact. Listeria monocytogenes tends to live in drains, floor mats, condensation lines, and other places nobody's actively cleaning during a normal shift, and it can survive there for a long time without showing up in a finished-product test until it does. What the course actually teaches is how to find and monitor those environmental niches before a problem reaches a wheel.

## What changes in your day-to-day

Members who've completed it describe the biggest shift as swab-based environmental monitoring becoming a routine rather than an event. That means a written schedule for testing floor drains, equipment surfaces, and employee footwear zones, not just the finished cheese. It also means a real corrective-action plan for what happens when a swab comes back positive on a surface even if the product itself tests clean, because a clean product result today doesn't tell you what a colonized drain will do next week.

The course also spends time on something that's easy to underrate: how you document all of it. If a state inspector or a wholesale buyer asks for your environmental monitoring records, "we're careful" is not an answer that holds up. A dated log with a corrective-action trail is. That documentation habit is also what the Food Safety Committee looks for when members ask about recall-readiness more broadly — the two build on each other.

Research on small and mid-sized dairy plants backs up why the course leans so hard on corrective action rather than just testing. A recent multi-plant study found that corrective actions — cleaning, re-sampling, sometimes reworking a piece of equipment — made up the majority of what these operations actually spent on their monitoring programs, more than the testing itself. That matches what instructors tell us: a monitoring plan that only tests and never fixes what it finds isn't really a monitoring plan, it's a very expensive way of confirming a problem you already suspected. Earlier work sampling nine small cheese facilities, several of them farmstead operations like many of our members, found a low but real background rate of Listeria turning up in environmental swabs — enough to make the point that "we've never had an issue" is often a matter of not having looked closely, not a matter of not having a problem.

## Who should sign up now

If you make anything soft, washed-rind, or otherwise higher-moisture, and you don't currently have a written environmental monitoring plan, this is the course to prioritize before your production volume climbs for the season. It pairs naturally with a Food Safety & HACCP Certificate if you don't already hold one, but it doesn't require it — the course stands on its own.

Enrollment for the fall cohort is open now on the certification page. If you're not sure whether your current practices already cover what the course teaches, the Food Safety Committee can do a quick gap check before you commit the time.
