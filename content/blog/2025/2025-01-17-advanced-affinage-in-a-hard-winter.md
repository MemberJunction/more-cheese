We get a version of the same question every January: which credential is worth starting this year. For members who are already running an aging program — not learning affinage from scratch, but doing it daily and wanting the vocabulary and the formal grounding to go with the instinct they've built — the answer we give most often is the Advanced Affinage Certificate.

It's not an entry-level credential, and we'd steer someone brand new to cheesemaking toward Cheese Foundations first. Advanced Affinage assumes you already know what a good rind looks like. What it adds is the science underneath that instinct: the microbiology of rind flora and how organisms like the salt-tolerant bacteria behind a washed rind actually develop over time, the biochemical processes — proteolysis, glycolysis, lipolysis — that turn a fresh curd into a matured wheel, and a working framework for diagnosing a defect rather than just noticing one.

## Why the timing works this winter

Affinage is fundamentally a temperature-and-humidity discipline, and most caves run coolest and most controllable through the winter months, which makes this a natural season to be paying close attention to your own room rather than fighting summer heat. It's also, this particular winter, a season when heating fuel prices have been running a little higher than last year across the Northeast, which means a lot of members are watching every line of their energy budget more closely than usual. That's relevant here in a specific way: a chunk of the certificate's material is about running an aging program efficiently — understanding which adjustments actually change outcomes and which are just tradition — and members who've gone through it have told us it paid for itself by identifying one or two habits in their own cave that weren't doing what they assumed.

That's not us promising the course will cut your propane bill. It's a more modest claim: understanding why you're flipping wheels on the schedule you're flipping them, and why your target humidity is set where it is, sometimes reveals that a costly habit was inherited rather than necessary. A precise process is usually a more efficient one, even when efficiency wasn't the original goal. The assessment combines a written component on the biochemistry and microbiology with a practical evaluation — reading a set of aging wheels at different stages and diagnosing what's happening in each. It's not a memorization test. Members who've struggled with it typically struggled because they could describe a defect but not explain the underlying mechanism causing it, which is exactly the gap the credential is designed to close. If you can already tell a cave is running too dry by the feel of a rind but can't say what specifically it's doing to the cheese's moisture migration, this is built for that gap.

## Getting started

The certificate doesn't require a fixed course sequence, but most members who complete it start with Affinage Fundamentals if they haven't already taken it, then move into the assessment once they've got a season or two of hands-on aging under their own supervision. If you're weighing whether now is the right winter to start, our honest advice is that the credential rewards people who are already deep in the daily work of a cave — if that's you, an unusually cost-conscious winter is as good a time as any to formalize what you already know.

Reach out through your member portal for the current assessment schedule, or ask in the forum — several members who hold the certificate are generally willing to talk through what surprised them about the process.
