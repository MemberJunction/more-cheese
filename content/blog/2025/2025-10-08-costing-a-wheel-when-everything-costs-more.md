Here's a pattern our Education Committee keeps running into this fall: a member calls with a pricing question, mentions offhand that feed costs are down this year, and seems surprised when we ask what their total cost per pound of finished cheese looks like right now. Feed is genuinely more affordable than it was a year ago, corn supply is strong and grain prices have eased, and that's real relief for anyone buying hay or grain. But feed is one line on a make sheet, not the whole sheet, and the other lines — labor, packaging, utilities, repairs — have kept moving in the other direction all year. A creamery that priced its wheels off last year's total cost, or worse, off a general sense that "costs are up," is probably pricing off a number that's now wrong in a new direction.

This is exactly the gap our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course was built to close, and it's worth walking through the basic version here even if you're not ready to enroll.

## Rebuild the number, don't adjust it

The instinct when costs shift is to nudge last year's price by a rough percentage and move on. That works fine when every input moves together, but this year the inputs are moving in different directions at different speeds, which means an across-the-board nudge either overcorrects on feed or undercorrects on labor. The course's first module has members rebuild their cost-per-pound from scratch, listing milk cost, cultures and rennet, labor hours at current wages, packaging, energy, and a depreciation allowance for equipment, then adding them fresh rather than adjusting an old total. It takes longer than eyeballing it. It also catches the line items people forget, which in our experience is almost always energy — a small creamery's cave climate control and hot-water usage for washing and sanitizing add up to more than most makers estimate until they actually track a full month.

## What the course adds beyond the spreadsheet

Two exercises matter more than the spreadsheet itself. The first is yield tracking: you can't do much about the utility rate or the price of packaging film, but you can control how much finished cheese you get per hundredweight of milk, and that's where a lot of margin quietly leaks out. A batch that runs a little dry loses paste weight you're paying full milk cost for and getting no revenue on, so the course spends a full session on tracking yield batch to batch — a half-percent improvement across a year's production is often worth more than any single pricing decision.

The second is saying the number out loud. The part of the course members tell us matters most isn't the math, it's the exercise where you say your actual cost per wheel to another member in a small group before deciding what to charge for it. A farmstead producer from a recent cohort told us she'd been pricing her aged wheels for two years without ever having said the true cost per pound out loud to anyone, and hearing herself say it made clear how thin the margin actually was — thinner than she'd been telling herself. Costing in isolation lets you round in your own favor without noticing; saying it to someone else doesn't.

If your last full cost rebuild was more than a year ago, this fall is a reasonable time to redo it, given how unevenly costs have moved.
