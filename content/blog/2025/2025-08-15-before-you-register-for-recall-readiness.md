The Webinar: Recall Readiness 2025 session goes live on August 21, and registrations have been coming in steadily enough that we want to say a word about what this one actually is before more members sign up expecting something it isn't. It's not a food-safety fundamentals refresher, and it's not a HACCP course in miniature. It's narrower than that, and more useful for it: what to do in the first hours after you suspect a lot needs to come off shelves.

We built this session because of a pattern we kept hearing about from members — not that recalls are common, but that when a scare does happen, the first hour is the one people are least prepared for. Everyone knows they should have a plan on paper. Fewer people have actually walked through what happens when the plan meets a real phone call from a distributor asking which lots are affected.

## What the session covers

The webinar walks through four pieces: how to isolate a suspect lot before you know the full scope, who needs to be notified and in what order (your distributor, your certifying body, and depending on your state, a regulator), what records you need pulled and ready before anyone asks for them, and how to talk to a retail buyer who's asking "is my case safe" before you have a full answer yourself. That last piece is the one members tell us they were least prepared for — the technical response and the communication response aren't the same skill, and a lot of recall plans only cover the first one.

It's a free session and it's virtual, which makes it an easy one to put in front of more than one person on your team. That's actually the point. A recall plan that lives in one person's head is a recall plan that fails the day that person is on vacation or out sick. If you've got a quality manager, a production lead, and whoever answers your general phone line, all three should probably be on this call, even if only one of them usually handles this kind of thing.

Part of why we built this session now is that the paperwork side of recall readiness is getting more demanding, not less. A federal traceability rule aimed at high-risk foods, cheese among them, has been working through a multi-year phase-in, and this August brought a formal proposal to extend the compliance deadline by roughly two and a half years rather than the requirements simply arriving as first announced. That's the kind of regulatory motion that's easy to lose track of if you're not checking for it, and it doesn't change what a real recall asks of you in the meantime — lot numbers, ship dates, who received what, produced fast, not eventually.

That's really the throughline of this whole session: readiness is mostly a records problem wearing a food-safety costume. The technical steps matter, but they only work if you can answer "which lots, which dates, which customers" in minutes rather than hours.

## The credential this feeds into

If the webinar leaves you wanting more structure than an hour can give you, it lines up well with the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate, which goes considerably deeper into building and documenting a full recall and traceability plan rather than just walking through the first-hours response. Several members have used the webinar as the thing that convinced them the credential was worth the time, rather than the other way around.

Registration is still open through the week of the 18th. If you haven't signed up yet, do it before you're the one making the phone call this covers.
