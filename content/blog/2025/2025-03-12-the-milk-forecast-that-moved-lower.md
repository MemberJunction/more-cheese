The federal government's monthly agricultural supply-and-demand report came out this week with a number nobody wanted to see: the 2025 all-milk price forecast dropped a full dollar, from $22.60 per hundredweight to $21.60. That's roughly a dollar below where the same forecast had the market sitting a year ago, and for members already managing tight margins after a winter of high feed and energy costs, it's not a small revision. The report also trimmed its 2025 milk production estimate, citing slower growth in output per cow that's offsetting a modest rise in the number of dairy cows nationally.

## What actually changed

A dollar per hundredweight sounds abstract until you run it against real volume. For an operation producing 25,000 pounds of milk per cow annually, even a mid-sized herd feels that kind of forecast cut as real money over a full year — not a catastrophe on its own, but one more line pushing against margins that were already thin. The class-level breakdown moved further than the headline number: the Class III forecast, which anchors a lot of cheese-milk pricing, came down more than a dollar, while Class IV eased by a smaller amount. If your milk contracts key off Class III specifically, this revision hits harder than the topline figure suggests.

The forecast reduction reflects softer expectations on the demand side rather than any single dramatic event, which is in some ways harder to plan around than a price shock with a clear cause. Slow, broad softening doesn't give you a single villain to react to; it just means the ground under next quarter's budget shifted a little. Not every part of the market moved the same direction, either. Dairy exporters watching international markets have seen some strength overseas this spring, with a few major dairy-exporting countries reporting notably higher export values for milk powder, butter, and cheese compared to a year earlier. That's cold comfort if your business doesn't touch export channels directly, but it does suggest the softness is domestic and demand-side rather than a broad global downturn — worth knowing if you're deciding how much weight to put on this forecast versus the one that follows it next month.

## What to do with a forecast, not a fact

We'd caution against overreacting to any single monthly revision. Forecasts get revised again next month, and the month after that, and the further out a projection reaches, the more it should be treated as a planning input rather than a verdict. What we'd suggest instead: if you haven't already run your spring numbers against a price a dollar below where you budgeted in January, do that this week, before planting decisions, hay contracts, or hiring plans lock in around an optimistic number. A forecast cut you've already stress-tested against is a Tuesday. One you haven't is a scramble in June.

If your creamery buys milk rather than produces it, the same logic runs the other direction — a softer forecast may mean more room to negotiate on supply contracts renewing this spring, though we'd encourage caution about assuming softness holds through the full year; one monthly revision is not a trend. Members weighing supply agreements or pricing strategy this spring are welcome to bring specific questions to the Membership & Outreach Committee, which fields more of these calls in a soft-forecast month than in almost any other kind. If your books show the revision hurting more than a rough back-of-envelope number, that's also worth raising before your next renewal conversation, not after.
