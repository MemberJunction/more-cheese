A member called our office last week with a question we've been fielding a lot this spring: her state's dairy program just added her creamery's bulk tank to a federal testing rotation, and she wanted to know what, exactly, it was testing for and whether she needed to do anything differently. The short answer is no, not on the production floor. The longer answer is worth walking through, because the testing program has grown a lot since it started and most of the growth has happened quietly.

The federal order behind this testing requires raw milk samples from farms and processing silos to be screened for a specific avian influenza strain that's been circulating in dairy herds in a growing number of states. States move through stages as they onboard: silo-level sampling first, then routine bulk-tank sampling, then a faster response tier if a detection turns up, and eventually a stage confirming a state's herds are clear. Most dairy states have been through at least the first two stages by now. If your state's program reaches out to schedule a sample from your farm or your receiving dock, that's a standard part of the rotation, not a sign anyone suspects a problem at your operation specifically.

## What this does and doesn't mean for your cheese case

Here's the part that matters most for members who make or sell cheese rather than raise the herd: this testing is a surveillance tool for animal health, not a food-safety alert on finished product. Every published sampling program to date, run against pasteurized milk, butter, and cheese, has come back negative for viable virus, and the reason is straightforward — pasteurization does what it's designed to do here. If you're a licensed processor working with pasteurized milk, this order changes essentially nothing about what leaves your cave.

Where it does touch you is upstream, if you also milk your own herd or buy directly from a small number of farms you know personally. A positive bulk-tank result there triggers a herd-level response, not a recall of anything already aged and boxed. And if you sell or make anything from unpasteurized milk under your state's raw-milk provisions, this is exactly the kind of monitoring program worth tracking closely with your own state veterinarian's office — that's outside our scope and belongs with them, not with us.

## Where to actually put your attention

If you want to understand a food-safety plan well enough to answer a buyer's question about it without hedging, that's what the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate is built for, and our Milk Quality & Herd Health course goes deeper into the herd-health side specifically for members who also manage animals. Neither course will tell you anything about this particular strain that your state's animal health office can't tell you faster and more specifically — but both will make the next surveillance program, whatever it turns out to be, a lot less confusing to sit through.

If your bulk tank gets added to a rotation this season, the Food Safety Committee has been collecting members' questions about how the process actually works in practice — timing, notification, what a result letter looks like — and would rather hear from you before you're guessing than after.
