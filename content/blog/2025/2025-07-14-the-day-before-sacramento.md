By the time this posts, a good share of you are already in the air or on the road, and the rest are loading a car with a cooler you swore you wouldn't bring this year. Tomorrow morning the ICF Annual Conference opens in Sacramento, and we wanted to use today — the quiet day, the one before the badge goes on — to walk through what actually happens once you arrive, not what the program says will happen.

Check-in opens early, and it is worth getting there before the line does. Bring the confirmation email even if you're sure you registered; the front desk sees more "I know I paid for this" conversations than you'd think, mostly from members whose company card renewed the registration and never told them. If your registration is tied to your employer rather than to you personally, that's also the moment to confirm your name badge lists you and not whoever booked the room.

Sacramento in mid-July usually runs hot, and this week has actually been milder than the early-July stretch, but the forecast still has afternoons in the upper eighties, and the walk between the main hall and the breakout rooms is mostly outdoors on this year's site. Dress for the room you're heading to, not the one you're leaving — the general sessions run cold with the air conditioning fighting the heat, and the smaller workshop rooms don't always keep up. Water bottles are provided at the registration table this year, which is a small thing, but it's one fewer bag to carry.

## What the first morning is actually for

The opening session is short by design. It exists to get everyone in one room once, point you toward the schedule board, and get out of the way. The real work of the conference happens in the tracks that follow — food safety, standards, pricing and margin, and the always-crowded room where the Awards & Competition Committee talks through what judges are looking for this cycle. The standards track has extra weight this year: the comment period on a federal front-of-package labeling proposal closes tomorrow, the same day the conference opens, and the Standards Committee's session is the place to hear what a comment on your own label copy should say before that window shuts. If you've never been before, pick one track and stay with it for the morning rather than bouncing between rooms; you'll get more out of three connected sessions than six disconnected ones.

The Food Safety Committee's session tends to fill up fastest, and this year is likely no exception given how much has shifted in federal pathogen surveillance over the past few weeks — more on that in Friday's post. If you make raw-milk cheese or run a small aging room, it's worth showing up early enough to get a seat rather than standing in the back.

## The part nobody puts on the agenda

The hallway conversations are where a lot of the actual value of this conference lives — the five minutes with a distributor rep who mentions a route change before it's official, the aside from another cheesemaker about a cave humidity problem you've been fighting for a year. Leave slack in your schedule for it. Don't book every session back to back.

If this is your first conference, find the new-member table near registration. It's staffed by volunteers who've been coming for years and are genuinely glad to point you toward the right session, the good coffee, or the quietest place to make a phone call. You don't need an appointment. Just show up and say it's your first year.

We'll see you there tomorrow.
