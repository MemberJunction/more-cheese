Butter has now set a wholesale price record for four months running, and the federal milk production report that came out this month showed output up again, a bit more than one percent over last year in the major dairy states. Read quickly, that sounds like good news for anyone who sells milk for a living. Read slowly, it's a reminder that a record in one column doesn't always help the column you're actually watching.

Members who buy fluid milk for cheesemaking, rather than sell it, have been telling us the opposite story. When butterfat commands that kind of premium, more of the milk that would otherwise go into vats gets pulled toward butter and powder instead, and the cheese milk that's left gets priced accordingly. A few smaller creameries on this month's committee call described the same pattern: a milk price that's technically flat on paper but harder to secure at the volume they need, on the schedule they need it, without paying up for it.

## What actually moved, and what didn't

The federal order pricing changes that took effect earlier this month are still working their way through contracts, and several members said their processors haven't finished explaining what the new formula means for their invoices. That's not unusual — formula changes tend to show up in the fine print before anyone notices them in the total. If your co-op or processor hasn't sent a plain-language explanation of what changed and why, it's reasonable to ask for one before your next renewal conversation.

Global milk prices, meanwhile, dipped for the first time this year, with softer export demand cited as the main driver. That's a market signal worth watching if you sell finished cheese into export channels, less so if your business is entirely domestic and direct. We'd rather members read too much market commentary than too little, but it's worth sorting which numbers actually touch your business before you let them change your plans.

None of this changes the basic math a lot of small operations are up against this year: feed costs that haven't fully come down, energy bills that spike with every cave or vat that runs longer than planned, and a milk supply picture that rewards scale more than it rewards care. We don't have a fix for that in a blog post. What we can do is keep pointing members toward the tools that help at the margin — the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course has a new cohort starting next month, and it's built around exactly this kind of "the market moved, now what" math rather than general theory.

## A practical next step

If you haven't sat down with your own numbers since the spring flush ended, this is a reasonable week to do it. Compare your milk cost per pound of finished cheese against where it sat in January, and bring that comparison — not just a feeling that "things are tighter" — into any conversation with your processor, your lender, or your own board. Members in the Membership & Outreach Committee's small-creamery working group have been swapping worksheets for exactly this purpose; reach out through the member portal if you'd like a copy.
