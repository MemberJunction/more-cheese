This week's spotlight isn't one member so much as a phone call we've had with several members, all farmstead operators running caves that used to run on a fuel bill they didn't think much about. We're describing a composite, not any one creamery, because the story is common enough that naming a single name would undersell how many of you are living it.

She's the third generation to run this particular operation — grandmother started it, mother built the wholesale accounts, she added the cave. Through most of the artisan boom years, the propane line on her budget was an afterthought next to milk and labor. This winter it isn't. Wholesale propane has been running noticeably higher than it did a year ago through this heating season, and what shows up on an actual delivery invoice for a small rural account tends to run well above that wholesale number besides. A wet, short hay season pushed feed costs up before the heating season even started, and the fuel contract she locked in for the cave came in higher than the one before it. Two line items moving the same direction at once is the part that gets a producer's attention.

## Cutting degrees, not wheels

What she didn't do is what we worried she might: shrink the aging program to save on heat. Instead she worked with an HVAC contractor to insulate the cave door and add a simple curtain airlock, dropped her target temperature by two degrees within the range her styles can tolerate, and moved her highest-turnover wheels to the warmest shelf so they finish faster and leave the space sooner. None of that shows up as a dramatic fix. Together, by her own estimate, it took a meaningful bite out of the season's fuel use without changing what's on the shelf or slowing her fastest-moving cheese.

The other thing she did was call us in November instead of March. We don't have a lending program and don't pretend to — but Membership & Outreach was able to walk her through what other members in her situation have tried, including a couple of practical building fixes that cost far less than a new furnace. They also pointed her toward the federation's cost-tracking worksheet so she could see, in her own numbers, exactly how much of her margin pressure was fuel and how much was feed before she decided what to change. If your own cave budget looks tighter than last year's, that's the same door to knock on. Waiting until the invoice is overdue narrows your options; calling while you're still just worried keeps more of them open.

None of this is a one-season fix, either. A federal forecast update issued late last month pointed to higher milk production alongside softer cheese and butter prices for the year just closed — a mixed signal, not a rescue. The producers weathering this winter best seem to be the ones treating heat, feed, and milk price as three separate numbers to manage rather than waiting for one of them to bail out the other two.

## What we'd ask of the rest of you

If you've found a fix like hers — a cheap airlock, a shelving change, a fuel-buying cooperative with neighboring farms — tell us. We're hearing enough of these stories this winter that a short members' guide to low-cost cave energy fixes is worth putting together, and the best version of it will come from people who've actually done it, not from us guessing.
