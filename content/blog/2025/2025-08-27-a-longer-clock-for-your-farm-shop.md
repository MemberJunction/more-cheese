A proposal earlier this month would push the compliance date for an expanded food-traceability recordkeeping rule back by roughly two and a half years — from January 2026 to sometime in 2028. Comments on that proposed extension are due by September 8. If you sell any wheels through a farm shop, farmers market stand, or other direct channel, this is worth knowing about even though the deadline it affects just got a lot less urgent.

We want to be careful about what this actually means. A longer runway to comply isn't the same as no obligation, and it definitely isn't the same as "this doesn't apply to farm shops." Plenty of direct-to-consumer sales already sit outside the rule's scope if the product is packaged on the farm, stays sealed until the customer has it, and the package is labeled with your farm's name, address, and phone number. What the extension buys is more time to figure out, without a January deadline hanging over the decision, whether your specific mix of direct sales and wholesale distribution needs a more formal lot-tracking system than what you're doing now.

For members who sell almost entirely direct, none of this may end up applying at all — and that's worth confirming for your own operation rather than assuming either way. For members who split volume between a farm shop and a wholesale account or two, the exemption gets harder to rely on cleanly, because the wholesale side of the business was never going to be exempt in the first place. The honest answer for most of you is somewhere in between, which is exactly why this is worth a closer look now rather than a guess later.

## Where our September workshop comes in

Workshop: Cave Farm Shop Retail runs September 11 in Ludlow, and it was built around exactly this kind of decision — what running a farm-shop counter alongside a wholesale account actually requires in terms of labeling, recordkeeping, and knowing which product went out which door. That question doesn't disappear just because a federal compliance date moved. If anything, the extra time is best spent building a system you'd have wanted anyway, rather than one built in a scramble against a deadline.

Several members who sell both wholesale and direct told us the hardest part isn't the farm-shop side — it's keeping the direct-sale paperwork and the wholesale lot codes from drifting into two different systems that don't talk to each other. That's a recall-plan problem as much as a labeling problem, and it's one worth solving on your own timeline rather than a regulator's. A farm shop with a simple notebook log and a wholesale account with a proper lot-code system are both fine on their own; the trouble starts when a wheel crosses from one side to the other and nobody wrote it down consistently.

## If you can make it to Ludlow

The workshop runs a half day and is built for owner-operators who are handling this themselves, not delegating it to a compliance department they don't have. Bring your current label templates and your farm-shop sales log if you keep one — the exercises work better against your actual paperwork than a generic worksheet. And if September 11 doesn't work for your schedule, ask your regional rep about whether a similar session is planned for your area later this year.

None of this is a substitute for reading the actual proposed rule and comment docket yourself, or for a conversation with whoever handles your regulatory filings. It's a place to get the practical side sorted while the deadline isn't breathing down your neck.
