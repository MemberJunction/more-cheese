Cheese exports had a genuinely strong January this year, with volume up double digits from a year ago and gains showing up in markets well beyond the usual ones. If you sell overseas, or you've been thinking about starting, that's the kind of number that gets attention. It's also the kind of number that can turn on you fast if a trade dispute changes the tariff picture for one of those markets, and economists who watch trade policy closely have been warning all month that a fight with a major destination market could push a supply glut back into the domestic case and pull farmgate prices down with it.

We can't do anything about the policy side from here. What the federation can do is make sure that when a member does decide to ship overseas, the paperwork isn't the thing that sinks the shipment — and that a member who's watching this uncertainty and wondering whether exporting is worth the risk has an accurate picture of what it actually takes before they decide.

## What the workshop covers

On February 4th, the Events Committee is running "Workshop: Cedar Export Readiness" in Brattleboro. It's a full day built around the actual documents an export shipment needs — certificates of origin, health certificates, the harmonized codes that determine what tariff line your cheese lands on, and the specific paperwork mistakes that get a container held at a border instead of delivered to a buyer. It draws heavily on the federation's Export Documentation Essentials course, so if you've already taken that course, this is a chance to work through real forms with someone checking your answers; if you haven't, the workshop stands on its own and covers the fundamentals first.

It's a small-group, hands-on session by design, capped low enough that instructors can walk the room rather than lecture at it. Bring an actual shipment you're planning, if you have one, and they'll work the paperwork with you rather than around a hypothetical example that doesn't match your product or your destination market.

A record export month is a good problem to have, but it also means more members are looking at overseas accounts for the first time, right as the policy picture gets less predictable. That combination is exactly when documentation mistakes get expensive — a shipment held at a border while a certificate gets sorted out costs a lot more in a volatile tariff environment than it would in a calm one, because you may not get the same rate, or the same buyer, on the other side of the delay.

## Who this is for

This isn't only for members already exporting. If you're a domestic producer weighing whether an overseas account is worth pursuing, seeing what the documentation actually requires — the lead time, the specific certificates, which of them need advance scheduling with a lab or an inspector — is the fastest way to find out whether it fits your operation before you commit to a buyer's timeline. Several members who attended a similar session last year told us afterward that the paperwork walkthrough changed their launch date more than their strategy — they still exported, just three weeks later than they'd planned, once they saw what needed to be lined up first.

Seats are limited because the format depends on small groups. If you're planning to attend, register through the events page sooner rather than later, and if you have questions about whether the workshop is the right fit for where your export plans stand, the Events Committee can talk it through with you ahead of time.
