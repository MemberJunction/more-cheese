The federal milk price figures for this month came in higher again, continuing a pattern we've been watching since spring: the Class I base price moved up more than a dollar per hundredweight from June, and estimates for the year's all-milk price are running well above where most farmstead budgets were set last winter. If you make cheese from your own herd's milk, that's good news on the input side and complicated news everywhere else, because your competitors buying milk on the open market are absorbing the same increase and passing at least some of it through to the case.

Cheese production nationally has been running ahead of expectations too — the most recent federal production report showed total cheese output up more than 3% year over year, with cheddar output alone up nearly 10%. That's not really a story about artisan cheese — it's mass production capacity — but it matters to our members because it sets the floor price the wholesale market compares everyone against. When commodity cheese production climbs, the price gap between a mass-market block and your farmstead wheel gets easier to justify to a buyer, provided you're telling the story of why the gap exists. A shop that can't explain the difference between a $6 block and an $18 wheel loses that argument at the register every time.

## The export side is real, and it's not small anymore

U.S. cheese exports have been setting records for a while now, and international cheese pricing has stayed firm even as some regional markets softened. For most of our membership that's background noise — you're selling within a couple hundred miles of your cave, not shipping pallets overseas. But it affects you indirectly: a strong export market absorbs domestic supply that would otherwise compete for space in the same regional cases you're selling into. It's one more reason wholesale cheese prices have held up better this year than the milk-price headlines alone would suggest.

There's also a trade policy layer worth knowing about even if it doesn't touch your business directly. Tariff commitments between major trading partners have been shifting through the summer, easing some duties on U.S. goods entering certain markets while leaving others unresolved. If you export at all — even a handful of gift boxes to an international retailer — it's worth checking with your distributor or freight forwarder on current duty schedules before you commit to a shipment, rather than assuming last year's numbers still hold.

## What to do with this at the counter

None of this changes your make sheet, but it should change your pricing conversation. If a buyer pushes back on a wholesale quote this quarter, the honest answer is that milk costs are up across the board, not just at your creamery, and the commodity cheese they're comparing you to is dealing with the same input pressure. Members working through a wholesale renegotiation this summer should bring the actual hundredweight numbers to that conversation rather than a general sense that "prices are up" — buyers respond better to a specific figure than a vague one.
