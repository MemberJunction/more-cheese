One of our farmstead members did her herd count twice this summer. Once in June, out of habit, and again last week, out of necessity. Between the two counts nothing dramatic happened — no barn fire, no disease scare — just a slow accumulation of numbers that finally added up to a question she couldn't put off: does every animal in the barn still pencil out.

She milks a small mixed herd and makes a washed-rind and a young tomme, selling most of it through a regional distributor and the rest off the farm. It's the kind of operation this year's conditions were built to squeeze, at least on paper. Feed costs have actually come down this summer — August's average feed cost per hundredweight was reportedly the lowest in nearly five years, as corn and alfalfa prices both eased from spring highs. That should have been good news across the board. For her, it helped, but it didn't fix the underlying problem, because her milk price has also drifted down with the season's abundant supply, and the federal margin-support program that's supposed to catch a squeeze like this hasn't triggered a payment for most of the year. The formula benchmarks a national feed cost that doesn't fully reflect what she actually pays, and it doesn't touch the cooling, labor, and vet costs that have crept up steadily since 2021.

## What the second count changed

The second count didn't lead her to sell off the herd. It led her to sell off three animals — the lowest producers, the ones costing more in feed and vet visits than their milk was worth — and to renegotiate her distributor contract from a floating rate to something closer to fixed for the next two quarters. Neither move is dramatic. Both were overdue. "I kept telling myself I'd deal with it after the fall cheeses were in the cave," she told us. "The count told me I couldn't wait that long."

She also went back through her own books, cwt by cwt, rather than relying on the national averages that the support programs are built around. Her actual feed bill, cooling costs, and labor ran meaningfully above the benchmarks used to calculate whether she'd qualify for assistance — which is a big part of why the math looked fine at a glance and wrong up close.

She's not alone in doing this kind of mid-year recalculation. Several of our farmstead and small-producer members have mentioned similar conversations with their accountants this summer — not panic, just a harder look at which parts of the operation are carrying the rest. That's consistent with what we're hearing across the membership this year: margins look reasonable in the aggregate reports and tighter than that on an individual farmstead's ledger, especially where feed, energy, and labor costs sit above whatever national number a support formula is using.

## Where the federation can help

If you're a farmstead member running your own version of this math, two things are worth a look. The Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly this kind of per-animal, per-wheel breakeven analysis, built for producers who don't have a full-time controller doing it for them. And if a distributor conversation is on your list, your regional rep has seen a wide range of contract structures across the membership and can talk through what's worked for creameries your size.

None of this is about scaling down for its own sake. It's about knowing, animal by animal and contract by contract, what's actually paying for the cave — and not assuming a national average tells you that.
