Cheese made from raw milk sold within a single state answers to that state's rules, not a single federal standard, and this spring a growing number of state legislatures are actively revisiting theirs. Some are loosening restrictions on intrastate sales; others are tightening testing requirements in response to livestock disease concerns moving through dairy herds this year. For a member whose creamery sits near a state line, or who ships within a region that spans two or three different rule sets, that patchwork isn't an abstract policy story. It's the difference between a wheel you can sell next Tuesday and one you can't.

Tom Reyes has spent more legislative hours on this issue than almost anyone in the federation. He doesn't attend our conferences much — ask around and people will tell you Tom is not a stage person — but he has logged more than thirty advocacy actions with us over the years, most of them written testimony or public comment on raw-milk rulemaking in his home state and the ones next door.

## Why the aging rule keeps moving

Raw-milk cheese in the United States generally must be aged at least 60 days before sale under the federal standard that governs interstate commerce, but states retain their own authority over sales that stay within their borders, and that's where the real variation shows up. Some states permit direct farm sales of raw milk itself; others prohibit it outright. Aging-rule enforcement, testing frequency, and labeling requirements for raw-milk cheese differ from one legislature to the next, and none of it stays still for long — a handful of states have introduced new bills already this year, some expanding access and some adding testing steps in response to the spread of a cattle illness that's drawn fresh attention to dairy herd biosecurity.

"The rule you learned five years ago might not be the rule on the books today," Tom told us. "I've testified in front of the same committee three sessions in a row on the same basic question, because the bill kept coming back in a different shape. If you only check the rule once, you're checking it out of date."

## What member testimony actually does

The federation doesn't lobby on members' behalf in the way a paid advocacy shop might, and we're careful not to overstate our role here — we don't write legislation and we don't promise outcomes. What we do is make it easier for a working cheesemaker to get a testimony or written comment in front of the right committee, with language that's accurate about aging science and production realities rather than either side's talking points. Tom's approach, which he's refined over years of doing this, is to keep it plain: what he makes, how he ages it, what testing already happens on his farm, and what a proposed change would actually require him to do differently.

"Committees hear a lot of testimony that's mostly opinion," he said. "What they don't hear enough of is someone describing their actual swab schedule and what a new rule would cost them to comply with. That's the only kind of testimony I know how to give, and it's usually the kind that gets remembered."

If your state has an active raw-milk or aging-rule bill this session — and there's a reasonable chance it does — our advocacy program can help you find the right committee, the right comment window, and a template that starts from your actual production practice rather than a form letter. Members with questions about a specific state's current rule can also reach our Standards Committee directly; getting the citation right before you testify matters more than getting there first.
