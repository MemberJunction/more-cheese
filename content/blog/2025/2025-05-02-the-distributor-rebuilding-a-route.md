A refrigerated truck doesn't care why a stop disappeared from its route — a closed creamery and a relocated one look the same on a delivery sheet, just a blank space where a pickup used to be. One of our distributor members spent the winter staring at two blank spaces like that on her regional route sheet, and rather than shrink the route to fit what was left, she rebuilt it.

Both accounts were small farmstead producers she'd carried for years, and both closed within a few months of each other — one to retirement with no buyer, one to the kind of feed-and-energy cost math that's been quietly ending a lot of small operations this year. She told us the second call was the harder one, because she'd watched that creamery's owner do everything right and still lose the arithmetic.

## Filling the truck differently, not less

Her instinct wasn't to consolidate the remaining stops into a tighter, smaller run. Instead she went looking for producers who'd outgrown their own delivery capacity but hadn't yet found a distributor willing to take them on at low volume — the kind of account that's a rounding error to a large distributor but a real relationship to a smaller one. She picked up three new farmstead accounts this spring, each shipping less volume individually than either of the two she'd lost, but together filling more of the truck than before.

It's slower work. Three new relationships mean three new sets of packaging quirks, three different aging schedules to plan pickups around, three producers who don't yet know her routing habits well enough to trust a delayed pickup isn't a problem. She's spent more hours on the phone this spring than she has in years, mostly just building the kind of trust that lets a small producer hand their cheese to someone else's truck without hovering.

## What the route actually taught her

Ask her what she'd tell another distributor watching accounts disappear, and she doesn't talk about pricing or contracts first. She talks about timing — that the producers most worth picking up are usually the ones who are almost, but not quite, ready to ask for help, and that showing up before they ask tends to matter more than any rate she could quote them. A cold call to a struggling creamery reads as vulturing. A conversation that starts with "I've got room on a Tuesday route near you" reads as something else entirely.

She's also blunter than she used to be about the accounts she can't save. Not every small producer she talked to this year could be folded into a viable route, and she's stopped pretending otherwise to be kind about it. The honesty, she said, is what her other accounts trust her for now.

If a closure or relocation has left a hole in your own distribution plan — on either end, producer or distributor — the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps informal notes on members looking for exactly this kind of match. It's not a formal marketplace, just a place to say what you have and what you need before the gap sits empty for a season. In a year like this one, a filled truck is worth more than a fast one.
