Two weeks ago a new round of border duties took effect on goods crossing to and from our two
largest neighboring markets, and the phone lines to our advocacy staff have not slowed down
since. If your creamery ships wheels north, or your distributor brings in cultures and
equipment from across either border, you have probably already seen a line item on an invoice
that wasn't there in December.

We want to be plain about what we know and don't know. Tariff schedules on specialty dairy
products have moved before and settled before, and nobody on our staff is going to predict
where this lands by summer. What we can do is tell members what to check this week, and tell
regulators what small exporters are actually experiencing, which is not always the same
picture that gets described in the trade press.

## What members are telling us

A mid-sized fromagerie in the Northeast, exporting maybe a dozen pallets a month, told us their
distributor absorbed the new duty for February shipments but flagged that March contracts will
need repricing. A cheesemaking supply importer working with a European affineur relationship
said the bigger disruption wasn't the tariff rate itself but the uncertainty about whether it
would still apply by the time a shipment already at sea arrived. That's the pattern across the
comments we've collected: it's the volatility, more than any single number, that's forcing
smaller operations to hold off on signing new supply contracts.

We've passed member comments like these to the parts of the federal government that handle
trade remedy petitions and small-business exemptions, asking specifically for two things: a
predictable phase-in window for any new schedule, so a shipment loaded under one set of rules
isn't taxed under another by the time it lands, and a small-exporter carve-out modeled on the
one that already exists for de minimis shipments. Neither is guaranteed, but both are requests
we can make on the record, and we will keep members posted through the advocacy digest as the
comment period develops.

## What to check on your own paperwork this week

If you export or import as part of your business, three things are worth five minutes each:
confirm your customs broker has your current harmonized tariff schedule codes on file rather
than a saved template from last year, ask your distributor directly whether new duties are
being absorbed or passed through on your specific product line, and hold off on locking in
multi-month supply contracts across either border until the schedule has held steady for at
least a few weeks. None of that is legal advice — talk to your own trade counsel or customs
broker for anything contract-specific — but it's the same checklist our advocacy staff has been
giving members on the phone.

If you want your own experience with the new tariff lines on the record, the advocacy team is
collecting member comments through the end of the month. A short note about what changed on your
invoice is more useful to us than a general complaint, so be specific if you write in.
