Most of what we write in June assumes a calendar where the seasons run the way they do across most of our membership — flush milk, market season, cave humidity climbing with the outside heat. One of our members reads those pieces from the opposite side of the year. She runs a small sheep dairy on an island south of the mainland, and by early June her flock is settling into lambing, not milking. It's a rhythm we don't get to feature often, so we asked her what the last few weeks have actually looked like on the ground.

"Lambing is the calendar event down here in June," she told us. "Everything before it is prep, and everything after it is watching." Her operation ties milk supply directly to the lambing schedule, which means the cheese she's aging right now came from ewes that are mostly dried off for the season. It's a different kind of quiet than the summer rush most of our membership is heading into — less about managing volume and more about managing patience, checking on new lambs and letting the cave do its slow work on wheels that won't be ready to sell for months.

## A dry season complicating an old rhythm

This year the seasonal quiet has a harder edge to it. Her region has just come through its driest start to the year in more than a century, and a stretch of thin fodder supply has left milk volumes running below where they sat at the same point last year. She's had to make some early calls about herd size that she'd normally put off until later in the season. "We've had dry years before," she said, "but this one's asking us to decide things in June that we usually don't decide until September." Hay has been the sharpest cost — high demand against a short supply has pushed prices up across the region, and for a small operation that buys in supplementary feed rather than growing all of it, that line item alone has reshaped her spring budget. It's a version of the cost pressure a lot of our farmstead members have described this year, just arriving on a different clock than the one most of the federation runs on.

The dry conditions have also changed how she's thinking about the coming lambing season itself. Ewes going into lambing under feed stress need closer watching than usual, and she's added an extra round of paddock checks she wouldn't normally budget the time for. None of it is dramatic on its own — a shorter grazing rotation here, an earlier decision on which ewes to keep milking there — but added together it's meant a noticeably busier June than she's used to, even before the lambs are born.

What she wanted other members to hear isn't really a complaint about timing — it's a reminder that a fair number of us are working an entirely different season than the one implied by any given month's newsletter, on top of whatever else that season happens to be handing them this year. She's kept up with the federation mostly through recorded course sessions and the occasional early-morning video call with a course cohort sitting down to lunch on the other side of the planet, and she's watching this year's Annual Conference dates from a long way off, knowing the travel math for a trip like that rarely works for her operation.

## What we took from the conversation

If you're one of our smaller number of off-calendar members, we'd like to hear how your year actually runs against ours — reach out to the Membership & Outreach Committee and let them know. And if you're heading into your own busy stretch this month, it's worth a moment to remember that somewhere in the federation, this week looks like lambing pens, a feed budget rewritten twice, and a quiet cave — not a market stand and a full case.
