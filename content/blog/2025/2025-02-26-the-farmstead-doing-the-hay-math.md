Most weeks, we ask members to tell us about a cheese — a rind that finally set right, a wheel
that took a ribbon. This week we asked a farmstead producer with a twenty-cow herd in the upper
Midwest to walk us through a different document: the spreadsheet she opens before she opens her
affinage log.

"I used to do the milk math in my head," she told us. "Now I do the hay math first, because
that's the number that tells me whether the milk math even matters."

## The number behind the number

She milks a small mixed herd and makes a washed-rind and a young tomme, selling most of it
through two regional shops and a farmers' market stand from May through October. For years, feed
was a line item she checked monthly. This past year, she checks it against her milk price every
time she buys hay, because the gap between what her herd costs to feed and what her milk and
cheese bring in has gotten narrower than she's comfortable with.

Industry cost-of-production estimates for small herds have been circulating in exactly the
direction her own ledger shows — feed, fuel, and other input costs pushing the true cost of a
hundredweight of milk well above what many small operations are getting paid for it, even with
cheese sales layered on top. She isn't alone in watching that gap, and she isn't shy about saying
so on the members forum, where she's become one of the more regular voices in threads about
whether to expand a herd, hold steady, or scale back to something more sustainable for one or two
people to run.

"I'm not trying to scare anyone," she said. "I'm trying to make sure the people reading the forum
know the math changed under us, and it's worth rerunning your own numbers even if nothing about
your operation feels different day to day." Her approach since has been unglamorous, and she'd be
the first to say it isn't universally applicable: she locked in part of next winter's hay early,
at a price she could live with, even though it meant tying up cash sooner than she liked. She also
moved her tomme to a slightly larger format, which trims her labor cost per pound without changing
the recipe. Neither move fixes the underlying squeeze. Both make it survivable for another season.

## What she's asking of the federation

She's also become a regular at Membership & Outreach Committee calls — not as a leader, just a
member who shows up and says what a twenty-cow operation actually needs, whether that's a
resource on aging-space heating costs or a plainer explanation of how a margin-coverage program
applies to a herd her size. "The committee can't fix hay prices," she said. "But they can make
sure the resources we build actually match the size of farm most of us are running."

If your own feed line has been creeping past your milk check lately, you're not imagining it and
you're not behind — a lot of small operations are recalculating right now. The federation doesn't
have a fix for input costs, but the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a running list of
member-submitted resources on exactly this kind of cost pressure, and it's worth a look before
you make your own hay decision this spring.
