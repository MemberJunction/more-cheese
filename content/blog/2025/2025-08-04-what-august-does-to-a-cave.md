Every cave keeper has a month they dread, and for most of our members it's this one. A heat dome settled over much of the central and eastern country for stretches of this summer, and August brings the highest outdoor humidity of the year in a lot of our growing regions on top of it. That combination asks your compressor and your humidifier to work against each other instead of together — the compressor pulling moisture out to hold temperature, the humidifier trying to put it back to hold your target humidity for the rind you're growing. When that tug-of-war goes unmanaged, it shows up as a slick rind, a dry crust, or a wheel that ages faster or slower than its make sheet predicted.

A recent industry review of small-scale cave equipment made a point worth repeating here: a standalone humidifier is often the wrong tool for a room that's already fighting a heat load, because most humidifiers add heat right back into a space your compressor is working overtime to cool. If your cave runs hot and damp at the same time in August, that mismatch between two systems working against each other is frequently the actual cause, not a sign that you need a bigger compressor.

The good news is that August is a known problem with known fixes, not a mystery. If your readings have been drifting the last few weeks, here's where to start looking before you start second-guessing your make.

## Check the sensor before you check the cave

The single most common cause of a "the cave changed" complaint is a sensor that's drifted or been placed somewhere it's catching a draft from the compressor cycling. Before you adjust anything about your humidification setup, verify your hygrometer against a second reference instrument. A five-percent error in your reading can send you chasing a problem that doesn't exist, or ignoring one that does. This is basic enough that it's easy to skip, and skipping it is how members end up over-correcting a cave that was reading wrong, not running wrong.

Once you trust your numbers, look at airflow next, not humidity level. A cave that's technically at the right average humidity but has poor circulation will still show uneven results wheel to wheel — dry on the shelf nearest the vent, slick in the back corner. Small rooms are especially prone to this because there's less air volume to buffer a hot spot. Rotating shelf position on a set schedule, not just rotating the wheels themselves, is a cheap fix that a lot of small operations skip.

Salt can help buffer humidity swings in a pinch — a shallow tray or a salt block near a vent will pull excess moisture out of the air slowly rather than in the sharp swings a dehumidifier cycle can cause — but it's a stabilizer, not a fix for a room that's fundamentally undersized for its heat load.

## When it's worth a bigger conversation

If you're running a walk-in that was sized for a smaller operation than you've grown into, August heat load is often the month that exposes it — the compressor running near-constantly and never quite catching up. That's not a settings problem, and no amount of sensor calibration fixes it. If that's where you are, it's worth a real conversation about capacity before next summer rather than another season of fighting the same fight from June through September.

The Humidity & Airflow Control course covers this in more depth, including how to read a psychrometric chart for your specific room rather than relying on a rule of thumb built for a bigger cave than yours. And if you're troubleshooting something that doesn't match the usual August pattern — a sudden change rather than a seasonal drift — the Food Safety Committee's guidance on documentation is a good place to start before you assume it's just the weather.
