Most of our spotlights land on the people making or selling cheese. Renata Colby does neither. She books export shipments for a specialty cheese exporter at Pacific Rim Cheese Imports, which means her working week is spent on certificates, customs codes, and calendars — and this fall, on a paperwork backlog that had nothing to do with cheese and everything to do with her job anyway.

"People think exporting is the interesting part and the paperwork is the boring part," she told us. "The paperwork is the part that decides whether the interesting part happens on time."

## What actually got stuck

Renata's job depends on a chain of federal certifications and data releases that mostly run on a predictable schedule — predictable enough that she plans shipment dates around it months out. When the federal funding lapse hit in October, that chain didn't break so much as go quiet. "Nothing was denied," she said. "It just stopped moving. You'd call and the answer was 'we're working through a queue,' and nobody could tell you where in the queue you were."

She had three container shipments scheduled for late October that slid into November. None of them fell through, which she's quick to point out — this wasn't a crisis for her employer the way a tariff change or a spoiled shipment would have been. It was six weeks of not being able to promise a date to the buyer on the other end, and having to say so. Industry trade reporting from the same period has described dairy exporters generally working around shipping and documentation headwinds even as overseas demand for U.S. cheese held up — which matched what Renata was seeing on her end: the cheese was wanted, the paperwork was the bottleneck, not the buyer.

With the backlog clearing on a staggered schedule rather than all at once, Renata's approach for the next few months is to build in more slack than she used to. "I used to book a shipment date and work backward from it," she said. "Now I book a window and tell the buyer it's a window, not a date. That's a harder conversation up front and an easier one later."

She's also started checking two things she used to check once: the certificate status and the agency's posted processing estimate, rechecked closer to the ship date rather than trusted from when the paperwork was filed. "The estimate you get in week one isn't the estimate you'll get in week four," she said. "I'd rather look twice than explain to a buyer why the date moved again."

## Why we asked her about this

Most of what members hear about a federal paperwork backlog is the policy version — dockets, deadlines, agency notices. Renata's version is the version that actually shows up in a shipping calendar: dates that move, buyers who need an honest window instead of a confident wrong one, and a job that runs on a chain of approvals most of us never think about until a link in it goes quiet. She's been a federation member for four years, mostly to keep up with export documentation guidance through our Education Committee's course offerings, and she says this fall is the first time that guidance felt urgent instead of routine.

If your work touches export certification and you want a place to compare notes with other members doing the same job, our Export Documentation Essentials course has a member discussion thread that stays active well past the course dates — Renata's in it, and says it's where she heard other members were seeing the same slowdown before we'd confirmed it federation-wide.
