Happy new year. Whatever is sitting in your case this morning was ordered by a version of you making decisions weeks or months ago, before you knew how the holiday season would actually land. That's always the strange part of January 1 in this business — you're standing in the middle of someone else's bet, even when that someone was you.

The good news is that last year gave us a reasonably clear read on which bets paid off, and it's worth taking twenty minutes today, before the year gets busy, to write down what you actually learned rather than trusting yourself to remember it in March.

## What the year actually taught the case

Milk and feed costs stayed close enough together for most of last year that margin pressure eased off compared to the leaner stretch a couple of years back, which gave more members room to experiment with product mix instead of just defending what already worked. That's not a small thing — a member operating on a thin margin rarely has the appetite to swap out a reliable mover for something untested, and last year enough members had that appetite to give us a real read on what happened when they used it.

The shoppers who showed up kept reaching for cheeses with a more specific story than "imported" or "aged" — a particular region, a particular style, something a clerk could actually explain in two sentences rather than a label alone. That's not a dramatic pivot. It's a nudge in a direction most of you were already leaning, and the members who leaned into it hardest tended to report it holding shelf space better than what it replaced.

## Carrying that forward without overcorrecting

None of this means throw out what's working. A case built entirely around novelty is its own kind of fragile; you still need the reliable movers that pay the light bill in a slow month. The lesson from last year is narrower than that: when you're deciding what to cut to make room for something new, cut toward the generic end of the case first, and give the freed-up space to something regionally specific, even at a smaller order size than you'd normally commit to for an unproven item.

If margins stay steady into this year the way current projections suggest, you'll likely have a bit more room to try things than you did a couple of years ago. Use that room deliberately, in a quiet month when a misstep costs you a few dollars of shrink, rather than waiting until a busier one makes the same experiment more expensive to get wrong.

Whatever you decide, write it down somewhere you'll actually look at again in twelve months. The case you inherit next January should be built on this year's real numbers, not on what you remember feeling like it sold.

That's a smaller ask than it sounds. It doesn't need to be a spreadsheet with formulas, just a page in a notebook you already carry: what came out of the case this year, what replaced it, and whether the replacement earned its space. The federation can't do that accounting for you — every case is different, every regional market pulls a little differently — but we can tell you that the members who keep even a rough version of this record make faster, more confident calls the next time a slow mover needs replacing.
