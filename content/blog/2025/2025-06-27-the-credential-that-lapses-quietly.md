Of the seven credentials in the federation's ladder, the judge accreditation credential has the shortest clock — two years, against three or more for most of the others — and it's the one we hear about most often after it's already too late. A member emails a week before an event asking to confirm they're still accredited, and the honest answer is no, not since last spring, because the accreditation doesn't just expire on a date, it expires quietly.

That design is intentional, not an oversight. Judging is a skill that erodes without practice, and unlike a make procedure or a labeling rule, there's no way to look it up again the night before. The accreditation is built to track active judging, not just a passed exam, which is why it lapses faster than the credentials built around static knowledge.

## What keeps it active

Accreditation requires the Sensory Evaluation Certificate as a prerequisite, plus supervised panel experience logged within the two-year window — not a one-time qualifying panel, but ongoing rotation through actual competition judging. If you were accredited three years ago and haven't judged since, you're not accredited now, even if nobody told you. That's the part that catches people: there's no expiration notice, because the system is measuring activity, not a clock running down to a fixed date.

If your accreditation has lapsed, or you're not sure whether it has, the fastest way to check is your member portal credential page — it shows your last logged panel date, not just a pass/fail status. Renewing generally means getting back onto a supervised panel at a regional event before you can judge independently again, so if you're hoping to be on a panel at the Annual Conference in Sacramento next month, this is the week to sort that out, not the week you arrive.

## For members newer to judging

If you've never held the accreditation and want to start, the path runs through the Sensory Evaluation Certificate first, then the Judging & Competition Preparation course, which walks through scoring against style standards, defect identification, and the mechanics of panel rotation before you're paired with an accredited judge for supervised rounds. It's a slower on-ramp than some of our other credentials, and that's by design — a panel is only useful if everyone on it is calibrated to the same standard, and calibration takes repetition, not a single exam.

The course itself splits roughly evenly between two kinds of judging a panelist actually does. One is technical: identifying a defect, naming it correctly, and deducting for it consistently whether the wedge in front of you is a friend's cheese or a stranger's. The other is closer to taste itself — scoring what a style is supposed to taste like at its best and recognizing when a maker has hit that mark, even in a style you don't personally reach for on your own cheese counter. Members who've been through both halves say the technical side is the one that actually needs the course; most working cheesemakers already have strong opinions about what tastes good, but far fewer have practiced writing down exactly why in a way another judge would score the same way.

For those already accredited and current, no action needed beyond what you're already doing — though it's worth a quick look at your logged panel history regardless, since a portal error is more common than members expect and easier to fix in June than in the week before a competition. For everyone else, check the portal this week — before the conference, not during check-in.
