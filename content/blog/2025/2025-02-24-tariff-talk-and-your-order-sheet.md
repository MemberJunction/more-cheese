If your inbox looked anything like ours this week, half of it was distributors asking the same
question: what happens to my landed cost if a new round of tariffs actually takes effect. We
don't have a clean answer yet, and neither does anyone else — the announcements so far have been
about rate levels and target countries, not the finished paperwork importers use to price a
truck. But the direction is clear enough that it's worth getting ahead of it now instead of in
April.

## What we know today

New tariffs on goods from several major trading partners took effect or were announced earlier
this month, and further cheese-specific rates have been floated that would sit well above where
most import contracts were priced a year ago. For members who buy imported wheels — alpine
styles, blues, aged sheep's milk — the practical effect isn't abstract. A distributor who built a
spring case around a landed cost from last fall may be looking at a different number by the time
that container clears.

It isn't only finished cheese. Cultures, rennet, some packaging inputs, and specialty aging
supplies cross borders too, and a tariff line written for "dairy products" doesn't always
distinguish between a wheel of Gruyère and a case of affinage wraps. If your supply chain has any
import step in it — even one you don't think about because a domestic distributor handles it —
this is the month to ask them directly what changes and when. Three things we'd suggest before
your next order, none of them dramatic: ask your supplier for a landed-cost breakdown rather than
just a new price, so you know whether it's a one-time bump or something that compounds; check
your own price list against your last cost update, since a lot of members are still pricing from
a fall sheet; and line up a domestic supplier before you need one, so you're not shopping for one
under pressure. The membership directory is a faster way to find one than a cold search.

## What's different this time

None of this is new advice, exactly. It's the same discipline members have used through currency
swings and freight spikes for years. What's different this season is the pace — rate
announcements are landing faster than most order cycles turn over, which means the lag between
"we heard something changed" and "our price list reflects it" matters more than usual. We're not
going to publish a rate table here. Announced tariff levels have moved more than once already
this cycle, and a number we print today could be stale by the time you read it. If you need
something you can act on, your customs broker or import partner's own paperwork is the source of
truth — not a trade association's blog post. What we can do is keep pointing members to recent
coverage as it firms up, and we'll follow up here if the picture clarifies before the next
newsletter.

If your business runs on a thin import margin already, this is a good week to loop in whoever
handles your pricing and walk through the order sheet together, line by line, before the next
container is due.
