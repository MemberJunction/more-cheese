This week's spotlight is another composite rather than one member, because the pattern showed up across more than one committee seat this spring and it seemed more honest to describe it than to single someone out for it. A handful of our committee chairs took the gavel — such as it is, these are committees, not courtrooms — earlier this year after two years as members, and several of them told us some version of the same thing: the job they expected and the job they got weren't quite the same.

They expected protocols and testing cadence. What's actually landed on their desks is a mix of that and something closer to triage, shaped by a hard season for small, farmstead-scale operations. "I thought I'd spend my year on drafting language," one told us. "I've spent at least as much of it on calls with members where the plan is fine and the problem is that a fine plan costs money to run, and money is short this year."

## A queue shaped by two different pressures at once

Part of what's driving that is heightened attention to herd health and raw-milk handling this cycle, which has pushed more members to ask sharper questions about testing and contamination risk than committees have fielded in past years. The chairs we spoke with see that as a net good, even where it creates more work. "The biology hasn't changed," one said. "Milk-borne pathogens were always a real risk if handling slips somewhere in the chain. What's changed is that more members are asking about it before an inspector does, which is the direction you want that conversation to move."

The other pressure is financial, and it's reshaping the kind of question chairs are fielding. Feed and energy costs have squeezed exactly the operations that lean hardest on committee guidance, and more than one chair described being asked not "is this compliant" but "what's the minimum version of this that's still compliant." None of them love giving that answer, but they give it. "My job isn't to tell someone to spend money they don't have," one said. "It's to help them find the version of a plan that still protects the product and the customer, at whatever scale they're actually operating at right now. Sometimes that's a smaller ask than people expect. Sometimes it isn't, and that's a harder conversation — but it's still a conversation, not a lecture."

## What they'd tell the next person considering a seat

Asked what they'd say to a member weighing whether to put their name forward for a committee seat next cycle, the answer was consistent: do it before you feel ready, not after. Most of what a new chair actually uses in the first few months, several said, was picked up by watching a predecessor make calls, not by reading the bylaws in advance. And every one of them was careful to note that a chair doesn't rule alone — recommendations still go out under the full committee's name, arrived at together, not handed down.

If you're a member with a question this cycle — plan review, testing cadence, or what a "minimum viable" update to your food safety plan looks like on a tight budget — the relevant committee takes questions through the member portal. More than one chair told us the busier that queue gets, the more convinced they are that answering it is exactly where a trade federation is supposed to earn its keep.
