Our stretched-curd workshop wrapped in Querétaro earlier this month, and the feedback we've gotten back since has been unusually consistent for a hands-on session. Members who attended keep describing the same moment: the point in the workshop where the instructor stopped explaining temperature and time and just said, "now feel it change." Pasta filata is the stretched-curd family that gives you mozzarella and provolone, and the method is simple to describe on paper — soften the fresh curd in hot water or hot whey, usually somewhere in the 70 to 85 degree Celsius range, then knead and stretch it until the protein aligns into long fibrous strands. It's one of the few cheesemaking skills in our curriculum where that description and the physical sensation genuinely don't match up until you've done it with your own hands a few dozen times.

A farmstead producer who drove up from a smaller operation to attend told us afterward that she'd read the temperature and acidity windows correctly for two years and still couldn't get a consistent stretch until the workshop gave her something to compare it against in real time. "I knew the numbers," she said. "I didn't know what 'ready' actually felt like coming off my own hands until I watched someone else's curd do it wrong first." That's a common thread in the debrief notes: several attendees said watching a batch fail in front of them, and seeing exactly where it went sideways, taught them more than watching a perfect demonstration would have. The standard test the instructor kept coming back to is an old one — pull a piece of heated curd, fold it in on itself a few times, and read the texture. If it folds easily and holds together, it's ready. If it tears or crumbles, it needs more heat or more time, and now you know what that failure looks like before it happens on your own line.

## Why we keep running this one in person

Pasta filata is a hard skill to teach remotely, and it's the clearest example we have of why some of our workshops stay in-person even as more of the catalog moves online. You can watch a video of curd stretching correctly and still not know what "too cool" or "too acidic" feels like in your hands until you've held both. The workshop format gives members a controlled place to make that mistake once, cheaply, with an instructor standing next to them, instead of working it out alone on a batch meant for the case.

That's also why the workshop caps attendance smaller than most of our virtual sessions. Every attendee gets hands-on time at the vat, not just a seat in the room, and that only scales so far. If you've been on a waitlist for one of these before, our events calendar for early 2026 includes another round of hands-on workshops; watch for the announcement if stretched-curd styles are part of your lineup or something you're considering adding.

## What to bring if you sign up next time

Members who'd taken the workshop before offered the same advice to first-timers: bring a curd sample from your own make if the location allows it, even an imperfect one. Working a stretch on your own recipe's curd, rather than only the workshop's reference batch, is what several attendees said made the technique actually transfer back to their own cave and equipment once they got home. A stretch that works beautifully on the instructor's reference curd can behave completely differently on a curd with a different moisture content or a different acid development curve, and the gap between those two is exactly what members said they wished they'd understood going in.

If pasta filata styles are on your list for 2026, that's worth planning for before you travel, not after. Bring your own curd, bring your own questions about where your batches have gone wrong before, and expect to leave with a feel for the process rather than just a cleaner set of notes.
