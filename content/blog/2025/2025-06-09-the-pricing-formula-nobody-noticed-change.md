Two things happened to the price of milk this month, and neither one arrived with a press conference. A revised formula for how Class I fluid milk gets priced took effect, changing the skim-milk value calculation it's built on. At the same time, butter has spent four straight months at record highs while nearly every other major dairy commodity has softened. Members keep asking us which of these numbers actually matters to a cheesemaker. The honest answer is both, and not in the way you'd guess.

The pricing formula change is the quieter of the two, but it's the one worth understanding if you buy any milk at all rather than making entirely from your own herd. The new calculation compares Class III and Class IV skim values and uses the higher one, replacing an older method that had drifted out of step with how those markets actually move. Updated make allowances — the manufacturer-cost credits baked into the formula — also shifted, and for most of the country that meant a modest downward pressure on Class III and IV prices relative to the old rules. If your milk cost line looked slightly different on your June statement than you expected, this is very likely why. It's not a mistake. It's a recalculated floor.

## Butter is the tell, not the story

The record butterfat prices are getting more attention, and we understand why — a fourth consecutive month above $7,900 a ton is the kind of number that shows up in a supplier email even if you don't buy butter. But for most of our membership, high butterfat prices aren't really about butter. They're a signal about where milk components are getting pulled, and cream demand at that level tends to tighten butterfat availability for everyone downstream, cheesemakers included. A few members who buy cream or high-fat milk for triple-crèmes and other rich-paste styles have already told us their supplier conversations got shorter and their quotes got firmer this spring.

Meanwhile, the wider commodity picture is mixed enough that it doesn't support a single story. Global milk price indicators actually ticked down for the first time this year, export demand has softened in a few key markets, and yet U.S. cheese exports posted a record month in May. If you're trying to read all of that as one trend, you'll end up frustrated. It isn't one trend. It's several markets moving at once, some of them working in your favor and some of them not, and the formula change underneath all of it just made the starting line a little different than it was in May.

## What we'd actually do with this

If you buy milk on a federal order basis, pull your June settlement sheet and compare the skim value line to April or May — not to catch an error, but to understand your own new baseline before your next pricing conversation with a buyer or a distributor. If you're a farmstead operation making entirely from your own herd, the formula change matters less directly, but the butterfat squeeze on your input costs — feed, hauling, energy — is worth tracking against your own margin math, especially in a year where several of you have already told us those numbers are the tightest they've been in a decade.

We don't have a clean one-line takeaway here, and we're not going to manufacture one. The federation's Standards Committee is watching the make-allowance question as part of its ongoing input to the federal order process, and we'll keep members posted as anything firms up. In the meantime, if your bookkeeping needs a refresher on reading a component pricing statement, that's a conversation worth having with your own accountant, not a blog post.
