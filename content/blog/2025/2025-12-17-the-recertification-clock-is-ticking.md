Of the seven credentials in the federation's lineup, the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate has the shortest clock: two years, not three. That's deliberate. Practice in this area moves faster than affinage technique or sensory scoring does, and a certificate that hasn't been refreshed in three or four years can genuinely lag what an inspector expects to see on your floor today. If yours was issued in late 2023, it's either already lapsed or close enough that this is the week to check.

We built the shorter window around a simple observation: the members who stay current on food safety treat recertification as routine maintenance, not a scramble. December is a natural checkpoint for that, mostly because it's the one month a year when most operations sit down and look at what needs to be renewed, replaced or reordered before January anyway.

## The recordkeeping requirement that just moved

This has been a live year for exactly the kind of rule that recertification is supposed to keep you ahead of. A federal traceability recordkeeping requirement for higher-risk foods, first finalized a few years back, had been on track to take effect in January 2026. This fall, the compliance deadline was pushed back roughly two and a half years, to mid-2028, giving processors more runway to build the systems the rule requires. If your creamery had been scrambling to get lot-tracking and critical-tracking-event logs in place before January, you now have more time — but "more time" is not the same as "no longer your problem." The requirement itself didn't go away, and members who stop paying attention to it now are the ones who'll be scrambling again in 2028 instead of building the habit gradually.

This is exactly the kind of development our recertification coursework is built to surface: which requirements are live, which are extended, and what recordkeeping habit you should be building regardless of the exact deadline. A shipping and receiving log that already captures lot numbers, dates and quantities in one place is worth building now, on your own timeline, rather than assembling from scratch under a compliance deadline later.

## Booking it before the calendar turns

The recertification course runs as a compact, practical review rather than a full re-sit: hazard analysis, environmental monitoring, recall readiness, and this cycle, an updated module on what the traceability recordkeeping extension does and doesn't change for a small or mid-sized operation. Members tell us the most useful part isn't the material they already knew — it's the portion at the end where the instructor works through what's actually different since they last sat for it.

If you're not sure when your certificate expires, that's worth five minutes to check before the holiday closure rather than after. A lapsed credential doesn't cost you anything dramatic the day it expires, but it does mean you're one records request away from an awkward conversation with a buyer or an inspector who asks for it. And if your operation is still building out lot-tracking records ahead of the extended deadline, the recertification course is a reasonable place to start that work rather than treating it as a separate project.

Reach out to the Food Safety Committee if your renewal window doesn't line up with the course calendar — they can usually point you to the next open cohort, and they're the group tracking how the traceability requirement continues to move as 2026 opens.
