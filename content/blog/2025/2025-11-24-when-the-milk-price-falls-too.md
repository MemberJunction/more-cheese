For most of this year, the conversation among members has been about what's going up: feed, energy, insurance, everything that sits between a cow and a cave. This month the story flipped, and it's not the relief you'd expect. Milk prices are falling, and for a good number of small producers, that's its own kind of problem.

The wholesale numbers back it up. The international dairy auction index has now posted its seventh straight decline, the lowest reading in over a year, with cheddar and mozzarella both down roughly three percent in the most recent round. Milk supply has been outpacing demand for a few months, and forecasters expect that gap to persist well into next year rather than close quickly.

## Why cheaper milk isn't automatically good news

If you buy milk to make cheese, a falling price can look like margin room. If you *are* the dairy supplying it, the same number is your paycheck. A lot of our farmstead members are both at once, and the math doesn't simplify the way it would for a pure buyer or a pure seller. Feed costs have eased some this fall too, which helps, but not by enough to offset a milk price that's dropped this much this fast for anyone whose production side outweighs their processing side.

That's part of why federal margin coverage hasn't been doing much for most members this year. The program tracks the gap between a national milk price and a national feed cost index, and that gap has stayed wider than the trigger level for most of 2025 even as individual members felt real pressure — because the program measures purchased feed costs, not what it actually costs a small operation to grow or source its own hay and grain locally. A falling milk price without a matching drop in local costs can make that gap look fine on paper while it doesn't feel fine on the ledger.

## What to actually check right now

If you haven't looked at your own numbers against the national figures recently, this is a good week to do it. Pull your last few milk price statements next to your actual feed and energy spend, not the published national average, and see how far apart they've drifted. If you're a wholesale buyer, this is also a reasonable moment to check in with your suppliers rather than just enjoying a lower invoice — a supplier squeezed on the sell side and the cost side at once is not a supplier you want surprising you with a shortfall in March.

We don't have a tidy fix to offer here, and any post that told you one would be selling something. What we can say is that the direction of the milk price and the direction of your own margin are not the same chart this year, and the members handling this well are the ones checking both instead of assuming a falling input means a falling cost.
