A trade report out this spring looked at what actually moves specialty cheese off a deli shelf, and the answer wasn't pricing, packaging, or a clever shelf tag. It was sampling, trial-size formats, and plain shopper education — a taste, a small enough commitment to try something unfamiliar, and someone nearby who can answer a question about it. None of that is a surprising finding to anyone who's worked a cheese counter for more than a season, but it's useful to see it confirmed at the trade-research level rather than just as counter folklore, especially in a year where several members have told us margin is tighter than it's been in a decade.

That gap between "we already knew this" and "someone measured it" matters more than it might seem. When a specialty producer or a small retail account is deciding where to put a limited marketing budget this year, it's easier to justify a sampling program or a trial-size SKU when there's research behind it, not just a counter manager's instinct. If you've been on the fence about adding a smaller-format offering — a two-ounce wedge instead of committing a shopper to a half-pound — this is a reasonable season to try it.

## Why this lands differently in a tight-margin year

We'd normally file a report like this under general retail interest and move on, but the timing matters this year specifically. Several farmstead and small-batch members have told us this spring was their tightest margin stretch in a decade, driven by feed costs, energy bills, and hauling — the input side of the ledger, not the sales side. A finding that says the sales side of the business responds well to relatively low-cost tactics — sampling, small formats, a knowledgeable person at the counter — is genuinely good news for a member who can't spend much on marketing right now but can spend an afternoon cutting sample-size pieces.

It's also a finding that favors smaller producers more than it favors scale. A large specialty operation selling through a deli program it doesn't staff directly can't easily put a person at every case to answer a question. A member running their own counter, or working a market stand personally, already has exactly the ingredient the research says matters most: someone who knows the cheese and can talk about it in the thirty seconds a shopper is willing to give it. If you've felt like you're competing against bigger producers on price or shelf space, this is a place where you're not at a disadvantage — you may actually have the edge.

## What we'd try this summer

If you're weighing where to put limited time or budget before the fall wholesale season, this points toward a few concrete, low-cost moves: a standing sample plate at your counter or market stand rather than an occasional one, a smaller trial format for your slower-moving styles, and making sure whoever's behind the counter can say something specific about each cheese rather than a general "it's good." None of that requires new equipment or a marketing consultant. It requires deciding it's worth the extra ten minutes of prep before your shift starts, and members who've made that switch tell us it shows up in repeat customers faster than they expected.
