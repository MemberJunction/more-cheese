Most of our member spotlights land on someone at a creamery or a counter. This one is about a member who sits between both: a regional sales representative for a specialty dairy distributor, twelve years into the job, covering an account territory that stretches across several states. She asked that we not use her name or her employer's, since some of what she told us touches accounts she's still actively managing, but she was glad to talk about the middle seat she's occupied all fall.

"I'm the one who hears it from both directions," she said. "A producer tells me their costs are up and they need a better price than we agreed on in the spring. A grocer tells me their shelf space for specialty cheese isn't growing and they can't take a price increase without cutting volume somewhere else. I don't set either number. I'm just the person in the room for both conversations."

## What this fall has actually looked like

Milk production nationally has been running higher even as the number of dairy operations keeps shrinking, which is not the contradiction it sounds like — fewer, larger operations producing more per farm. For a rep working with small farmstead accounts specifically, though, that national picture doesn't translate cleanly. "The big co-ops aren't who I'm calling on," she said. "My producers are smaller, and for them this year has mostly been about costs that didn't come down the way milk prices sometimes do. Feed, energy, all of it stayed expensive even in a month when the wholesale number softened a little."

She's had two long-standing producer accounts tell her this fall that they're not sure they'll be shipping to her next year, and she's had to have the same conversation with the grocers who buy from them: a line they've carried for years may not be there in six months. "That's not a sales problem I can solve with a better pitch," she said. "It's just information I have to pass along accurately in both directions, even when neither side wants to hear it."

The regional split makes it harder to generalize, too. She's heard from colleagues in other territories whose producer accounts are doing fine this year — better than fine, in a couple of cases — while her own patch has been the one absorbing the pressure. "It's not one story nationally," she said. "It's a different story by region, sometimes by county, and I think that gets lost when people talk about 'the dairy industry' like it's one thing having one kind of year."

## Why she stays a member

"The value for me isn't a discount code," she said. "It's that when I'm sitting across from a producer who's stretched, I want to be able to point them at something real — a course, a committee, a person who's dealt with exactly their situation — instead of just sympathy. That's the membership doing its job, even when it's not doing anything for me directly."

She's started sending smaller producer accounts toward the federation's membership and course offerings more directly this year than she used to, partly because she doesn't have a better answer for "what do I do now" and partly because she's seen it help before. If you're on the distribution or wholesale side and want to talk through what you're seeing in your own territory, the Membership & Outreach Committee is compiling exactly these accounts this year — a note through the member portal reaches them directly.
