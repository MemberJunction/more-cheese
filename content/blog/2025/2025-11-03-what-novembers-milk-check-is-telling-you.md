The federal milk prices for October came in lower across the board, and for members who buy milk rather than produce it, that sounds like good news until you look at what's driving it. Production is up, not demand — the national herd and per-cow output have both been climbing toward a multi-decade high, and the price relief on your ingredient line is the flip side of a farmgate check that's fallen sharply from where it sat a year ago. If you're a farmstead operation, this is the same number from the other direction, and it isn't a comfortable one.

For members tracking the federal support formula, the reassurance is that margins have stayed above the subsidy trigger for most of the year. That's real, and it matters. But "above the trigger" and "comfortable" are different claims, and several members who called the office this week made the same point: a milk price that's down several dollars from last October changes the math on expansion, on hired help, on whether next spring's calves get raised or sold. None of that shows up in the trigger calculation, which only tracks a national feed-cost average and misses what homegrown feed actually costs a given farm in a given year.

## Two different problems wearing the same headline

If you're buying milk for a wholesale cheese operation, lower Class prices are a genuine break on your input costs going into the holiday season — worth banking, not worth assuming will last. Milk pricing has swung the other direction fast before, and a case built around today's cost won't forgive you for not padding it.

If you're a farmstead making your own cheese from your own herd, the same number is a warning about 2026, not a relief. We've heard from more than one small producer this fall who is already recalculating whether the herd needs to shrink before the spring flush, rather than after it, the way that decision usually gets made. That's a harder conversation to have in November than in March, and we'd rather members have it now, with time to plan, than get forced into it later.

Either way, the number is the same and the response isn't. If your make sheet or your feed budget hasn't been rebuilt since summer, this is the week to do it — the input side of your business just moved, whichever side of the milk price you sit on.

There's a second wrinkle worth flagging for members who sell direct rather than through a co-op check. A lower Class III or Class IV number doesn't automatically show up in what a distributor or a wholesale buyer offers you for finished wheels — that price gets negotiated separately, and it tends to move slower and less transparently than the underlying milk market. If a buyer cites "the milk price is down" as a reason to renegotiate your wholesale rate this month, it's worth asking exactly which number they mean and over what period, rather than accepting the framing at face value.

## What we're doing about it

The federation doesn't set milk prices and won't pretend to have a forecast better than the industry's own analysts, who themselves keep revising production estimates upward. What we can do is keep the pricing and margin data current on the member portal, and we've updated it this week to reflect the October figures. If your own numbers don't match what you're seeing on your milk check, the Membership & Outreach Committee can usually help you figure out why — sometimes it's a component pricing detail, sometimes it's a hauling deduction nobody explained clearly. Either way, it's worth asking before you assume the worst, and it's worth doing before your December budget gets finalized rather than after.
