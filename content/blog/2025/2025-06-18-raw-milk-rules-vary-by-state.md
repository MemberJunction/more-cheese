A handful of states moved on raw-milk law this year, and the changes don't point in one direction. Arkansas signed a bill this spring letting raw goat, sheep, and whole milk move from the farm to a farmers' market stand or straight to a customer's door by delivery, where before it could only be sold on-site. North Dakota's legislature has been advancing its own expansion, extending raw-milk provisions to cover raw milk products like cream, cheese, and yogurt sold direct to a consumer, while keeping wholesale and retail sale off the table. Other states are still working through proposed testing and permitting rules. If you make an aged raw-milk cheese and you've been operating on what you learned five years ago, it's worth checking your state's current rule rather than assuming it hasn't moved.

This matters to more of our membership than the raw-milk-only producers. Federal rule still requires that milk sold across state lines be pasteurized, so the "raw-milk aging" question that our Food Safety Committee tracks most closely is really about the 60-day aging exemption that lets certain raw-milk cheeses made and matured domestically avoid that requirement — and states differ on how they license, test, and document a creamery working under it. A cheese aged to that standard in one state does not automatically travel the same way into a state with a different testing regime. Getting this wrong isn't a paperwork inconvenience; it can mean a batch that can't legally leave your cave.

## What the federation is doing

Our Food Safety Committee, chaired this term by member Gwen Whitfield, has spent the spring compiling a state-by-state comparison for exactly this reason — not to lobby for one uniform national standard, since our members are genuinely split on whether that would help or hurt them, but so a member weighing a move, an expansion, or a new sales channel can see what actually changed and where. A short letter-writing push on aging-rule clarity went out to members in a handful of states this spring, and several of you signed on. Thank you for that. It matters more than a single letter usually feels like it does, because state agriculture committees do track constituent volume on a topic like this one.

We also want to name something plainly: this is not guidance on whether raw-milk cheese is safe to make or sell in your specific situation. That determination sits with your state regulator and, where relevant, your own food-safety counsel — not with us, and not with a blog post. What we can do is keep the comparison current and make sure the committee's advocacy reflects what members are actually telling us they need, which this spring has mostly been clarity, not a particular policy outcome.

## If you want in

If your state touched raw-milk or aging rules this year and you haven't told us, we want to hear it — it's exactly the kind of on-the-ground detail that keeps our comparison accurate between updates.
