We've had more questions about the Certified Cheesemonger credential this spring than any other in our lineup, and most of them come with some version of the same assumption: that it's a polish credential, something a counter clerk picks up to sound a little sharper with customers. That's not wrong, exactly, but it undersells what's actually being tested.

The credential is built around the judgment calls a working cheesemonger makes dozens of times a day, not trivia. Anyone can memorize that a washed-rind cheese smells strong; the exam wants to know whether you can taste a wheel, place it correctly against the rest of a case, and talk a customer through why it's priced the way it is without either oversimplifying or losing them.

## The three things it actually checks

Sensory judgment comes first — tasting and describing cheese accurately enough that another professional could find the same wheel from your description alone. That's harder than it sounds under exam conditions, and it's the section where prepared candidates most often say they were surprised by how specific the questions got.

Case and storage knowledge is the second piece: knowing which cheeses can sit next to each other, which need their own humidity, and how to read a wheel that's starting to go past its window before a customer notices. This is where the credential earns its keep for a retail business — a monger who can catch a cheese drifting a day or two early is worth more than the certificate fee many times over across a year.

The third is customer-facing judgment: pairing recommendations, substitution suggestions when something's out of stock, and — this part doesn't get talked about enough — knowing when to say a cheese isn't right for what a customer's describing rather than making a sale anyway. Members who've hired credentialed staff tell us that's the piece that actually shows up in repeat business.

## Why the interest is spiking now

Part of it is straightforward: a year where several small creameries have scaled back or closed means fewer new artisan lines landing on some counters, and retailers are leaning harder on staff who can make an existing case work — cross-selling adjacent styles, keeping customers engaged with what's actually available rather than what's not. A well-trained monger does more with a smaller, tighter case than an untrained one does with a full one.

The credential doesn't require a prerequisite course, though most candidates who've come through our Sensory Evaluation Certificate first tell us the tasting section felt considerably less foreign. Either path works; it's really a question of whether you want the sensory vocabulary built up gradually or want to go straight at the full credential and backfill what you're missing. If you manage counter staff, the Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding group-enrollment questions all month, and there's real value in certifying more than one person from the same case at once — they end up comparing notes on tasting descriptions, which sharpens both of them faster than studying alone would. If you're the one behind the counter and wondering whether it's worth your own time, look at your last month of customer questions you couldn't answer with total confidence. That's usually the honest answer.
