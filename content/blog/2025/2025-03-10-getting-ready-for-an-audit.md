Some member stories start with an event. This one starts with an email that didn't have a date in it. A quality manager at a small farmstead creamery in our membership — she asked us not to use her name or her creamery's, since the audit in question hadn't happened yet at the time we spoke — got a note from her largest wholesale account in February saying a food-safety audit was "likely in the coming weeks." No date, no checklist, just enough warning to make her stomach drop and not enough to plan around.

## What she actually did with three weeks of uncertainty

"My first instinct was to reread our whole HACCP plan cover to cover, which took an evening and told me almost nothing useful," she told us. "The plan was fine. What I actually needed to check was whether we'd been *following* the plan, which is a different question entirely." She spent the next several days pulling records instead of rereading policy: temperature logs, cleaning schedules, corrective-action notes from the last six months. Two gaps turned up — a week in December where a log had been filled in from memory two days late, and a sanitation step that had quietly drifted from written procedure once a part-time hire took it over.

Neither gap was dangerous on its own. Both were exactly the kind of thing an auditor looks for, because a plan that looks perfect on paper and doesn't match daily practice is a bigger red flag than an honest gap that's been caught and corrected. "I'd rather show up to an audit with two things I fixed in February than zero things, because zero things usually means nobody was actually checking," she said.

## The part that surprised her

What she didn't expect was how much the uncertainty itself became the hard part, separate from the audit. "You can't fully plan your week around a visit that might be next Tuesday or might be five weeks from now. I found myself doing a mini version of audit prep almost every day, just in case, which is exhausting in a way that a scheduled audit with a firm date wouldn't have been." She's since built a standing weekly half-hour where she checks one section of the plan against actual practice, regardless of whether an audit is looming — a habit she says she should have had in place already, audit or not.

Her advice to other quality managers reading this with their own version of an unscheduled-audit email sitting in their inbox: don't reread the plan first. Pull the records first, find what's actually drifted, fix what you can fix now, and document that you fixed it. A plan is a promise; records are the proof you kept it. If your creamery doesn't have a habit of periodic self-checks between formal audits, the Food Safety Committee's office hours are a reasonable place to start building one, before the next email like hers lands in someone else's inbox.

It's worth remembering that this kind of visit isn't punitive by design. A wholesale account sending its own auditor, rather than relying only on a regulator's inspection schedule, is a fairly standard part of how larger buyers manage risk across a supply chain built from dozens of small producers they don't see day to day. From the buyer's side, it's less about catching any one creamery doing something wrong and more about confirming that the paperwork and the practice line up everywhere they source from. That reframing helped her some, she said, though it didn't make the three weeks of waiting any shorter.

The audit itself, when it finally happened in early March, ran about ninety minutes and covered exactly the two areas she'd already caught and fixed, plus several she'd expected and had ready. She passed. "I don't think I would have, six months ago, before I started doing the weekly check-ins," she told us. "Not because anything was secretly wrong back then, but because I wouldn't have known where to look fast enough." For a membership where feed and energy costs have already made this a harder year than most to absorb a failed audit and a lost account, that kind of quiet preparedness is worth more than it usually gets credit for.
