A few members mentioned it independently this week, in the way a real pattern tends to surface: the hot stretch in late June hit their cows before it hit the thermometer on the porch. Lower intakes, softer yields, cows standing at the water trough instead of the feed bunk. Research published this month puts a hard number on what they were describing. A single day of extreme heat can cut milk production by as much as ten percent, and the effect doesn't clear when the temperature does — yield stays depressed for more than a week afterward, even on farms running fans and sprinklers. Cooling systems only claw back about half the loss.

That second part is the one worth sitting with if you run a smaller operation. The research is consistent with what federation members in tighter-margin regions have been reporting for two summers now: farms with fewer than a hundred cows lose a larger share of their annual yield to heat than larger operations do, even with comparable cooling investment. It isn't that small farms manage heat worse. It's that a percentage point of lost yield lands harder on a herd size where there's less volume underneath it to absorb the hit.

## Where it shows up on your sheet

The federal margin-support program that many members enroll in ties eligibility to the gap between milk price and a feed cost index, and that index has been sensitive to corn and soybean meal prices running higher than average since last year. A hot week that trims yield doesn't just cost you pounds shipped — it can shift where you sit relative to your coverage threshold for the month, especially if you're already watching that gap closely. If you haven't looked at your elected coverage level since you signed up, this is a reasonable week to pull it back out. A lot of members set it once at enrollment and don't revisit it until a bad month forces the question.

None of this is a case for a particular cooling technology or a particular coverage level — that math is specific to your herd, your barn, and your milk contract, and it isn't something we're in a position to tell you from here. What we can tell you is that the timing lines up with what members are reporting, and that the federation's Cave Management course covers airflow and thermal load planning for aging spaces, which is a related but distinct problem from herd-side heat stress and one that catches people off guard in a summer like this one. A cave that runs warm for two weeks doesn't announce itself the way a barn does.

## The demand side isn't compensating

It's worth noting what's not happening at the same time: specialty and artisan cheese sales are still growing this year, with deli case dollars up meaningfully over last year. That's a real tailwind, but it's a retail-side number, and it doesn't offset a production-side cost. A creamery that's shipping less milk and paying more to keep a cave in range isn't made whole by strong sell-through at a counter three states away. If your make schedule has been thinner than usual this month, you are not alone in that, and it is not evidence you're doing anything wrong.

If a hot week has pushed your numbers somewhere uncomfortable, the Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded enough of these calls this summer to have a reasonable sense of what other members in your region are doing about it. That's not a substitute for your own accountant or your own vet, but it's a place to start a conversation that doesn't feel like you're the only one having it.
