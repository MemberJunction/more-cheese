A rule that has been sitting in a federal queue for roughly twenty years is finally moving, and it touches a question more members ask us about than you'd expect: what counts as "milk" on a standardized cheese label when the milk has been ultrafiltered or microfiltered before it ever reaches the vat. The federal food-labeling regulator is working toward a decision on whether cheesemakers using those processes need to call it out separately on the label, or whether it can simply be counted as milk the way it has been treated in practice for years. It's one of several labeling questions in motion right now — the same regulator is also weighing new front-of-package nutrition labeling with a comment period that closes this spring, so label real estate is getting scrutinized from more than one direction at once.

If that sounds like a technical question that belongs to bigger operations with membrane filtration equipment, it partly is. But the standards written for the largest cheesemakers in the country apply, word for word, to a two-person creamery making a standardized style — dozens of legal cheese categories carry definitions that don't currently distinguish based on batch size. A labeling change here doesn't just affect who has to reprint a label. It touches how a standardized cheese gets defined at all, and any exemption written broadly enough to help a large processor could just as easily help, or quietly complicate, a small one.

## Why this is on our list

Our Standards Committee has been tracking this proposal since it reopened, and the plain-language version of what's being decided is this: should fluid ultrafiltered or microfiltered milk be exempt from separate labeling when it's used alongside ordinary milk in a standardized cheese, the way whole milk and skim milk are already treated as interchangeable under most standards. An exemption would mean less label space spent on a processing detail most consumers wouldn't parse anyway. No exemption means more disclosure, which some members will welcome and others will read as one more line item on an already crowded label. We don't have a house position on which outcome is better for every member — a small-batch maker who never touches filtered milk has a different stake in this than a member whose supplier does use it upstream.

What we do have is a Standards Committee that wants your actual practice, not your guess about the policy. If you use ultrafiltered or microfiltered milk anywhere in your supply chain, or if your label already carries language about milk processing you're not sure is still required, that's exactly the kind of detail that makes a comment letter useful instead of generic. A two-paragraph note describing what you make, whether either process touches any part of it, and what a labeling change would cost you in practice — reprinting packaging, relabeling existing stock, or nothing at all — is more useful to the committee than a position on the policy itself. We'll fold specific, dated member input into the comment the federation eventually files, and we'll credit the pattern of responses, not any one member's name, unless you tell us otherwise.

This is a slower-moving story than a tariff schedule or a milk-price forecast, which is exactly why it's easy to let slide. Rules that have been pending for two decades don't usually move quickly, but they also don't usually stay open for comment forever. If your label already says something about milk sourcing or processing, now is a good time to reread it with this question in mind, and to send the Standards Committee a note before the window narrows.

Members with label language questions can reach the Standards Committee directly, and we'll keep the membership posted as the timeline firms up.
