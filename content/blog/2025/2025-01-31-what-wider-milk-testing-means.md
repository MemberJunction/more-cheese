Earlier this month, the federal milk testing program aimed at dairy-herd surveillance added a batch of new participating states, bringing more of the country's milk supply under routine sampling than at any point since the program started. We're not going to interpret what that means for animal health policy — that's a federal and state veterinary question, not a trade association's to answer. What we can do is help you think through what it means for your own herd and processing records, because that part is squarely ours.

Federal agencies have also confirmed detections tied to the surveillance work this winter, which is part of why member questions about recordkeeping have picked up. We mention that only as background for why the topic is live right now, not because there's anything actionable in it beyond what your own veterinarian and state agriculture office would already be telling you.

## Two kinds of paperwork, one drawer

Most small operations keep herd health records and processing records as though they're unrelated — one binder for the vet's visits, one for pasteurization logs and cleaning schedules. An expanded testing environment is a good prompt to check that both are actually current, because a state inspector or a buyer asking about your biosecurity practices this spring is going to want to see that the two tell a consistent story. If your herd health log hasn't been updated since the fall, or your HACCP plan still references a supplier you switched away from, this is the week to fix that, not the week a question makes you go looking for it.

The Food Safety Committee has flagged this exact gap as a recurring theme in member questions this winter: not confusion about the testing program itself, but uncertainty about whether existing internal records would hold up if someone asked to see them. That's a fixable problem, and fixing it doesn't require guessing at policy — it requires an afternoon with your own binder and a willingness to admit which pages are stale.

If you've never sat down and compared your two logs side by side, start small. Pick one supplier, one monitoring point, and one corrective action from the last quarter, and trace whether the paper trail actually supports the story you'd tell an inspector out loud. Gaps tend to show up fast once you're looking for them on purpose instead of assuming they aren't there.

Pull your HACCP plan and your herd health log side by side this week and check that the dates on both are recent. It's a smaller job in January than it will be once spring production ramps up.
