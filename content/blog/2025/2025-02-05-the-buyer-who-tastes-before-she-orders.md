We're describing a composite this week rather than naming one member, because the story is common enough among our specialty buyers that picking a single name would undersell how many of you are doing exactly this. The buyer at the center of it works the case at a member-owned specialty shop, and she still tastes every order herself before it goes on the shelf — including the lines she's reordered a dozen times before.

"A wheel isn't the same wheel every time," she told us. "Same producer, same recipe, different cave, different week — it's not always dramatic, but it's there. If I stop tasting, I stop knowing that."

## What actually changes an order

We asked what's shifted in her buying over the past few years, and authenticity came up fast. Trade coverage this winter has pointed to a split in what shoppers want from a cheese case: roughly equal appetite for the classics they already trust and for anything that can tell a genuine, old-world story, with bold, less-familiar flavors also pulling real growth. She reads that as validation of something she's believed for a while — that a small producer's advantage was never going to be price or volume. "A customer isn't comparing my case to my invoice cost," she said. "They're comparing it to whatever's next to it, and what wins there is usually the one with an actual story behind it, not the one that's cheapest." Her answer is to make sure every wheel she carries has a story a mass-market line can't tell — who made it, what herd it came from, why it's on her shelf this month and not last month. It helps that wholesale prices for the styles she buys most have been moving in her favor lately, even as the broader global commodity index has drifted down — cheddar and mozzarella both firmed at the most recent international auction, which gives her a little more room to pay a fair price to a small producer without squeezing her own margin to do it.

That's part of why she says the number she actually watches isn't the first sale, it's the second one. "Anyone will try something once because it's new. Whether they come back for it is the real signal, and that's slower than any report." A rotating case slot for a newer or smaller producer costs her some certainty — an established line will move predictably, a new one might not — but she treats that as the cost of keeping the case honest rather than a risk to avoid. She's also watching the same wellness-leaning interest the trade press has flagged this winter, naturally low-fat and higher-protein styles gaining a little traction; she doesn't chase every trend into the case, but she'll give a producer working in that direction a longer look than she used to.

## What she watches, and what she wants from a pitch

The public-facing part of buying is the case, the tastings, the conversation with a customer trying something new. The less visible part is the relationship work behind it: checking in with producers between orders, tracking which wheels are aging on schedule, adjusting for a supplier's slow month without panicking about the gap on the shelf. "You can taste the difference between a producer who has a real system and one who's improvising," she said. "It shows up in the rind before it shows up in the flavor."

If you're a producer wondering what a buyer like her actually wants from an introduction, her answer was simple: send a sample before you send a sales pitch, and be honest about what the cheese isn't good at yet. "I've bought from people who told me their wheel had a flaw before I found it myself," she said. "That's who I call back first."
