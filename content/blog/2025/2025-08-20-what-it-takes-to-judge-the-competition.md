Every year, a handful of members ask the Awards & Competition Committee some version of the same question after the results come out: how does someone end up on that panel? The short answer is Competition Judge Accreditation, one of our seven credentials and, by our own admission, one of the least understood. Most members assume it's for people angling to judge a category at next year's competition. Some of them are right. But the credential shows up in more places than the judging table, and this is a good time of year to think about whether it belongs on your list — enrollment for the fall cohort opens shortly after the results from this year's competition are finalized.

## What the credential actually tests

Accreditation isn't a pass built on opinion. Candidates work through calibrated scoring exercises against reference wheels selected for known, deliberate flaws — an underdeveloped rind, an off-balance acid curve, a texture defect introduced on purpose — and are evaluated on how consistently their scores line up with a panel of established judges working the same samples blind. The goal isn't to produce judges who all like the same cheese. It's to produce judges whose scoring is internally consistent and defensible against a shared rubric, category by category: Alpine Styles, Washed Rind, Soft-Ripened, Blue Veined, Fresh & Pasta Filata, and Aged Cheddar & Territorials each carry their own specific defect vocabulary and scoring emphasis, and the coursework walks through the differences rather than treating "good cheese" as one universal standard.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. A judge who scores every category through an Alpine-styles lens will consistently undervalue a well-made fresh cheese for characteristics it was never trying to have. Accreditation exists to catch exactly that kind of miscalibration before it reaches a scoresheet that affects a real producer's result.

## Who uses it besides judges

This is the part that surprises people. Members who've completed accreditation tell us the sharpest change isn't in how they judge — it's in how they evaluate their own product before it ever leaves the cave. Learning to score against a defect rubric instead of a personal preference makes it considerably easier to catch a problem in your own wheel early, before a distributor or a competition does it for you. A handful of quality managers at larger creamery operations have pursued the credential specifically for that internal use, not because they have any interest in judging someone else's cheese.

It also matters for anyone training newer staff on sensory evaluation. A vague "this doesn't taste right" from an experienced maker is hard to teach forward. A specific defect vocabulary, applied consistently, is something a new hire can actually learn from and apply the next time a batch comes out of the vat looking a little off.

Getting started is straightforward. Competition Judge Accreditation builds on the Sensory Evaluation Certificate, so if you haven't completed that one yet, start there — the reference-standard vocabulary it teaches is the foundation the judging calibration exercises assume you already have. If you have completed it and you're weighing whether accreditation is worth the fall cohort's time, ask yourself whether you want the credential for the judging table, for your own quality process, or both — the coursework serves either goal well, but knowing which one you're after will change how you use it afterward.
