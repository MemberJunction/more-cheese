If you've tried to pull a federal milk production or crop report this month and come up empty, you're not doing anything wrong. A funding lapse that began October 1 has paused a chunk of the statistical work the dairy sector normally checks every week — supply and demand estimates, export tallies, some of the price series that feed directly into contract language. The people who compile those numbers are furloughed along with everyone else, and the agencies involved have said plainly that a few of the regular reports won't reappear until November, on a delayed schedule that's still being finalized as we write this.

For members who price by the wheel or the case, that's more than an inconvenience. A lot of contracts, farm-loan paperwork, and internal budgeting cycles lean on figures that assume the data keeps arriving on a predictable schedule. It hasn't, this month, and we'd rather say that clearly than let members find out the hard way when a number they were counting on simply isn't there when they go looking for it.

## What's still visible

Not everything went dark. Private market-news services and a handful of state-level programs have kept publishing straight through the lapse, and this month's numbers, where we could get them, aren't dramatic. Mailbox milk prices ticked down modestly from September, in line with the seasonal pattern of stronger fall production easing prices a little as processors build stock ahead of the holidays. That's a familiar rhythm, not a new one, and it tracks with what most members who've been in the trade a while would expect for this point in the calendar.

Export figures through the most recent complete reporting period showed continued strength — U.S. dairy exports, cheese included, were running well ahead of last year's pace by value, which is worth knowing if your creamery sells into any of that pipeline, whether directly or through a distributor who does. Retail milk prices, tracked separately from the paused federal reports, also held roughly steady through the month. None of this tells you everything a normal month of complete reporting would, but taken together it's enough to say the underlying market hasn't lurched in either direction. The silence itself is mostly a reporting problem right now, not, as far as we can tell, a demand problem.

## What to do without the rest

If your business plans around a specific federal release — a pricing formula tied to a class price series, a loan application that wants current production figures on file — start a conversation with whoever's on the other end of that paperwork now, rather than waiting until the deadline forces the issue. Lenders and buyers dealing with this same gap across the country are, by all accounts, used to it this month and mostly willing to work with delayed or interim figures if you raise it early instead of after a due date has already passed.

For members without a specific dependency on a paused report, the practical move is patience paired with whatever private and state sources are still running in the meantime. We'll keep an eye on the rescheduled release dates as agencies confirm them and note the ones that matter most to members here as they firm up. If a report you rely on for pricing or planning isn't showing up on anyone's radar yet, tell your Membership & Outreach Committee contact directly — it helps us know where the gap is actually being felt this month, rather than where we assume it probably is from the outside.
