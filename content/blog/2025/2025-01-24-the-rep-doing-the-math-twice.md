Most weeks, a distribution rep's job is unglamorous in a way nobody outside the trade appreciates: which farmstead accounts get a Tuesday stop, which get folded into a Thursday route, which small producer's order is worth the extra fifteen miles this month and which isn't. A regional rep who covers a cluster of small creameries in the upper Midwest agreed to talk us through what that job looks like this particular winter, on the condition we keep her account list out of it.

"I'm doing the same math I've always done," she told us, "except now I'm doing it twice, because the number I got last month doesn't hold this month." Nonfeed costs — fuel, packaging, cold storage — have kept climbing even in stretches where feed prices themselves have eased, and that shows up first on her end of the supply chain, in what a producer can afford to pay for pickup and what a retailer will pay to receive it. When both sides are squeezed, the margin she's routing around gets thinner from both directions at once.

## What she's watching for

She said the accounts that worry her aren't the ones calling with problems — those are usually fixable — but the ones that go quiet. "A producer who used to call me every week about volume, who suddenly stops calling, that's not relief. That's someone who's stopped asking because they already know the answer isn't good." She's started building informal check-ins into her route for exactly that reason, stopping at accounts even when there's no order to pick up, just to see how the books look before a small creamery gets far enough behind that there's nothing left to route.

Her read on this winter tracks with what agricultural economists have been saying since late last year: overall production costs for milk stayed elevated through 2024 even as feed costs came down from their peak, because the nonfeed side of the ledger — the part a distribution route sits inside of — kept rising. For a farmstead operation with thin margins to begin with, that's the difference between a tight year and a year that ends the business. She's also watching the tariff headlines the way everyone in distribution is this winter — a trade fight with a major export destination could push more milk back into the domestic supply than her routes can absorb, and that's exactly the kind of price swing that turns a manageable month into an unmanageable one for the smallest accounts on her list.

Not every small producer on her route is struggling the same way, and she was quick to point out the ones that adapted rather than just absorbed the hit. A few consolidated deliveries with neighboring farms to split her stop into a shared cost. One switched packaging suppliers mid-year rather than eating a price increase. "The ones doing best right now aren't the ones with the most cushion," she said. "They're the ones who changed something instead of waiting it out."

She's a federation member specifically for the price and margin data the association publishes — she said it's the fastest way she has to tell an account "this isn't just you" when a producer thinks they're the only one falling behind.
