Every year around this time, a member calls the office asking the same question in a slightly different shape: what should the sign under the holiday cheese case actually say? It's a fair question to ask now rather than in the second week of December, when there's no time left to rewrite anything. This year, with feed and energy costs pressing on smaller producers all season, the answer matters more than usual — because the story on your label is one of the few levers you control when the price on the wheel has to go up.

Retail buyers we've heard from this fall keep coming back to the same point: shoppers are still willing to pay for something they believe in, but "artisan" and "small-batch" on their own don't do that work anymore. Those words showed up on so many labels for so many years that they've stopped meaning much. What still lands is specific — the farm the milk came from, the number of hands that touched the wheel, the distance between the barn and the cave. A multi-generation farmstead with a real story to tell has an advantage here that a distributor case card can't manufacture, and it's worth using.

## Where this shows up in your own case

The Storytelling & Provenance in Marketing course walks through this in more detail than a blog post can, but the shape of it is straightforward. Start with the one true, specific detail about your operation that a shopper can't get anywhere else — not "family owned," which every producer's sign already says, but the actual thing: the herd is grass-fed on a particular hillside, the affinage room is under the same roof as the milking parlor, the recipe hasn't changed since a specific relative wrote it down. Put that detail in the first sentence of your case card, not the third paragraph of a website nobody at the counter is going to open.

Second, be honest about price increases rather than silent about them. A shopper who sees a wheel cost more than last year and gets no explanation assumes the worst about quality or the retailer's margin. A shopper who reads one sentence about feed costs and a farmer's decision to keep the herd size steady rather than cut corners tends to read that as integrity, not an excuse. We're not suggesting you turn a price tag into a sob story — just that silence reads worse than a plain, short explanation.

Third, resist the instinct to say everything. A case card with six adjectives and a paragraph of history gets skimmed, not read. One sentence about the farm, one about the make, and the name — that's usually enough to earn a second look, and a second look is what turns into a sale during a season when your buyer is standing in front of four other cheese cases making the same choice.

## A place to start

If your team hasn't touched its holiday case copy since last year, the course is running again before the season gets fully underway, and it's a short, practical session rather than a semester's worth of marketing theory. Members who've taken it tell us the biggest shift wasn't learning new language — it was cutting language they'd been using out of habit long after it stopped saying anything.
