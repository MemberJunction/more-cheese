Every February we hear from a handful of members who want to apply for the Certified Cheese
Professional credential and discover, partway through the application, that they don't have
the documented working hours to show for it. It's an honest mistake — the hours requirement
sits quietly in the background until you need it — so this is the week to start a log if you
don't already keep one.

The CCP is the federation's flagship credential, and it's built to be broad on purpose: make
procedures, affinage, food safety, and business practice, all in one application. It requires
a foundation certificate first — most members come in through the Cheese Foundations
Certificate — and then documented hours actually worked in the trade before you sit for it.
Coursework helps you learn the material, but it does not substitute for the hours themselves.

## What actually counts, and how to track it

Working hours means time spent on the floor, in the cave, or on the make line — cutting,
wrapping, aging, tasting, training newer staff. It does not need to be a single employer or a
single job title; a counter clerk who picks up cave shifts on weekends can count both, as long
as the hours are dated and attributable. A simple spreadsheet with a date column, a task
column, and a rough hour count is enough. What trips people up isn't the format, it's starting
late — six months of memory is a lot harder to reconstruct than six months of a running log.

If your shop or creamery already tracks staff scheduling for payroll, ask whether that export
can double as your documentation. It usually can, with a note on what kind of work each shift
covered. Members who've been through the process tell us the biggest time-saver isn't a
fancier spreadsheet, it's simply starting the log the same month they decide to pursue the
credential, rather than the month they plan to apply.

This matters more this year, not less. Staffing has been a live worry across the wider dairy
trade this winter, and shops that are shorter-handed than usual are leaning harder on whoever
is already on the floor to cover more ground — which, if you're that person, is exactly the
kind of varied counter-and-cave experience a CCP application wants documented. Meanwhile
product development in the wider industry keeps leaning toward bolder, more unusual flavor
profiles and imported styles, but the hands-on skills the CCP tests for — reading a paste,
judging a rind, explaining a make decision to a customer — haven't changed. A candidate whose
hours log spans a broad range of counter and cave work tends to test more evenly across the
exam's sections than one whose experience is narrow but deep in a single area.

## Where the coursework fits

Once your hours are underway, the Sensory Foundations course and Pricing & Margin for
Specialty Cheese are the two most commonly paired courses for members heading toward CCP —
one builds the tasting vocabulary the exam expects, the other covers the business-practice
section that catches people who came up purely on the production side. Neither is required
before you apply, but most members find the exam easier having taken at least one.

The credential is valid for three years, and recertification asks for continuing engagement
rather than a full hours count again — so the work you put in now compounds. If you're not
sure whether your current role would qualify, the Education Committee fields these questions
directly through the member portal, and it's easier to ask before you've logged three months
of hours the wrong way than after. Start the spreadsheet this week; the application will
thank you for it later.
