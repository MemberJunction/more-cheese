Most of the calls our Education Committee fields this time of year aren't about make technique. They're about caves suddenly behaving differently than they did in October, and members trying to figure out whether that's normal or a problem. Mostly, it's normal — but it rewards a little attention rather than none.

Aging rooms hold their target range best somewhere in the neighborhood of 50 to 55 degrees Fahrenheit and 80 to 95 percent relative humidity, with the exact number depending on style: hard grating cheeses and alpine styles want it closer to 80 to 85 percent, while bloomy-rind and washed-rind wheels want the higher end. Both numbers get harder to hold once outdoor temperatures drop hard. Cold outside air is dry air, and any cave that pulls in outside ventilation for cooling will pull that dryness in with it. If you've noticed rinds cracking or wheels losing weight faster than they did a month ago, that's usually the first thing to check, not the culture or the milk.

## What actually shifts in winter

The failure mode isn't always dryness. Some caves swing the other way once a furnace or space heater kicks on nearby, especially in a converted basement or outbuilding where heating and aging space share air. A heater running to keep pipes from freezing can quietly drop humidity in the same room it's meant to protect, or push a cave's temperature up out of range entirely if the thermostat isn't zoned separately from the aging area. Neither problem announces itself the way a mold bloom does — you generally catch it in the numbers before you catch it on a rind.

For members running smaller setups, even a converted mini-fridge aging chamber needs the same seasonal recheck as a walk-in: a dual-stage temperature controller holds the compressor to a target near 11 degrees Celsius instead of a stock fridge's factory setting, and a small humidity controller paired with a cold-mist humidifier keeps relative humidity from crashing every time the compressor cycles in a cold garage or unheated shed. The colder it gets outside that fridge, the more often the compressor wants to run, which dries the box out faster than it did in September — the same physics as a full-size cave, just on a smaller and less forgiving scale.

That's the actual habit worth building this month: check your hygrometer and thermometer readings at the same time of day you did in warmer weather, and compare, rather than assuming the room is holding steady because it looks the same. A saturated salt solution or a simple humidifier can correct mild drift; a cave running consistently outside range for more than a few days is worth a harder look at airflow and insulation before you troubleshoot anything else. It also helps to keep two independent readings rather than trusting a single sensor — a cheap hygrometer drifts, and a reading that looks fine on one device but off on another usually means the device, not the cave, needs attention.

## Where to take this further

If you're newer to affinage or inherited a cave you didn't design, the federation's Affinage Fundamentals course covers humidity and temperature management as its own module, separate from the culture and coagulation material — worth taking on its own if that's the part giving you trouble. Members running larger or multi-room aging spaces might get more out of Maturation Planning for Small Rooms, which goes deeper on zoning and seasonal drift specifically. Either way, this is the season to be checking numbers a little more often than you think you need to, not less, and to fix a drifting reading before it shows up as a cracked rind or a slipped skin on next month's wheels.
