If your case sits an Alpine import next to a domestic tomme, the last three weeks have probably meant a spreadsheet you didn't plan to build. A broad new federal tariff schedule took effect in early April, layering duties of 10 to 20 percent or more on cheese arriving from major exporting regions, and members who carry both domestic and imported product have been recalculating margins ever since.

We've heard from a range of members since the schedule landed — retailers rewriting shelf tags, distributors renegotiating minimum orders, a few smaller shops quietly dropping an import line rather than repricing it twice in a month. None of that is unusual when a cost input moves this fast. What's unusual is how many different parts of a member's business it touches at once: landed cost, shelf price, supplier relationships built over years, and the conversation you have with a regular customer who now pays more for a cheese they've bought for a decade.

## What this actually changes

The tariff applies at the border, on the declared value of the import, before it ever reaches your loading dock. That means your existing contracts with domestic producers are untouched — this is specifically a cost added to cheese crossing into the country, not a change to how anything already made here is priced or labeled. If your case is entirely domestic, the direct cost impact is limited, though you may still feel indirect pressure if competitors who carried imports start bidding harder for your domestic suppliers' volume to fill the gap.

For members who do carry imports, the practical questions are the same ones you'd ask about any input cost shock: how much of the increase can the shelf price absorb before a regular customer notices, how much needs to be passed through, and whether a supplier is willing to share any part of the difference to protect a long relationship. There's no universal answer. A specialty shop with a loyal following for a particular Alpine style has more room to hold price than a wholesale account competing on a distributor's price sheet.

This is also arriving on top of the input pressure many of our farmstead and small-creamery members have already been managing this year — feed and energy costs that have been running high enough to squeeze margins even before anything crossed a border. If your business touches both sides, the case and the cave, this spring has not been a gentle one.

## What we're watching

We don't have a forecast for how long the current rates hold, and we're not going to pretend we do. Tariff schedules can change with little notice, and members who lock in supplier pricing for the next quarter should build in language for that. A few practical steps members have found useful:

- **Get the landed cost in writing** from your importer or distributor before you reprice, rather than estimating off the wire rate. The declared value the duty applies to isn't always the number on the invoice you're used to seeing.
- **Talk to your supplier before your customer does.** A short note explaining why a favorite import moved is almost always better received than a silent price change on the shelf tag.
- **Revisit your case mix, not just your prices.** A few members have used this as a prompt to feature more of their domestic and regional lineup this season — not as a political statement, just as a hedge.

If you want to talk through how this is landing on your specific mix of domestic and imported product, our Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding these calls all month and can point you toward members managing something close to your situation.
