If your feed bill or your propane bill has surprised you twice already this year, you are not imagining a trend. Corn and other feed costs climbed sharply over the winter even as milk prices softened, and several members told us their input costs didn't wait for a forecast to catch up — they moved first. For a farmstead creamery pricing wheels six weeks out, that gap between what you pay for inputs and what the market says milk is worth is exactly where margin quietly disappears.

Federal margin coverage reports have swung month to month over the past year, which is its own kind of warning: a program built to smooth out volatility is itself showing more of it than usual. That's useful context, but it's not a pricing tool for your specific creamery, because the margin it tracks is a national blend, not your feed supplier's invoice or your electric cave's utility bill.

The instinct in a squeeze is to hold your price and wait it out. Sometimes that's right. More often, holding a price you set under different input costs is how a good year turns into a break-even one without anyone deciding it should.

## Repricing without guessing

Our course *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* walks through a repricing method built for exactly this situation — not a spreadsheet full of theory, but a worksheet you fill in with your own numbers. It starts with your true cost per wheel: milk, cultures, labor hours in the make room, aging loss, packaging, and yes, the energy it takes to hold your cave at temperature. Members are often surprised how much of that last line has crept up without a corresponding line item ever getting revisited.

From there the course walks you through setting a floor price by style and format, so a small wheel of a fast-turning cheese and a large wheel aged a year aren't priced off the same gut instinct. It also covers the harder conversation: what you say to a longtime wholesale account when your new floor is higher than your old price, and how to have that conversation before an invoice does it for you.

## What this doesn't cover, and a smaller habit if a full repricing isn't realistic

This is a pricing and cost-accounting course, not investment or tax advice, and it won't tell you whether to expand, hold steady, or scale back — that call is yours and your accountant's to make. What it will do is make sure the number you're working from actually reflects what your cheese costs to make today, not what it cost when you last sat down and worked it out.

Not every operation has a free afternoon for a full worksheet mid-quarter. If that's you, the course also teaches a shorter monthly check: pull your feed and energy invoices, compare them to the same month last year, and flag anything that's moved more than ten percent. That single habit catches most of the drift before it compounds into a real problem, and it takes less time than a single make session.
