The international auction platform that sets a rough global benchmark for dairy commodity prices posted its seventh consecutive decline this week, and the index is now sitting at its lowest point in more than a year. Cheddar was down a few percentage points from the prior round on continued oversupply. If you export, buy imported components, or simply watch the number because everyone in the industry does, this is the week it stopped being a blip and started being a trend.

Supply is the story, not demand. Milk production has kept climbing on both sides of the Atlantic — more cows, more output per cow — and it's outrunning the growth in where that milk actually goes. That's a different problem than a demand collapse, and it matters for how members should read it. Nobody we've talked to this month thinks people have stopped wanting cheese. They think there's simply more of it than the market can absorb at last year's price.

## Reading the number from your side of the case

For members buying imported cultures, specialty milk components, or finished product for resale, a soft global index is one more piece of good news layered onto the domestic price relief we wrote about earlier this month — worth locking in on a contract if your supplier will let you, because these swings reverse without much warning.

For members who make and sell finished wheels into any kind of international channel, the read is harder. A falling benchmark abroad puts pressure on what a foreign buyer will pay, even for a specialty product that doesn't directly compete with commodity block cheddar. We heard from a member this week whose export contract renewal conversation got noticeably tougher once the buyer's own procurement team started citing the global number, regardless of whether it actually applies to a small-batch aged wheel. If you're in that conversation yourself, it's fair to ask a buyer to be specific about which benchmark they're referencing and why it should move your price — a global commodity index and a negotiated specialty contract are not the same instrument, even when a buyer would rather treat them as one.

Domestically, the picture is calmer for now, though not fully settled. The federal reports that feed the dairy safety-net program are still working through a backlog left over from this fall's shutdown, and enrollment and indemnity processing have both run behind schedule as a result. In the reports members have been able to see, the margin used for that program has stayed comfortably above the support trigger. That's a genuine cushion, not a reason to stop watching, and it's worth remembering the figure is a national average — feed costs move independently of milk prices, and a herd that looks fine on paper this month can look different by spring if either side of that ratio shifts.

## What the federation is tracking

We update the pricing and margin dashboard on the member portal whenever new federal figures land, and we've folded this week's global auction result in alongside it so members can see both numbers next to each other rather than chasing them down separately. If your own contract renegotiations are citing the global index and you want a second read on whether that's the right benchmark for your situation, the Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded enough of these calls this fall to have a reasonably good sense of what a fair reference point looks like. Ask before you agree to a price cut premised on a number that may not describe your product.

The broader pattern — more milk than buyers currently want at last year's prices — isn't unique to this week, and it isn't going to resolve in one auction cycle either direction. We'll keep flagging it as it moves, and we'd rather members hear it from us in plain terms than piece it together from headlines that don't distinguish between a commodity swing and their own contract.
