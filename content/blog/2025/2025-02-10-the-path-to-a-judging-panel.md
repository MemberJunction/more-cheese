We get some version of the same question every February: "How do I end up on a judging panel?"
The honest answer is a little less glamorous than the question implies. It runs through a
credential most members walk past on the way to something flashier, and it's worth stopping
for, especially with competition entry windows opening across the industry this month.

The **Sensory Evaluation Certificate** teaches structured tasting, defect identification, and
scoring against style standards — the actual mechanics of comparing twenty wheels against a
rubric instead of a personal preference. It's also the documented prerequisite for **Competition
Judge Accreditation**, which requires the sensory certificate plus supervised panel experience
before it's issued. Skip the sensory work and the judging credential simply isn't available to
you, no matter how many competitions you've entered as a producer.

## What the course actually covers

If you've only ever tasted your own cheese, or your case, the certificate work can be
disorienting at first. You're taught to separate what you like from what's correct for the
style — a washed-rind wheel with real barnyard funk can still be a well-made washed-rind wheel,
and a mild, clean one can still be off-style for its category. Defect vocabulary gets specific:
the difference between a "bitter" note from over-ripening and a "bitter" note from a starter
culture problem matters to a judge even when it tastes similar to a shopper.

Members pursuing the credential typically pair it with a formal course rather than
self-study — **Advanced Sensory Evaluation** runs most years and gives you a scored panel
experience under supervision, which is the piece that's genuinely hard to replicate on your own.
It's not a large cohort, and it fills earlier than members expect once word gets around that
competition season is close.

Large panels elsewhere in the industry can run into three or four dozen accredited judges
working a single contest across a few packed days. Our own flagship competition is a smaller
affair, but the underlying discipline is the same: a judge who has never been trained out of
their personal preferences will score inconsistently against members who have, and inconsistent
scoring is the fastest way to lose a producer's trust in the results.

## Why this is a February conversation, not a June one

Competition season planning starts well before entries open, and panels get built from an
accreditation roster the awards committee maintains year-round. If your goal is a seat on a
panel for this year's flagship competition, the math doesn't work if you start the sensory
certificate after entries close — the supervised-experience requirement for judge accreditation
alone can take a full course cycle to satisfy properly, and that's before the accreditation
itself is issued.

None of this is about gatekeeping the judging table. Interest is genuinely welcome, and the
Awards & Competition Committee would rather have a longer bench of accredited judges than a
short one relying on the same handful of members every year. It's simply a credential with real
runway, and runway is exactly what a February start gives you before entries open later this
year.

If you're interested, the certificate has a three-year renewal cycle once you have it — plenty
of room to build on it with the Advanced Affinage Certificate or move toward the judging track
once you've put in a season or two on a supervised panel. Talk to your regional rep about which
upcoming Advanced Sensory Evaluation cohort has room, and start there.
