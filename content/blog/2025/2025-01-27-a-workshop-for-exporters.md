Two weeks ago we wrote about the tariff pledge hanging over two of our largest dairy trade relationships and the members asking us what to do while it sits unresolved. One answer, at least for the paperwork side of the question, arrives next Tuesday. Workshop: Cedar Export Readiness runs February 4 in Brattleboro, and as of this week it's close to full.

That's unusual for a January workshop. Most years, export documentation is a topic members circle back to when a shipment gets held up, not before. This year, registrations came in steadily from the day we opened them, which tells us members are treating the tariff uncertainty as a planning problem now rather than a scramble later. Federal trade data released this month actually showed cheese exports opening the year on a strong note, with volume up again over last January — a reminder that the current uncertainty is sitting on top of a healthy export trend, not a declining one, which is exactly why members don't want to lose ground to a paperwork gap.

## What the day actually covers

The workshop walks through the documentation side of cross-border shipment — certificates of origin, health certificates, the paperwork a customs broker will ask for before a truck crosses either border — alongside a working session on what changes in your contracts and your margin math if a new tariff line lands mid-shipment. It's a practical day, not a policy briefing. Nobody teaching it can tell you whether a tariff takes effect on any particular date, and the syllabus doesn't pretend otherwise.

For members who've never exported and are only now considering it, this is also a reasonable entry point. Cross-border shipment looks intimidating mostly because the first shipment's paperwork is unfamiliar, not because it's actually complicated once you've done it once. The federation's Export Documentation Essentials course covers the same ground at a slower pace if a single workshop day isn't enough runway, and several members who took that course last year have told us it's what made a first shipment feel manageable instead of overwhelming.

The workshop's afternoon session is built specifically around contract language — what a force-majeure or price-adjustment clause needs to say to hold up if a tariff changes the economics of a shipment already in transit. That's the piece producers say is easiest to skip and hardest to fix after the fact, since renegotiating a clause after a shipment leaves the dock is a much harder conversation than writing it in up front.

## If Brattleboro isn't reachable

We know a one-day, in-person workshop in Vermont isn't within driving distance for a lot of members, particularly anyone shipping out of the west coast or the Gulf states. The Events Committee has fielded enough of that feedback this month that a virtual repeat is under discussion for late spring, timed to whatever the tariff situation looks like by then. If you'd attend a virtual version, tell your Events Committee liaison now — the case for scheduling one gets easier to make with names attached to the interest, not just a general sense that people want it.

In the meantime, if you're shipping across either border in the next month and haven't looked closely at your documentation, don't wait for a workshop seat to open up. The Membership & Outreach team can point you to the same checklist the Brattleboro session is built around, and can also tell you whether a seat opens up on the waitlist between now and Tuesday.
