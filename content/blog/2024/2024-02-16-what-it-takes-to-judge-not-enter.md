With entries for this year's flagship competition already opening, we're getting the annual round of questions from the other side of the table: members who've entered for years and now want to know what it takes to judge instead. Competition Judge Accreditation is the answer, and unlike most of our credentials, it isn't really about proving what you know about making cheese. It's about proving you can score someone else's, consistently, under time pressure, without your own preferences leaking into the number.

That distinction trips people up. A member who's brought home ribbons for a decade told us she failed the accreditation's calibration exercises on her first attempt, because judging isn't graded on taste — it's graded on agreement. Candidates score a flight of cheeses independently and then compare notes against a panel of accredited judges; the credential measures how close your scores land to that panel's consensus, not whether your palate is sophisticated. It's a humbling exercise for people used to trusting their own nose above all else, and most candidates say that's exactly the point.

## What the accreditation actually covers

The program walks candidates through separating aroma, texture, and appearance into distinct scoring dimensions rather than one gut reaction — the same skill the Judging & Competition Preparation course teaches from the entrant's side, just from the other chair. There's a written component on the standard defect vocabulary judges use to describe faults consistently, so that two judges independently writing "chalky paste" or "ammoniated rind" mean the same thing to a competition's organizers. And there's a supervised scoring session where a panel of currently accredited judges watches candidates work a real flight and gives direct feedback.

Recertification runs on a shorter cycle than most of our credentials, because judging calibration drifts if it isn't refreshed. If your palate has been shaped by a specific style you make yourself, that bias tends to creep back in gradually, and the recert process exists to catch that before it shows up on an actual competition panel.

## Why this season, specifically

Panels for the flagship competition get assembled well ahead of the summer event, and the Awards & Competition Committee draws first from members who are already accredited. If you finish the credential in the next couple of months, you're in the pool for this year's panel; finish it in May, and you're realistically looking at next year. It's also, frankly, an easier credential to focus on right now than it will be once spring make schedules get busy — the calibration exercises take real, uninterrupted attention, and February gives most members more of that than April will. Entry windows for several major national contests are open right now too, which is exactly the kind of season that reminds long-time entrants how much they'd rather be reading the wheels than sweating over their own.

One honest note: accreditation doesn't guarantee a panel seat. Committees also weigh category expertise and travel availability, and a first-year accredited judge is more likely to start on a regional or virtual panel before working up to the flagship event. That's normal, and most judges say the smaller panels taught them more anyway — there's less noise and more direct feedback from co-judges when the stakes are lower.

Enrollment details, the exam calendar, and the full recertification cycle are posted with the rest of the certification pathways on the member portal. If you've been entering long enough to have opinions about how judging goes, this is the credential that lets you do something about it.
