Not every federation member has quit a day job to make cheese. One longtime member, who asked us
to keep her creamery unnamed while she's still building toward year-round production, has spent
the past three years running a two-cow operation entirely around a full-time job in town. Milking
happens before work and again after. Makes happen on weekends and the rare weeknight she can carve
out. It is, by her own description, "cheese made on the margins of a life that's mostly about
something else."

"People assume it's a hobby because I have another job," she told us. "It isn't. It's a business
that's growing slower than a full-time operation would, on purpose, because I'm not willing to bet
the mortgage on it before I know it can carry that weight."

## What a two-shift schedule actually looks like

Her make days are built around whatever window her day job leaves open, which means she's learned
to work with a compressed and sometimes unpredictable schedule rather than the steady rhythm a
full-time creamery can plan around. "A make that should take four hours sometimes gets stretched
across two evenings because I ran out of daylight, or energy, or both," she said. "You learn to
build slack into every step, because your schedule doesn't care what the culture wants."

She's leaned hard on the federation's coursework precisely because it's self-paced. "I can't make
a Tuesday afternoon workshop. I can watch a recorded module at ten at night after the second
milking," she said. "That flexibility is the reason I've been able to keep learning at all, instead
of falling behind while I wait for a schedule that has more room in it."

## The trade-off she's made peace with

Asked whether she resents the slower pace, she pushed back gently on the framing. "I used to think
of this as the compromise version of the dream. Now I think of it as the version that let me test
whether I actually wanted the dream before I bet everything on it. Some members who went full-time
early burned out or ran out of runway. I got to find out slowly whether I had the stomach for this,
without risking the thing that was paying my bills the whole time."

Her advice for other members building a creamery around another job is blunt: protect the time you
do have fiercely, and don't apologize for the pace. "Nobody hands out extra credit for doing this
the hard way," she said. "But nobody can take away what you actually built, either, once it's
there."

There are trade-offs she's more candid about than others. Wholesale accounts are mostly out of
reach for now — she can't guarantee volume on a schedule dictated by a day job, and she's learned
to say so upfront rather than overpromise to a distributor and disappoint them later. Farmers'
market sales fill the gap instead, on the one Saturday a month her schedule allows, which she
admits caps her growth in a way a full-time operation wouldn't accept. "I've made peace with
growing at the speed my actual life allows," she said, "instead of the speed a business plan says
I should."

She's also had to get comfortable asking for help in small ways that used to embarrass her — a
neighbor who checks the cave when she's away for a weekend, a fellow member a few towns over who
answers a two-line question about affinage over text rather than making her wait for the next
committee call. "This community carries people like me more than people realize," she said. "It's
not always the flagship conference or the big course. Sometimes it's just someone answering a
message at nine at night because they remember what the early years were like."

Members balancing a creamery with outside work can find flexible, self-paced coursework and peer
