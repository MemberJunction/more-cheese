Most weeks the wholesale numbers move together, cheese and milk drifting in roughly the same direction because they're made from the same raw material. Late March into early April broke that pattern in a way worth flagging: cheese and butter both firmed to multi-month highs on the trading exchange, at the same time the federal milk production forecast for the year was trimmed on slower expected yield per cow. That's not a contradiction, it's a supply story, and it changes how you should be reading your own price sheet this spring.

## Why less milk can mean a firmer cheese market

When production forecasts come down, it isn't because cows are producing less cheese-worthy milk, it's because there's simply less volume moving through the system than buyers had priced in. Less cheese sitting in cold storage, combined with retail demand that held up better than expected, pushed the exchange price for blocks and barrels up through the end of March. Butter told a similar story, breaking through a threshold it hadn't cleared earlier in the year.

None of this shows up instantly in what a small creamery is quoting a wholesale account, and that's the gap worth watching. If your supply contracts or list prices were set when the production outlook looked more generous, this spring's tighter numbers are an argument for revisiting them sooner than your usual quarterly cycle, not for panicking, but for making sure your margin reflects where the market actually sits today rather than where it sat in January.

## What this means for your spring price sheet

For members selling primarily direct or through their own counter, the practical move is simpler: check whether your current pricing still clears a reasonable margin against this spring's input costs, and don't assume last quarter's numbers still hold. For members with wholesale accounts, this is a reasonable moment to have the pricing conversation with a buyer before they bring it to you — a firmer market gives you more room in that discussion than a soft one does.

The instinct when wholesale numbers move up is to raise a price sheet across the board, and that instinct is usually a little too blunt. A firmer cheese market doesn't lift every product the same amount — the movement we've been reading about this spring has concentrated in commodity block and barrel prices and in butter, while more differentiated specialty items tend to move on a slower, separate track tied to their own ingredient and labor costs rather than the exchange. If your price sheet mixes both, a single across-the-board increase risks either leaving money on the table for the commodity-adjacent lines or pricing your specialty items out of step with what actually drove them. The safer approach is to walk your product list line by line and ask which items are actually exposed to the milk and cheese exchange price and which are priced mostly on your own labor, aging time, and packaging. For a small creamery running a handful of SKUs, that's a half-day exercise, not a project, and it tends to catch at least one line that's been quietly under- or over-priced for months regardless of what the broader market is doing.

One month of firmer prices is not the same thing as a trend, and members who lived through the swings of the past several years know that better than most. The production forecast that's driving this particular move is itself a moving target, subject to revision as the year goes on, and it would be a mistake to lock in a long-term supply contract on the assumption that April's tightness holds through the summer. Treat this as a signal to check your numbers now, not as a forecast for the rest of the year.
