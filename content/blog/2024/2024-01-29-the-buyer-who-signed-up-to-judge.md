A specialty cheese buyer at a regional distributor spent ten years building a reputation for a good palate before she ever wrote it down anywhere official. She tasted for her own case: would this wheel move, would this account reorder, would this rind hold up on a truck. Late last year she finished the federation's Competition Judge Accreditation, and she told us the strangest part wasn't the studying. It was noticing how differently she now tastes for her own job.

"Judging isn't buying with extra steps," she said. "Buying is one question — will this sell. Judging is four or five questions at once, and none of them are about whether I'd stock it."

## What the accreditation actually asks of you

The Competition Judge Accreditation is built around structured sensory evaluation: scoring rind, paste, aroma, and balance against a category standard rather than against personal preference. For a buyer, that's a harder switch than it sounds. Preference is fast — you know in a few seconds whether something belongs on your shelf. Judging asks you to slow down and score a wheel that isn't to your taste but is a clean, well-executed example of its style.

She said the credential forced her to separate two things she'd been quietly running together for years: "I don't like it" and "it's flawed." A washed-rind wheel with more funk than she'd ever put in her own case can still score well if the funk is controlled and intentional rather than a sign of a cave problem. Learning to write that distinction down, category by category, was most of the work.

It's also changed a conversation she has constantly with producers who send her samples. Where she used to say "not quite right for us," she now can often say why in terms a cheesemaker can act on — a paste that hadn't fully knit, a rind that read as underdeveloped for its age rather than intentionally young. That's more useful to the person on the other end of the sample, even when the answer is still no.

## Why this matters beyond the judging table

Buyer demand for specialty cheese has been leaning harder toward sheep's and goat's milk styles and toward newer, less familiar entries this year, according to recent industry coverage. Even flavor-trend reporting this January pointed the same direction — more unusual pairings on cheese and other dairy products, not the safest, most familiar combinations. Which means buyers, and now judges, are seeing more wheels they don't have a personal frame of reference for. A structured way to evaluate something outside your usual lane is useful whether or not you ever sit behind a judging table. She said the accreditation gave her a vocabulary for "unfamiliar but well-made" that she didn't have before, and that vocabulary shows up in her buying notes now, not just her judging scoresheets.

She's judging her first regional round this spring. She's also, for the first time in a decade, rewriting her own tasting notes template — the one she uses at her actual desk, for her actual case.

If you buy, sell, or make cheese and want the same structured vocabulary, Competition Judge Accreditation is open to members with prior sensory evaluation coursework; details are on the certifications page, and the Awards & Competition Committee can answer questions about upcoming judging opportunities.
