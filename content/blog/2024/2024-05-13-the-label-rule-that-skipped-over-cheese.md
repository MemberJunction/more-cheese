A federal rule finalized in March tightened the standard for a voluntary origin claim like "Product of USA," requiring that anything carrying that language on a label actually be born, raised, slaughtered, and processed here rather than just packaged here. It's a meaningful change for the products it covers, and it arrived with real teeth: a claim that doesn't meet the new standard is treated as misleading, not just optimistic marketing copy. We've had a steady trickle of members ask our advocacy volunteers the same follow-up question since it was announced — does this apply to cheese?

The direct answer is no. The rule was written for the agency that oversees meat, poultry, and egg products, and cheese doesn't fall under that agency's jurisdiction at all. A member putting "Product of USA" on a cheese label isn't bound by this specific rule one way or the other, which is exactly the gap several members have flagged to us as a problem rather than a relief.

## Why the gap bothers members more than the rule itself

A handful of our members compete directly against imported cheese marketed with origin language that would never survive the new meat-and-poultry standard, and they've told us plainly that a shopper standing at a case doesn't know, and has no easy way to find out, that "made with care in an American kitchen" on one label means something completely different from the underlying standard now required next to it on a package of ground beef. Inconsistent rules across food categories don't just create a compliance headache — they create a shopper who reasonably assumes a label means roughly the same thing everywhere it appears, when it doesn't.

That's the case our advocacy work has started building this spring under the "Labeling standards" banner: not asking for the exact meat-and-poultry rule to be copied onto cheese wholesale, since our members' supply chains and import realities are genuinely different, but asking that whatever oversight body governs dairy labeling take a real look at whether cheese's much older, much looser origin-claim standard needs the same kind of tightening. A member with a fully domestic milk supply and a genuinely local make process has a real interest in a label that means something specific, not just a phrase nobody's required to back up.

## What to check on your own label now

Regardless of what happens with a future rule, this is a good spring to reread your own label copy with fresh eyes. If you're making any kind of origin claim — "local," "farmstead," "American-made" — make sure you can back it up with your own sourcing records today, not just a general sense that it's true. A voluntary claim you can't document is exposed the moment anyone asks, rule change or not.

If your business has run into a shelf conversation where an origin claim on a competitor's cheese felt misleading, our Standards Committee wants to hear the specifics — a real example is what makes a case like this land with a regulator, far more than a general complaint about fairness.
