If your cave has felt a little off this month, you're not imagining it. Coverage of this summer's North American heat waves has focused mostly on crops and power grids, but members running a small aging room have their own version of the same problem: late July tends to be the week a cooling system that's been fine all spring finally shows where its limits are. A compressor works harder to hold the same set point, a humidity reading drifts a few points higher overnight, a rind softens faster than the same wheel did back in May. None of that is a crisis on its own. It's a signal, and this time of year it's worth paying attention to rather than assuming the cave will settle back down once the weather turns.

Humidity and airflow don't move independently of each other, which is the part that trips up a lot of members who are managing a cave for the first summer. Pushing humidity down to fight a mold bloom can starve airflow if your fans aren't sized for the extra load, and cranking airflow to cool a room faster can dry a rind out before it's ready. The two systems have to be tuned together, and what worked in a cool spring cave often needs a real adjustment once ambient temperatures climb and stay there for weeks at a time rather than a single hot afternoon.

## What the course covers

Our Humidity & Airflow Control course walks through exactly this trade-off — how to read a cave's actual behavior rather than just its target numbers, when a slight humidity drift is worth a manual correction versus waiting it out, and how to size a backup plan for the day your primary cooling unit fails during the hottest week of the year, because eventually it will. The course leans heavily on worked examples from small and mid-sized rooms rather than industrial-scale refrigeration, which is the gap a lot of members say they feel most acutely when they're troubleshooting alone at 11 p.m. with a wheel they can't afford to lose.

It also covers instrumentation — where to place a second thermometer or hygrometer in a room that isn't behaving uniformly, and how to tell the difference between a sensor problem and an actual environmental one. More than one member has spent a stressful week chasing a humidity swing that turned out to be a probe reading wrong in a warm corner of the room, rather than anything actually happening to the cheese.
