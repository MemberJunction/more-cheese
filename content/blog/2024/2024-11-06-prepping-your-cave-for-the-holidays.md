Every cave has a personality, and every cave's worst month is usually the one where you're
asking it to do more work than usual on a tighter clock. For a lot of members, that month is
right about now. Holiday cheese demand builds fast between now and mid-December, which means
more wheels turning over faster, more traffic in and out of the room, and less patience from
customers who want a wedge that's actually ready, not a wedge that's three weeks from ready.

This is exactly the stretch our Cave Management coursework was built for, and enrollment tends
to spike this time of year for a reason: most of what goes wrong in a cave during a busy season
isn't a mystery, it's a known failure mode that shows up every single November.

## The two variables that actually matter

Humidity and airflow. Everything else — temperature, turning schedule, brushing routine — sits
downstream of getting those two right, and both get harder to manage exactly when you need them
most. More door traffic during a busy season means more temperature and humidity swings every
time someone goes in for stock. A cave that's been quietly stable for months can start drifting
the moment you're pulling wheels twice a day instead of twice a week.

The course walks through building a monitoring routine that catches drift before it shows up on
a rind — checking readings at the same time each day rather than whenever you happen to remember,
and knowing which swings are noise and which ones are an early warning. One member who took the
course last winter told us the biggest change wasn't equipment, it was discipline: "I used to
check humidity when something already looked off. Now I check it on a schedule, and by the time
something looks off, I already know why."

The other piece members underestimate is how much a busy production month changes the physical
choreography of the room. If you're moving more wheels through on a compressed timeline, your
turning and brushing schedule needs to flex with volume, not stay fixed to a calendar built for a
quiet month. The coursework covers how to reorganize a cave's physical layout temporarily —
grouping wheels by how close they are to ready, rather than by type or arrival date — so the
wheels that need attention this week aren't buried behind ones that don't need touching until
January.

## Knowing your own room, and signing up before the rush

None of this replaces knowing your own room. Every cave has quirks a course can't fully account
for — a corner that runs drier, a door that never quite seals, a rack that catches more airflow
than the rest. But a systematic approach gives you a baseline to notice when those quirks are
behaving normally and when they're not, which matters more in a month where you don't have time
to investigate a slow surprise.

The irony of cave management coursework is that the best time to take it is before your busiest
month starts, and the worst time to realize you needed it is partway through. If your cave is
about to see more traffic than it has all year, this is the week to get into the coursework
rather than the week you're troubleshooting a rind that's gone soft on you at the worst possible
moment.

Members can enroll through the Education Committee's course catalog on the member portal. The
coursework is self-paced, so it fits around a production schedule that's already busier than
usual this month.
