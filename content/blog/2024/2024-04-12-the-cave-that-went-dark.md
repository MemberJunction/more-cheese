A farmstead member of ours sits almost exactly on the line of Monday's total solar eclipse, and she closed her retail counter for the afternoon so the whole crew — three of them, plus an apprentice on a work-study rotation — could stand in the parking lot and watch it happen together. "We make cheese in a room with no windows most of the week," she told us afterward. "It felt strange to be the ones standing in the dark on purpose for once."

She's been a member since the early years of the artisan boom, running a small mixed-milk operation that leans hard on a single washed-rind wheel as its signature. It's the kind of cheese that punishes a distracted maker — the rind wants daily attention, the cave wants a humidity reading logged morning and night, and a missed day doesn't show up until three weeks later as a crack or a slip you can't fully undo. So closing for an afternoon, even a historic one, wasn't a small decision.

## What actually happens when you leave a cave alone

The honest answer, this time, was: not much. Four minutes of darkness in the parking lot didn't touch the temperature or humidity in a cave that's built to hold steady regardless of what the sky is doing outside. The bigger risk wasn't the eclipse at all — it was that closing the counter meant turning away a Monday's worth of walk-in customers, and spring is exactly when foot traffic starts picking back up after a slow winter.

She made the trade anyway, and said the calculation was simple once she framed it that way: a missed afternoon of counter sales is recoverable. A once-in-two-decades astronomical event with her own kids and her apprentice standing next to her is not. The cheese, meanwhile, didn't need her in the room. That's the part she says took her the longest to learn as a newer maker — that a well-built cave and a disciplined logging habit buy you the ability to walk away sometimes, on purpose, without anything going wrong.

Plenty of farms across the eclipse's path spent the weeks beforehand thinking hard about exactly this kind of planning — what animals and equipment need a person present, and what can run unattended for an afternoon. Her creamery did the same exercise for the cave, and came out the other side with a written answer she's kept using since.

## The part worth borrowing even without an eclipse

Most members won't get a once-in-a-generation reason to close early this year. But the underlying lesson travels: if your cave depends on you personally checking it every single day with no backup plan, that's a fragility worth fixing before it costs you a wedding, a funeral, or just a sick day. A written routine — target ranges, what to do if a reading drifts, who else on staff can read a hygrometer — turns "I can't leave" into "I chose to stay."

If you're building that kind of documentation for the first time, our Education Committee has course material on cave management fundamentals that covers exactly this: what to log, how often, and what actually needs a same-day human decision versus what can wait. Ask about it the next time you're on with us, and tell your own crew's eclipse story while you're at it — we're collecting a few for the newsletter.
