Every April, one of our members - a quality manager at a small farmstead creamery who has been with the federation for several years - does the same thing before her production floor really picks up for the season: she walks the entire floor with a clipboard, start to finish, and writes down everything that's changed since the last walkthrough. "It's not an audit," she told us. "An audit is somebody checking whether you did what you said you'd do. This is me checking whether what I said is still true."

Her spring walkthrough has become something other members ask her about at conference. Not because it's unusual - most food-safety plans call for regular internal reviews - but because of how specific she's gotten about timing it to April rather than January or June.

## Why spring, specifically

"January is when everybody reviews their plan on paper," she said. "April is when the plan meets the actual weather." Warmer outside air changes how a cave's dehumidifier cycles, how often a cooler door gets propped open by someone carrying cases, how quickly a newly hired seasonal hand learns where the sanitizer log lives. None of that shows up in a document review. It shows up when you're standing in the room.

She keeps her walkthrough list short on purpose - long enough to be thorough, short enough that she actually finishes it in one afternoon instead of pushing it to next week. This spring's list included recalibrating her cave's humidity sensor against a handheld reference, retraining two new hires on glove changes at the cutting table, and rewriting a temperature-log form that had gotten confusing after last year's cooler upgrade. "The form thing sounds small," she said. "But if the form is confusing, people fill it out wrong, and then you don't actually know what happened last Tuesday."

A recent multi-facility study of small and mid-sized dairy processors found that floor drains were among the most common places for a persistent contamination risk to take hold - not because small operations are careless, but because a drain that isn't part of a deliberate monitoring rotation is easy to overlook for months at a time. That tracks with what our member has found on her own floor: the riskiest spots are rarely the ones anyone's actively worried about.

Members who've heard her describe this over the years sometimes ask if the list ever gets shorter. It doesn't, she says - it gets different. A creamery that's added a second cheesemaker, brought in new equipment, or started shipping to a new distributor has new places where a routine can quietly drift. The walkthrough isn't about eliminating problems; it's about catching the drift before it becomes a habit.

## What she'd tell a member doing this for the first time

Her advice for a smaller operation starting its own spring walkthrough is to resist the urge to make it comprehensive on day one. "Pick the five things most likely to have changed since you last looked closely, and start there," she said. "You can build the rest of the list over a couple of years. What you can't do is skip the year you're busiest, because that's exactly the year something moves."

She also pointed to a practical reason to write the list down rather than keep it in her head: continuity when she's out sick or on vacation. A written walkthrough checklist means a second staff member can run it without needing her memory of what changed last year.

Quality managers make up a steady share of our membership, often the people quietest at conference and busiest the rest of the year. If her approach to April sounds useful to your own operation, our Education Committee maintains a template checklist members can adapt, and it's worth pairing with a current course review rather than assuming last year's plan still fits this year's floor.
