Domestic cheese shipments headed overseas have been on a steady climb all spring, and the trade press has started calling it out as a genuine trend rather than a one-month blip. April was the second-biggest month for cheese exports on record, with growth spread across a wide set of markets rather than concentrated in one buyer. We've fielded more calls than usual this spring from members asking, in one form or another, "should I care about this if I sell entirely through a regional distributor?" The honest answer is: probably not directly, but the ripple effects reach further than most members expect.

## Why a wholesale trend touches a small counter

When export demand pulls volume out of the domestic market, it tightens supply everywhere else, and tighter supply shows up as steadier or firmer pricing at every scale down the chain, including yours. It also means the buyers you already work with are fielding more competing demand for the same milk, the same aging space, and the same trucking capacity, which can turn a routine reorder into a slower one. None of this is dramatic, but it's the kind of thing worth flagging to whoever handles your purchasing so a delayed delivery doesn't come as a surprise later this summer.

Milk markets themselves have also been a little steadier this spring than members got used to last year, with futures pricing improving and feed costs easing off, which takes some of the pressure out of the margin conversations a lot of small producers were having a year ago. That's a modest bit of good news on its own, separate from the export story, but the two trends are pulling in the same direction: less volatility, more room to plan a few months out instead of reacting week to week.

There's a second layer here that matters more directly to a handful of our members: a few long-running disputes over how much a trading partner can tax incoming cheese, and how domestic naming rules for cheese styles get treated abroad, are both active again this year in ongoing trade discussions. Neither is close to resolved, and we're not going to pretend either one is simple. But if you've ever wondered whether your style of wheel could realistically sell outside your region, this is a reasonable season to start that conversation with a distributor who already handles export paperwork, rather than trying to build that capability from scratch on your own.

## What we're watching on your behalf

The federation doesn't lobby on tariff schedules directly — that's genuinely outside our lane — but we do track how shifts like this affect the ordinary business decisions our members make: whether to lock in a supply contract, whether to expand a cave, whether this is the year to hire a second cheesemaker. If any of that is on your mind for the back half of the year, our Membership & Outreach Committee fields exactly these kinds of questions, and a fair number of members have found it useful just to talk the decision through with someone who's watched a full market cycle before.

None of this means every member should start chasing an export buyer. Most won't, and shouldn't — the paperwork, the cold-chain logistics, and the volume commitments involved aren't a fit for a small operation still finding its footing domestically. What it does mean is that the broader market you sell into is behaving a little differently than it did a year ago, and it's worth five minutes of thought about whether that changes anything for you this quarter, even if the answer ends up being no.

**Next step:** if you're weighing a supply contract or a capacity decision this summer and want a sounding board, reach out through the member portal — there's no formal process required, just a conversation.
