Members who've been staring at the same flat spreadsheet since January can put it down for a minute. Wholesale cheese has climbed to its highest level in months, and the reasons behind the move are worth understanding before you reprice anything on your own case.

The short version: milk prices have been climbing steadily since late winter, and federal market news reports through March pointed to tighter cheese supplies as the reason. Slower-than-expected milk yield per cow has tightened the supply side just as retail cheese demand has picked back up, and several regional dairy-market bulletins have described the same pattern in recent weeks: less cheese sitting in storage, and buyers less willing to wait for a dip before placing an order.

## What this means if you're not a commodity player

Most of our members aren't selling 40-pound Cheddar blocks into the spot market, but the spot market still sets the floor everyone else prices against. If your distributor's cost sheet moved this month, that's why. A few things worth doing before your next order:

- Ask your supplier whether the increase is being described as a blip or a trend. Right now the honest answer from most of the trade press is "we're not sure yet" — production is tight, but so is the margin every link in the chain is running on.
- If you buy young cheese to age yourself, lock in what you can now. A cave full of wheels bought at March pricing is a better hedge than a spreadsheet promise from a supplier.
- If you sell finished wedges at retail, this is a fair moment to explain a price change to a regular customer rather than let them discover it cold at the register. People understand "the milk supply is tighter this spring" a lot better than a silent forty-cent jump.

## The demand side is the more interesting story

The production tightness is one half of this. The other half — and the half that should matter more to our members long-term — is that specialty and artisan cheese categories keep growing even while the broader grocery basket stays cautious. Shoppers who are trading down on plenty of other items are still reaching for a good wedge. That's not a fluke of one good spring; it's been the shape of the category for a couple of years running now, through the return of in-person tastings, farmers' markets, and a wave of new small producers entering the space.

If you make cheese, that demand is the reason to keep investing in the case even while input costs wobble. If you sell it, it's the argument for holding your specialty shelf space rather than trimming it back at the first sign of a cost increase.

A few members watched Monday's total solar eclipse cross right over their home states this week — a rare shared moment for a trade that otherwise runs on very local weather. We'll take the good omen. Keep an eye on your supplier's pricing notices over the next few weeks, and if you want a second read on what a specific cost increase means for your margin, the Membership & Outreach Committee is glad to talk it through.
