Farmers' market season is fully underway for most of our members in the northern hemisphere, and
by mid-June the early scramble has usually settled into a routine — or exposed the gaps in one. A
few weeks ago we heard from a member whose stand ran out of ice by ten in the morning on a
Saturday that turned out warmer than the forecast promised. That's a small thing, but it's also
exactly the kind of small thing that our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course spends real
time on, because a stand lives or dies on logistics most cheesemakers never had to think about
back when their only customer was a distributor.

Markets around the country are opening their summer seasons this month, and several members have
told us this is the point in the calendar where a stand's early planning either pays off or falls
apart. A slow first Saturday is forgivable. A slow first month usually means something in the
setup needs rethinking before the good weather arrives and the crowds do too.

## What the course actually covers

It isn't a marketing seminar. The bulk of it is operational: how to price for a market crowd that
compares you to the honey stand next door and not to a grocery shelf, how to structure a sample
program that doesn't quietly eat your margin, and how to handle cold-chain logistics for a folding
table instead of a walk-in cooler. There's a module on payment logistics too — card readers, cash
float, and what to do when your connectivity drops mid-transaction, which happens more often at a
county fairground than you'd like.

The course also spends time on the conversation itself. A market customer wants a story, not a
spec sheet — what the milk is, where the herd grazes, why this batch tastes a little different
from the one they bought in April. Members who've taken it tell us the biggest shift wasn't
technical, it was learning to answer "what makes this special" in fifteen seconds instead of five
minutes, because that's about how long you get before the next person in line wants their turn.

Selling direct also changes what a producer actually keeps. A wholesale account takes a cut before
a wheel ever reaches a shopper; a market stand puts the full price in the maker's own hand, which
is part of why so many small creameries treat their market presence as more than a marketing line
item. It's real revenue, and it deserves the same planning a wholesale account would get.

## A quieter kind of due diligence

If you're new to direct sales this season, it's worth checking your state and local rules on
mobile food sales and any permitting specific to your market before your first Saturday, not
after an inspector asks. That's outside what any single course can cover for every jurisdiction,
but it's the first thing we'd tell a first-time vendor to confirm, and most market managers can
point you to the right office if you ask early.

For members who've been doing this for years, the course is also a decent gut-check on habits
that crept in without anyone deciding on them — a sample tray that's grown three cheeses too big,
a price that hasn't moved since a cheaper year for milk. Summer is a natural point to reset both,
and a slow week in June is a better time to do it than a packed one in August.
