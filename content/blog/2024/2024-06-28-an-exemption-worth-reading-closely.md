A federal food-safety proposal moving through the comment process this month would exempt certain fresh cheese categories, made under a specific state-level pasteurized milk standard, from an added layer of supply-chain traceability recordkeeping otherwise scheduled to take effect for the wider food industry over the next two years. We've had enough members ask us whether this means them that it's worth a plain answer: probably not, and here's why the "probably" matters.

The traceability requirement in question asks certain food categories to keep more detailed records of who supplied an ingredient and where a product went next, in a format regulators can request quickly during an outbreak investigation. It's a real compliance lift for a small creamery — new fields in your receiving log, new fields in your shipping log, and a records-retention window longer than a lot of members currently keep. The proposed exemption would carve out one narrow category of fresh cheese made under that specific milk standard. It would not touch aged cheese, washed-rind styles, raw-milk cheese, or most of what a typical federation member actually produces and ships.

## Why the category line matters more than the headline

We're flagging this because we've seen members skim a headline like "cheese exempted from traceability rule" and assume it covers their whole product line. It doesn't, and reading the actual category definition — not the news summary of it — is the only way to know whether a specific product you make falls inside or outside the carve-out. A member producing both a fresh, unaged style and an aged wheel from the same milk could find one exempt and the other still fully in scope, under the same roof.

If your product sits outside the proposed exemption, the compliance clock is still running. That means starting now on the parts of recordkeeping that take the longest to build into a routine: consistent supplier documentation at receiving, and a shipping log detailed enough to trace a batch forward if a recall ever required it. Waiting until the deadline is close to build that habit is the mistake we see most often, because a recordkeeping system that isn't already a habit tends to have gaps exactly when a regulator asks to see it.

## What we're doing, and what we're asking members to do

The federation is preparing formal comment on the proposal, focused on two things: asking for clearer category boundaries so members aren't left guessing which side of the line a given product falls on, and asking for a longer transition window for small creameries that would need to build new recordkeeping systems largely from scratch rather than modify an existing one. Neither ask is about weakening the underlying safety goal — traceability that actually works during an outbreak is worth the administrative cost. The ask is about giving small operations a realistic runway to get there.

If your creamery would be meaningfully affected either way — helped by the exemption or burdened by the full requirement — we want to hear specifics for the comment file. A concrete account of what a compliant recordkeeping system would actually cost you in hours per week carries more weight in a comment letter than a general objection does, and it's the kind of detail regulators say they weigh most heavily.

Members with questions about which category their product falls under, or who want to contribute a comment, should reach the Food Safety Committee through the member portal. The comment window runs through the summer, which gives us time to gather specifics before we file, but not so much time that it should wait until after conference.
