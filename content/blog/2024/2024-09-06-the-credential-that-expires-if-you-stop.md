Most of our credentials expire on a clock. The Sensory Evaluation Certificate is good for three years no matter what you do with it in the meantime. Competition Judge Accreditation is the one exception worth understanding before you start toward it, because it lapses quickly without active judging — you can hold the paperwork and still lose the standing if you go quiet on panels for too long.

That design isn't an accident. Judging a cheese competition well is a perishable skill in a way that, say, knowing the pH targets for a washed-rind wheel isn't. A palate that hasn't scored a panel in two years drifts, and a federation competition is only as credible as the judges behind it. So the accreditation is built to track active practice, not just past training.

## The path onto a panel

The route starts with the Sensory Evaluation Certificate, which covers structured tasting, defect identification, and scoring against style standards. Most members get there through Advanced Sensory Evaluation, which runs multiple cohorts a year and is the usual on-ramp for anyone who's spent years tasting cheese informally but has never had to defend a score against a rubric. The course is more demanding than it sounds from the outside — describing why a wheel is good is easy; scoring it consistently against members you've never met, on criteria that don't move, is a different exercise entirely.

From there, Competition Judge Accreditation requires supervised panel experience on top of the certificate itself. New judges shadow an accredited panel before they score independently, and the Awards & Competition Committee places shadowing slots at regional events throughout the year rather than saving them all for the flagship competition, so there's no need to wait for one specific date to get started.

## What keeps the credential alive

Once accredited, the practical requirement is straightforward: judge regularly. Members who take a panel assignment once or twice a year tend to keep their standing without incident. The ones who run into trouble are usually judges who accredited years ago, moved into a role with less panel time, and are surprised when a renewal check comes back showing a lapse. If that's you, the fix isn't a test — it's getting back on a panel, ideally a smaller regional one first, before you take on a flagship assignment again.

Competition judging conventions elsewhere in the industry follow a similar structure, pairing a technical judge with an aesthetic judge drawn from the retail and distribution side of the trade. We built our own accreditation the same way on purpose: a panel scores better when it isn't made up entirely of cheesemakers judging other cheesemakers. If your background is on the counter or the buying side rather than the make floor, that's not a disadvantage going into the certificate — it's exactly the perspective a balanced panel needs.

If you're weighing whether the credential is worth pursuing at all, ask yourself less about the title and more about whether you actually want to spend a recurring chunk of your calendar tasting other members' cheese under time pressure. For the members who do, it's consistently described as some of the most useful education the federation offers — not because of what it teaches about scoring, but because of how much it teaches about your own palate's blind spots. Contact the Awards & Competition Committee if you want to sit in on a panel before you commit to the certificate.
