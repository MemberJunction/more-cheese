Wholesale cheddar moved again this week, and for once the direction wasn't a surprise. Weekly cheese-market reporting through early September showed both 40-pound cheddar blocks and 500-pound barrels extending gains that had already started in August, with barrel prices in particular pushing toward territory the trade hasn't seen in years. Butter and dry whey firmed alongside them. If you've been watching the spot board with one eye while you salt curds with the other, you already knew something was shifting.

What's newer, and less discussed, is the production side of the story. National milk output has been trending softer year over year in the same reporting, with cow numbers down slightly and per-cow gains essentially flat. Some of that reflects manufacturers shifting capacity toward mozzarella and other export-oriented styles rather than straight cheddar, which tightens the supply of exactly the product whose price is climbing. It's not a dramatic swing on its own. It's the kind of quiet convergence — tighter supply, steady demand — that shows up in a member's margin sheet three months later and gets credited after the fact to "the market finally cooperating."

## What this means for your pricing

A handful of members have asked us this month whether it's time to revisit wholesale contracts locked in over the winter. We don't set your prices for you, and we're not going to pretend a few weeks of data settles a year's negotiating strategy. But if your contract has a reopener tied to a published cheddar benchmark, this is a reasonable week to actually read the clause rather than let it renew on autopilot.

For smaller producers selling direct or through a regional distributor, the practical question is usually simpler than a full renegotiation: does your current price still reflect what block and barrel cheddar are doing, or is it three seasons stale? A lot of the Membership & Outreach Committee's calls this quarter have been exactly that conversation — not "raise your prices" but "check your prices against something current before you decide."

## The part that doesn't show up in the spot report

None of this touches feed costs, which have their own separate and often contradictory trend line, or the labor picture at creameries that are still one sick week away from being short-staffed at the vat. A firmer cheese price is good news, full stop, but it is not the whole balance sheet. Several members in the Midwest told us this year's forage put-up came in ahead of last year's, which helps on one line even as another line moves in a less favorable direction. Reading a single benchmark and calling it a trend is exactly the mistake we'd ask you not to make.

For what it's worth, margin reporting this fall has been unusually favorable across the trade, with feed costs staying soft even as milk prices climbed — a combination that hasn't lined up this cleanly in a couple of years. That's a tailwind for producer members specifically, separate from whatever a cheese buyer or a retail counter is seeing on their end of the same market. It's worth remembering those are two different conversations even when they share a headline number.

If you want the underlying numbers rather than our summary of them, the weekly federal dairy market news report is public and updates every Friday afternoon — worth five minutes with your coffee even if half of it is noise for your particular operation and region.

We'll keep watching the board through the fall. If you want a second opinion on how a specific contract clause reads against current benchmarks, that's a fair question to bring to the Membership & Outreach Committee — they've fielded a few of these already this quarter and can point you to the right people, or at least the right spreadsheet.
