Registration for Webinar: Farm Shop Design opened last week, and it's already tracking ahead of where a virtual session usually sits four weeks out. We think we know why: more members have been asking us, informally, how to sell more of what they make without adding a wholesale account or a second delivery route. A farm shop — or a retail counter bolted onto a production space that never used to see customers — is usually the answer members land on, and this session was built for the ones weighing it seriously.

## Why the interest is showing up now

Direct-to-consumer food sales have been growing steadily for years, and small producers selling cheese, meat, and other value-added goods straight to shoppers make up a meaningful share of that growth nationally. For a lot of our members, that's not news — it's confirmation of something they've already noticed at their own gate or farmers' market stand. What's changed this year is the volume of members asking how to formalize it: a proper retail space, a consistent schedule, signage that doesn't look like an afterthought.

That's a different project than the pop-up table members have run for years. It touches zoning, food-safety inspection categories, staffing on days you didn't used to be open, and a pricing structure that has to work for a walk-in customer, not just a wholesale buyer. None of that is insurmountable, but it's also not something to improvise the week before a holiday rush.

## What the session actually covers

Webinar: Farm Shop Design is scheduled for 19 November and runs through the practical sequence in the order members usually hit it: what a small retail footprint needs structurally, how to think about a register and a case layout in a space that wasn't designed for either, and how to set hours that don't quietly become a second full-time job for whoever's already running the cave. It's a virtual session, open to any member, and it's built to be useful whether you're three months from opening a shop or three years from even deciding to.

We'd gently push back on the instinct to rush this for the holiday season. A farm shop opened in a hurry to catch December traffic tends to show it — in the layout, in the signage, in a staffing plan that falls apart the first busy Saturday. Members who've done this well usually spent a full season planning before they cut a ribbon, testing hours and staffing on a slower month before betting the whole plan on the busiest six weeks of the year. If this webinar gets you moving, treat it as the start of that season, not a shortcut past it.

Members who already run some kind of direct-to-consumer sales — a farmers' market table, a standing delivery route, a card-table stand at the end of the driveway — often have most of the hard parts solved already without realizing it. You've already priced product for a walk-in customer, already figured out packaging that survives handling, already learned which products move and which sit. A farm shop mostly formalizes a system you're already running in miniature; the webinar spends real time on that translation rather than starting from a blank slate, which is the part members tell us they find most useful.

It's also worth being honest about what doesn't change. A retail counter doesn't reduce the workload in your cave or your milking parlor, and members who expect it to free up time are usually disappointed in the first quarter. What it does is give you a second, higher-margin way to sell some of what you're already making, on your own schedule instead of a wholesale buyer's. That distinction is worth sitting with before you commit to a build-out.
