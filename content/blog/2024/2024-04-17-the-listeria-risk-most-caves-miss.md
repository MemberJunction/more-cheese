When members ask us where a listeria problem is most likely to show up, the answer surprises a lot of first-time creamery owners: it's rarely the cheese itself. It's the environment around the cheese - the floor drain nobody's cultured in months, the rubber mat under the cutting table, the wooden aging board that's been in service since the room opened. *Listeria monocytogenes* is a harborage organism. Once it establishes itself in a wet, hard-to-clean corner of a room, it can persist there for years, and every wheel that passes through picks up a little of whatever's living in the corners.

A study published earlier this year sampled small and mid-sized dairy processing facilities to see where listeria actually turns up once a monitoring program is in place, and the pattern held across plants of very different sizes: drains and floors were consistently among the most common positive sites, and the researchers pointed toward simplified, size-appropriate monitoring approaches as the practical fix for smaller operations rather than the more intensive programs designed for large-scale plants. None of that means small operations are careless. It means a drain that isn't part of a deliberate monitoring rotation is remarkably easy to overlook for months at a stretch, in a business of any size. A dairy recall earlier this year, tied to listeria found in a batch of soft cheeses well outside our federation's membership, was a reminder to a lot of our members that this isn't a hypothetical risk reserved for large-scale processors.

## Where to actually swab

A good environmental monitoring plan for a small creamery works in zones. Zone 1 is anything that touches the cheese directly - knives, molds, cutting boards, conveyor surfaces. Zone 2 is non-food-contact surfaces near the product - equipment housings, gaskets, the underside of a table. Zone 3 is the room itself - floors, drains, walls, door thresholds. Zone 4 is everywhere else in the facility, including break rooms and locker areas, because listeria travels on boots and cart wheels just as easily as it travels on hands.

Most of the small-creamery failures our Food Safety Committee has reviewed over the years trace back to Zone 3: a floor drain near a cheese cave that gets swabbed once a year instead of monthly, or a wooden aging shelf that's porous enough to hold moisture and never fully sanitizes. Wood shelving isn't automatically a problem - plenty of well-run affinage rooms use it - but it does require a monitoring rhythm that treats it as a harborage risk, not just a surface to wipe down. A useful reminder from that same research: a positive environmental swab is the start of the work, not the end of it, and the follow-up cleaning and retesting after a positive result matters more than the original test.

A workable plan sets a fixed swabbing schedule (monthly is a common starting point for Zone 2 and 3 sites), a written response protocol for what happens when a swab comes back positive, and a log that shows results over time, not just this month's snapshot. The response protocol matters as much as the schedule: a single positive environmental swab isn't automatically a product recall, but it is a signal that requires an investigation - deeper cleaning, more frequent testing of that site, and a look at whether the surface itself needs to be replaced rather than just re-sanitized.

Our *Listeria Control in Small Creameries* course walks through building this kind of plan from scratch, including how to interpret a positive result without either overreacting or ignoring it, and how to document corrective actions in a way that holds up if a state inspector asks to see your records. It's aimed at creameries that already have a written food-safety plan but haven't built out the environmental side of it in detail.
