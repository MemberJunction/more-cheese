If you make soft or semi-soft cheese, Listeria monocytogenes is the pathogen that keeps quality managers up at night, and this year gave the industry a fresh reminder why. A large soft-cheese recall earlier in 2024, tied to environmental contamination at a processing facility, made national headlines and pushed a lot of our smaller members to ask the same question: if a swab in my own cave came back positive tomorrow, would I actually know what to do next?

The federal food-safety regulator's standing guidance on controlling Listeria in ready-to-eat foods lays out an approach built around zoning your facility and responding in proportion to where a positive turns up. A positive on a food-contact surface — what the guidance calls Zone 1 — calls for a defined response: clean, sanitize, and run intensified sampling on the affected area to confirm the organism is actually cleared before you decide whether a broader recall response is warranted. That's not a loosening of the underlying goal, which is still zero tolerance for Listeria on food-contact surfaces. It's a structured middle step, and the middle steps are exactly where a two-person cave crew is most likely to either overreact and dump product they didn't need to, or underreact and miss the real problem.

## Where our members usually get tripped up

We hear the same three sticking points from members working through this. First, zone mapping — most small facilities have never formally divided their space into Zone 1 through Zone 4, which makes it hard to know where routine swabbing should even happen, let alone what an "intensified" schedule looks like after a positive. Second, drain and floor sanitation, which sounds basic and is exactly where environmental positives keep turning up, because water pools where equipment gets washed down and nobody swabs the drain as often as they swab the slicer. Third, and this is the one that surprises people: employee footwear and glove changes between zones, which is cheap to fix and is still the single most common gap our reviewers flag on a walkthrough.

**Listeria Control in Small Creameries** exists to work through all three of these with people who don't have a food-safety department to lean on — usually the owner, the head cheesemaker, or whoever inherited the food-safety plan when nobody else wanted it. The course covers zone mapping for a real facility layout, a sampling schedule you can actually keep up with between makes, and what an adequate corrective-action record looks like if a regulator or a customer's auditor ever asks to see one.
