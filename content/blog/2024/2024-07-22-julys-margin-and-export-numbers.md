Most of us were still unpacking from Des Moines when July's dairy market figures came out, and they were worth a second look once the conference dust settled. Federal reporting showed Class III milk prices easing slightly through the month while Class IV climbed, narrowing a gap that had been wider earlier in the year, and industry coverage put the farm margin over feed cost well above where it sat twelve months ago. For a lot of members, that's the first stretch in a while where the number on the milk check and the number on the feed bill haven't been pulling in opposite directions.

None of that means every creamery had the same July. A margin figure is an average across a lot of very different operations, and the gap between a bulk fluid producer and a small aged-cheese maker buying retail hay can be wide even when the headline number looks good. If your own numbers didn't move the way the national figure did, that's not a sign you're reading your books wrong — it's a sign the average is doing what averages do, smoothing over exactly the kind of variation that matters most to a small operation.

## What moved on the export side

The other number worth flagging is trade. Federal export data for the month showed U.S. dairy shipments running ahead of the same period last year, with cheese, skim milk products, and dry whey doing most of the lifting. For members who sell wholesale into export-facing supply chains — even indirectly, through a distributor who blends domestic and export orders — that's a tailwind worth knowing about heading into fall planning, even if it never shows up as a line item on your own invoice.

We'd caution against reading too much into any single month. Earlier this year, analysts covering global dairy markets were already tempering expectations for how fast prices would recover, and export volumes still move on currency, on competing suppliers overseas, and on buying patterns that have nothing to do with U.S. production. But a month where domestic margin and export demand both moved in a favorable direction at the same time is not the norm, and it's worth noting while it's true rather than assuming it'll still hold by the time you're setting fall pricing. A few members who exhibited at Des Moines told us the trade-desk conversations at this year's conference were more upbeat than they've been in several cycles, which tracks with what the federal numbers are showing.

## What to do with a good month

If July gave your operation some breathing room, this is a reasonable week to use it — paying down a piece of equipment financing early, building a small cash buffer ahead of the fall feed-buying season, or finally scheduling that cave repair you've been putting off since spring. None of that is exciting advice, but margin months don't come around on a schedule, and the members who do best with them are usually the ones who treat them as a one-time opportunity rather than a new baseline they can count on repeating.

If your own numbers don't match the national trend and you want a second set of eyes on what's driving the gap, our member directory has a handful of accountants and consultants who work specifically with creameries and specialty producers — a conversation with someone who reads dairy books for a living is often worth more than another month of guessing on your own.
