A recall notice for a batch of soft cheese made the trade press again this month, and a few members have written in asking what it means for them. We're not going to name the producer or repeat the details of an investigation that isn't ours to comment on, but the pattern is a familiar one and worth using as a prompt: a routine sample turns up a positive, a batch gets pulled, and everyone downstream from a shared ingredient or a shared facility starts checking their own records. That last part is where our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate actually lives.

The certificate covers hazard analysis, environmental monitoring, and recall readiness specifically for small and mid-sized creameries, and it's the shortest-validity credential we offer at two years, on purpose. Food safety practice moves faster than most areas of the craft, and a plan that was sound when you wrote it can quietly go stale as your equipment, your suppliers, or your production volume changes underneath it. If your certificate is coming up for renewal this spring, this is a reasonable week to actually start that process instead of letting it drift to the deadline.

## What "recall readiness" means in practice

It's easy to hear "recall readiness" and picture a binder nobody opens until there's a problem. What it actually means, in the version we teach, is a short list of things you can find and act on quickly: which lots came from which milk source and on which day, where a given batch physically went after it left your building, and who you'd need to call, in what order, if a test came back positive. Members who've been through an actual recall, even a small voluntary one, consistently tell us the same thing afterward — the plan mattered less than how fast they could answer basic questions under pressure, and that speed only comes from having practiced the lookup, not just written it down.

One quality manager who recertified last year told us she treats her environmental monitoring log the way she treats her make sheets: reviewed weekly, not just filed. "The certificate class is where I learned to actually read my own swab results instead of just collecting them," she said. "Before that, I had the data. I didn't have a habit of looking at it until something already went wrong." That shift, from collecting records to actually reading them on a schedule, is most of what separates a paper HACCP plan from one that holds up when it's tested.

## If your certificate is due, or you've never started one

If you're recertifying, our next cohort opens for registration in February, and current holders get first access to seats before we open it more broadly. If you've never taken the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate and you're running your own environmental monitoring program from scratch, start with the foundations course first; the certificate assumes you already know your basic hazard categories and builds recall planning and documentation on top of that base.

None of this is a claim that any particular practice keeps a creamery out of a recall notice — nothing does, entirely, and any credential that promised otherwise wouldn't be telling you the truth. What the certificate is built to do is shorten the distance between a problem showing up in a test result and your creamery being able to say, clearly and quickly, exactly what happened and what you did next. That gap is usually where the real damage gets done, and it's the one thing a good HACCP habit can actually shrink.
