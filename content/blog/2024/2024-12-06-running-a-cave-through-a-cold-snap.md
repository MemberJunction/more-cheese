Federal energy forecasters expect most households to see relatively little change in heating costs this winter compared with last, with propane averaging a bit less than a year ago nationally even though regional prices vary widely. For most households that's an inconvenience. For a cave that's trying to hold a steady 50–55°F and 85–90% humidity through a stretch of hard cold, it's the difference between a make sheet that comes out right and one that doesn't.

Cold snaps don't just threaten your heating bill. They threaten consistency. A cave that swings ten degrees over a weekend because the heater is cycling harder than it should stresses rinds, slows or speeds ripening unevenly across a batch, and can push humidity out of range right when you can least afford to open the door and check. Several members have written in over the past few winters asking some version of the same question: is it worth running the heater harder, or should we be managing the room differently?

## What actually drives cave stability

The Cave Management course spends real time on this, and the short version is that insulation and airflow do more for stability than raw heating capacity. A well-insulated room with a modest heater holds steadier than a leaky one with a powerful heater working overtime, and the powerful heater is also the one running up your bill. If your cave loses heat fast enough that the heater short-cycles — on hard, off hard, on hard again — you're both wasting energy and putting more temperature swing into the room than a slower, steadier system would.

A few things worth checking before the coldest weeks hit:

- **Seal the obvious gaps first.** Door sweeps, vent covers, and gaps around any plumbing or wiring penetrations are usually cheaper to fix than a heater upgrade, and they do more for stability.
- **Separate your heating and humidity systems if you can.** A single system trying to do both jobs at once tends to overcorrect one while chasing the other, especially in a cold snap when the outside air is also very dry.
- **Log your swings, not just your setpoints.** A thermometer that reads 52°F once a day tells you less than a data logger that shows you swung from 48 to 58 overnight. The course walks through cheap logging setups that catch this before it shows up in a batch.
- **Know your room's thermal mass.** Stone and earth-floor rooms hold temperature longer and swing less; a converted refrigerated trailer or metal shed swings fast and needs more active management in a cold snap.

## Why this is a food-safety question, not just a comfort one

Temperature and humidity stability aren't cosmetic. They're part of what keeps a cave's microbial environment predictable, which is a food-safety concern as much as a quality one — an unstable cave is harder to validate against your own food-safety plan, because the conditions you documented aren't the conditions you're actually running. If your plan describes a steady range and your logs show swings, that gap is worth closing before an inspector or a buyer asks about it, not after.

The Cave Management course covers all of this in more depth, including sizing a system to your room rather than your budget, and it's a reasonable one to have your team work through before the coldest stretch of winter rather than during it. Check the course calendar for the next cohort, and if your cave is fighting this winter's cold the way a lot of members' are, the Food Safety Committee is also a good place to ask what other members are doing to hold steady.
