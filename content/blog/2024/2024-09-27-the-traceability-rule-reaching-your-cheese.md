A federal rule finalized a couple of years ago is starting to show up in more member questions this fall, and it's worth walking through now rather than waiting until the compliance date is closer. It requires businesses that manufacture, process, pack, or hold certain higher-risk foods to keep enhanced traceability records — who sent you the milk, what lot it was, what you made from it, and where each batch of the finished cheese went next.

Hard cheeses made from pasteurized milk are largely outside the rule's scope. Soft, semi-soft, and any cheese made from unpasteurized milk other than a hard cheese are squarely inside it. That split matters because it doesn't track how a lot of members think about risk day to day — a small creamery that makes both a washed-rind and a cheddar is fully covered on one and exempt on the other, with the same milk coming in the same door.

A narrower carve-out moving through the rulemaking process this year shows how granular the line-drawing gets: regulators proposed exempting a specific category of cottage cheese, sold under a particular interstate milk-shipping program, from the recordkeeping requirement, while leaving every other soft cheese covered. Members read that and asked us the obvious question — if regulators are willing to exempt one narrowly defined product, why not others that carry comparable risk? We don't have an answer yet, but it's the kind of precedent our comment letter can point to.

## What the rule actually asks for

The recordkeeping is more specific than most food-safety paperwork members already keep. It calls for assigning a traceability lot code to a batch, capturing a defined set of data at defined "critical tracking events" — receiving, transforming, shipping — and being able to hand a regulator those records within 24 hours of a request. For a one- or two-person production floor, that last part is the sticking point: the records have to exist before anyone asks for them, in a form somebody can actually pull together same-day, not reconstructed from memory and a stack of invoices.

Our advocacy work this fall has centered on interstate cheese shipment specifically, because that's where the rule's reach is broadest and least forgiving. A creamery shipping wholesale across state lines is covered regardless of size in a way that a purely local farm-stand seller often isn't, and members have told us the rule reads as if it assumes a packing line, not a curd table. We've been pushing for recordkeeping guidance that's actually usable at that scale — plain-language critical-tracking-event examples built around a small creamery's real workflow, not a processor's, and a clearer answer on what a two-person operation can reasonably keep on paper versus in a spreadsheet.

We're also asking regulators to publish worked examples for the product types our members actually make — a washed-rind wheel sold through a single distributor looks nothing like a bagged shred line, and the guidance so far reads as if it was written with the second one in mind.

## What to do before the deadline gets close

You don't need to build a new system this month. You do need to know which of your products fall inside the rule's food list and start keeping the lot-code and shipping records now, even informally, so the habit is in place before it's mandatory. If you already track batches for your own recall plan, you're most of the way there — the traceability rule mostly asks you to keep those same records in a specific, retrievable shape and be ready to hand them over fast.

If you ship wholesale or across state lines and you're not sure whether your make falls inside the covered list, that's exactly the kind of question our advocacy line exists for — reach out through the member portal and we'll help you work through it, and pass along what we're hearing to the working group drafting our comment letter.
