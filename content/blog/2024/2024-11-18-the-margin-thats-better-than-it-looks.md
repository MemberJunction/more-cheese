A few members have asked us some version of the same question lately: is the margin picture actually as good as the trade coverage makes it sound, or is that a national number that doesn't mean much on a single farm. It's a fair thing to ask. Income-over-feed-cost margins — the spread between what a farm earns per hundredweight of milk and what it costs to feed the herd that produced it — have run unusually wide for most of this year, with milk prices holding firm and feed costs staying soft. Trade reporting has been calling the combination one of the stronger margin stretches in recent memory.

That's real, and it's worth taking seriously if your own numbers are showing the same pattern. But a national average margin and your farm's margin are related, not identical, and the gap between them is exactly where members get into trouble planning a year ahead on someone else's spreadsheet.

## What the wide margin actually reflects

The margin ran wide this year for two separate reasons that happen to be pointing the same direction at once: milk prices held up better than the softer stretch earlier in the year suggested they might, and feed costs — grain in particular — eased from where they'd been. Neither of those conditions is guaranteed to hold. Feed costs move with a planting season and a set of trade conditions that can shift in a single crop report. Milk prices move with herd size, export demand, and how aggressively cheese and butter inventories build or draw down. A margin built on two favorable inputs at once is worth appreciating this year. It is not a baseline to assume next year.

For members who feed a herd directly, the practical question isn't whether the margin is good right now — it obviously is, for a lot of operations — it's whether to lock in any of that margin while it's available. Forward contracts on feed, milk marketing agreements, and other tools exist precisely for stretches like this one, and members who've weathered a tight year or two tend to be the ones who treat a wide margin as a chance to buy some insurance against the next narrow one, not as a signal to expand spending against it.

## What it means if you don't feed a herd

Members who buy milk rather than produce it — cheesemakers sourcing from a co-op or a specific farm relationship — see this margin picture from the other side. A wide farm-level margin generally means calmer milk supply and steadier pricing on your end, which is a genuinely useful planning signal heading into contract renewals. It's also worth remembering that a good margin year for your milk supplier is not the same thing as a good year for your own production costs, which move on packaging, energy, and labor lines that don't track feed prices at all.

We'd rather members treat this margin stretch as useful information than as a forecast. If you're weighing a capital purchase, a herd expansion, or a longer supply contract on the strength of this year's numbers, it's worth running the decision against a tighter scenario too — what the math looks like if feed costs climb back to where they were eighteen months ago, or milk softens the way it did earlier this year. Members who've been through a margin swing before tend to plan for the range, not the best case.
