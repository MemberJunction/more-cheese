Most of this newsletter's December content assumes the same thing: your cave is cold, your case is full of gift sets, and you're bracing for a two-week sprint before the calendar turns. A member on Tasmania's southeast coast is looking at a completely different week. She co-owns a sheep dairy with her partner, who runs the flock, and this time of year the ewes are at peak lactation, the paddocks are green, and her affinage room is fighting heat, not cold.

"People here ask why our busiest cheese season lines up with everyone else's slow one," she told us. "It's not really a mystery — it's just that lambing happens in winter for us, which is your summer, and the milk peaks a few months after that. By December we've got the most milk we'll see all year and the most daylight to work it in."

## Running a cave against the season, not with it

She's been an ICF member for a few years now, joining not long after taking over full-time cheese production. The federation's course catalog and competition calendar are built around a Northern Hemisphere rhythm — spring workshops, a fall flagship conference, winter credential pushes — and she's gotten used to translating. A recent affinage course landed for her in the middle of her own harvest season rather than a quiet stretch, and the annual competition's submission window falls when Tasmania is at its hottest, which makes shipping wheels a logistics puzzle her Northern Hemisphere counterparts don't have to think about.

What doesn't need translating is the cheese itself. Her sheep-milk wheels age in a converted stone outbuilding that holds humidity well but needs active cooling assistance through December and January — the reverse of the heating problem most members are managing in their own caves this month. "We joke that our December is everyone else's July," she said. "Same problem, opposite season. You're trying to keep the room from getting too warm and drying out; we're doing the same thing at the same time of year everyone else is trying to keep theirs from freezing."

## What the federation is for, from the far side of the map

Being a member from the Southern Hemisphere means most live events happen at inconvenient hours, and the newsletter's seasonal rhythm rarely matches the actual seasons outside her window. What's kept the membership useful, she says, is the parts that don't care about hemisphere at all — the food-safety guidance, the standards documents, the sensory vocabulary that competition judges use no matter where a wheel was made. "The webinars I watch at 6 a.m. my time are the same ones everyone else watches at a normal hour. The content doesn't know what season it is. That's the part that travels."

If your own membership has ever felt like it was built for someone else's calendar, her experience is a useful data point: the credentials and the standards work travel fine even when the seasons don't. And if you're looking for a December read that isn't about holiday prep, her caves are worth thinking about the next time you're checking a dehumidifier instead of a heater.
