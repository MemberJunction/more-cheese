Ask a handful of members what changes in their cave between March and June, and you'll get some version of the same answer: not the setpoint, but how hard the equipment has to work to hold it. A room that coasted through winter on a modest dehumidifier cycle can suddenly need twice the runtime once the outside air warms up and starts pushing moisture in every time a door opens. If you haven't walked your cave with that question in mind yet this year, late May is a reasonable week to do it.

Most cheeses mature best in a fairly narrow band — around 50 to 55°F — but the humidity target moves a lot by style, and that's the number that gets away from people first. Hard, natural-rind styles like a territorial cheddar generally want the low-to-mid 80s percent relative humidity, while a bloomy or washed-rind wheel wants a stickier 90 to 95 percent. Run either style at the wrong end of that range for long enough and the failure mode is predictable: too dry and rinds crack while wheels lose weight and flavor faster than planned; too wet and you get slick, slipping rinds and mold you didn't ask for.

## What's actually worth checking this week

A few things drift together as ambient humidity climbs, and it's worth separating them instead of chasing one symptom:

- **Sensor placement.** A hygrometer mounted near the door reads differently than one at the back of the room, and as outside humidity rises, that gap widens. If you're only checking one spot, you're probably reading the easiest number, not the truest one.
- **Recovery time after handling.** Watch how long the room takes to settle back to setpoint after a flip-and-wash session with the door open. If that recovery window has stretched noticeably since March, your equipment margin is shrinking before you've hit peak summer conditions.
- **Airflow, not just humidity.** A room can hold the right humidity number on the gauge and still develop uneven surface mold because air isn't moving evenly across every shelf. This is the piece people notice last, usually after a rind problem shows up on one specific rack.

None of this requires new equipment before you've confirmed it's actually a problem. It requires walking the room with a notebook for a week and comparing what you find to what the wheels themselves are telling you — a rind that's drying unevenly is data, same as a hygrometer reading.

This is exactly the territory the Humidity & Airflow Control course was built to cover, and it pairs naturally with the Advanced Affinage Certificate for members who want the fuller maturation-planning picture — rind management and cave environment control together, not as separate problems. Members managing multiple styles in one room have told us the biggest shift isn't equipment, it's checking the room on a schedule instead of when something already looks wrong. Late May, before the real heat arrives, is about the last easy window to make that switch for the season.
