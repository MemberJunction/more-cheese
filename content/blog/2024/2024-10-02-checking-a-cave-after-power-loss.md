Between the storm-affected creameries calling this week and the ordinary run of cave incidents that happen every fall regardless of weather, we've fielded enough of the same question that it's worth writing down the answer once, plainly: your cave held above safe temperature for some period of time. What now?

Utility crews are still working through the hardest-hit counties, and hundreds of thousands of customers across the region remain without power days after the storm. For a member running a generator off a trailer, or waiting in a queue behind homes and hospitals for a line crew, "how long until my cave is back in range" isn't a hypothetical — it's the actual question of the week. So let's answer the one underneath it: once power is back, what do you do with what's inside?

The honest first step is not "smell it and decide." Listeria monocytogenes doesn't announce itself in the rind, and it tolerates refrigeration temperatures far better than most other pathogens of concern — which is exactly why a cave running warm for even a day or two changes the risk profile of a soft or washed-rind wheel more than it changes a hard aged one. Moisture, surface pH, and temperature all move together when a cave loses climate control, and a wheel that looked fine going in can be a different food-safety question coming out.

## The practical sequence

Start with what you actually know: how long was the cave out of range, and by how much. A brief dip that stayed under 50°F is a different conversation than four days above 65°F. Document it — a logged thermometer reading, even a rough one from a phone photo of a dial, is worth more after the fact than your memory of "it felt warm." Next, separate inventory by risk category rather than treating the whole room the same way. Fresh and soft-ripened cheeses in the affected window deserve the most conservative read; long-aged hard wheels with low moisture and a stable rind are lower risk but not zero risk, especially if the same warm air touched a shared aging space.

General guidance on refrigerated food after an outage draws a line around the four-hour mark for most perishables above roughly 40°F, and dairy is treated conservatively within that guidance. A cave is not a home refrigerator — the volumes are larger, the airflow is different, and a wheel's core temperature lags the room's air temperature by hours, not minutes — but the underlying caution is the same: time above safe temperature is cumulative, and it does not reset just because the power came back on.

Where you have any doubt about a specific batch — and "doubt" here means you cannot document that it stayed in safe range — that batch does not go out for sale on your judgment call alone. This is the one place where we'd rather see a member lose a batch than lose a customer's trust, or worse. If you need a second opinion on a specific lot, our food-safety-adjacent membership can point you toward a lab; we don't make that call for you and we're not going to pretend a blog post can either.
