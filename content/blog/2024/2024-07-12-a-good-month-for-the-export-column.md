Two numbers moved in the same direction this summer, and that doesn't happen every year: block and barrel cheddar prices climbed, and export volumes climbed with them instead of stalling out the way they sometimes do when domestic prices firm up. For members who sell wholesale, that's a rarer and better combination than it sounds.

Block cheddar has been trading up over the past several weeks, and barrel prices have moved even more. At the same time, feed costs have eased from where they sat a year ago, which means the farm-level margin between milk revenue and feed expense has widened noticeably. For a dairy farm supplying a member creamery, that's the difference between a summer spent watching the margin and a summer spent planning around it.

## Why exports didn't pull back this time

Usually, when domestic cheese prices rise, export competitiveness softens — a higher price at home makes a shipment overseas a harder sell. This year, overall dairy exports rose year over year anyway, with the growth concentrated in cheese, skim milk products, and dry whey. That suggests the demand pulling exports up is coming from buyers who want the product regardless of the domestic price move, not from a temporary currency or logistics quirk that could reverse just as fast.

That matters differently depending on where you sit in the supply chain. If you're a small creamery selling almost entirely through a farmers' market stand or a local counter, none of this touches your business directly — you're priced by your own costs, not by a commodity block-and-barrel market. But if any part of your product moves through a distributor with export relationships, or if your own milk supply comes from a larger operation exposed to that market, this summer's numbers are the ones setting the tone for pricing conversations you'll have later this year.

## The longer trend underneath it

The commodity swing is a month-to-month story, but it sits inside a longer one: the specialty and artisan segment of the category has kept growing steadily since the pandemic pulled back in 2021, driven by buyers who want something more distinctive than a mass-market block. That's the trend several members have described feeling directly at the counter — a customer willing to pay more for a wheel with a story behind it, and a wholesale buyer more willing to carry a smaller producer's line than they were five years ago.

None of this changes what any individual member should charge this week. It's context for a pricing conversation, not a directive — your own cost structure, your own relationships with your accounts, and your own read on your regulars still matter more than a national commodity number. But if a distributor or a buyer brings up "the market's up right now" as a reason to renegotiate terms, it's worth knowing which part of that is true and which part is just leverage.
