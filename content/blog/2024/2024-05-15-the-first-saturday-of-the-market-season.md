Farmers markets across a lot of our members' regions opened for the season this month, and one small farmstead operation told us this week that their first Saturday back at the folding table is the closest thing they have to a report card. Not the annual numbers, not a wholesale contract — the first hour a customer they haven't seen since October walks up and asks what's new.

This particular member runs a mixed-milk operation with a small cave and an even smaller crew, and their opening weekend this year came with two new wheels they'd been aging since the fall specifically to have something ready for market day one. That's not a small decision for an operation their size. Cave space is the scarcest resource a farmstead has, and choosing to hold two wheels back from wholesale, uncertain whether the market crowd would want them, is a bet made in October on a Saturday in May.

## The six months nobody at the table sees

What a market customer experiences as a table full of cheese is, on the production side, a make schedule set back in the fall specifically to land at spring markets, a cave rotation that had to accommodate those wheels without crowding out everything selling wholesale in the meantime, and a set of labeling and tasting-sample decisions made well before the first customer of the season ever showed up. None of that shows up in the conversation at the table, and that's exactly the point — a good market table is supposed to look effortless.

The member told us the actual hard part isn't the cheese, it's the reintroduction. A regular customer who hasn't been back since the market closed for winter needs a different conversation than a first-timer does — less explaining what the cheese is, more catching them up on what's changed since October. Getting that balance right, table by table, all morning, is its own kind of skill that has nothing to do with what happens in the cave.

## What this means if you're planning your own opening weekend

If you're a smaller operation weighing whether to hold cheese back from wholesale for a market debut, the calculation this member described is worth borrowing: pick a number of wheels small enough that a slow market day doesn't hurt your wholesale commitments, and treat the first weekend as information-gathering rather than a targeted sales figure. What sells out by ten a.m. and what sits untouched by noon tells you more about this year's crowd than any amount of planning from behind a desk in March.

It's also a good moment to remind members that a market table is a face-to-face sales channel in a way wholesale never is, and the conversation skills that make it work — reading a customer's hesitation, knowing when to offer a taste versus when to just answer the question asked — aren't something every make-focused cheesemaker walks in already having. If your crew is newer to market selling this season, that's a fair thing to practice on a slow Tuesday before you're standing behind the table on a busy Saturday.

Whatever your own opening weekend looked like this month, our Membership & Outreach Committee would like to hear about it — what sold, what didn't, and what you're already planning to change before next spring.
