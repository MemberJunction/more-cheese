A member reached out to us last month after a shipment of aged wheels got flagged at a distribution warehouse two states over, not because anything was wrong with the cheese, but because the paperwork accompanying it didn't clearly establish something the federal rule actually requires: that raw-milk cheese crossing a state line has to be cured for at least 60 days at a controlled temperature before it ships. She knew the rule. Her label said "raw milk" and gave an age range. What she didn't have on hand, when the warehouse manager called, was documentation tying that specific lot to a make date that cleared the threshold. The shipment sat for two extra days while she found it.

That's the kind of gap our Advocacy program exists to close, and it's a different kind of problem than the raw-milk sales debates that get most of the legislative attention. Selling raw milk itself across a state line is prohibited outright under long-standing federal rules. Raw-milk cheese is the one carve-out — legal to ship interstate, provided it clears the 60-day curing floor. Most of our producer members who make raw-milk cheese know this rule exists. Fewer have a clean, contemporaneous record that proves it for any given batch, which is exactly the document a distributor, a state inspector, or a nervous warehouse manager will ask for first.

## Why this keeps tripping up members who know the rule

Part of the problem is that the rule sits at the federal level, but enforcement and documentation expectations vary by where the cheese lands. Some states have clear guidance on what counts as adequate proof of curing time; others leave it to the receiving party's judgment, which means the same lot of cheese can clear one state's dock without a second look and get held at another's over a paperwork gap. A member shipping to three different regional accounts may be facing three different informal standards for what "proof" looks like, none of them written down anywhere she can point to. Regulatory attention to dairy standards has been active on more than one front this winter, which is one more reason we'd rather members have this settled before an inspector or a distributor asks, not after.

We hear a version of this story a few times a year, and it's rarely about a producer trying to skirt the rule. It's almost always a producer who followed it and still got tangled in a documentation gap nobody had flagged for them in advance — a batch log that tracks make date but not the specific lot that shipped, or a label that states an age range instead of the actual clearance date for that pallet.

## What we're asking members to send us

The Standards Committee is compiling examples of exactly this kind of friction — a shipment held, a distributor asking for documentation a member didn't have ready, a state agent applying a stricter standard than a neighboring state. We're not drafting new federal rules ourselves; that's above our lane. But when member examples pile up, we can put together clearer guidance for our own membership on what a defensible paper trail actually looks like, lot by lot, so fewer of you learn this the way our member did — with a truck idling at a dock.

If you make raw-milk cheese and ship across state lines, the practical fix is simpler than the rule sounds: keep a batch log that ties each shipped lot to its make date, calculate and note the exact date it clears 60 days at temperature rather than relying on a range, and make sure your label language matches what that specific lot can actually prove. It's a recordkeeping habit, not a legal filing, and our Food Safety Committee can point you to members who've built a version of this log worth borrowing from.

Send your own interstate shipment stories, good or bad, to the Standards Committee through the member portal. The more of these we collect, the better the guidance we can put in front of the whole membership before the next truck gets held at a dock over a rule everyone actually followed.
