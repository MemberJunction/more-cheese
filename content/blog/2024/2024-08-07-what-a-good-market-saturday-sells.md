This is the week the broader farm-to-market world sets aside to talk up direct sales, and it lands right in the middle of market season for most of our direct-to-consumer members — the point in the summer where a stand's habits have either paid off or they haven't. If your table looks the same today as it did in June, this is a good week to ask why.

## The stand is not the case

The most common mistake we hear about from members new to market selling is treating the table as a smaller version of a retail case — same wheels, same labels, arranged the same way, just outdoors. A market table works on different rules. Shoppers walking a row of stands are deciding in seconds whether to stop, and what stops them is rarely the cheese itself; it's a taste offered without being asked twice, a one-line answer to "what's this one like," and a stand person who isn't staring at a phone. None of that shows up in a wholesale relationship, where the buyer already knows your line and just wants the invoice to be right.

Members who've built a strong market presence tend to rotate what's up front every week or two, even when the underlying inventory barely changes — a different wheel gets the sample plate, a different note gets written on the little chalkboard sign. It keeps a regular customer's Saturday from feeling identical to the one before, which turns out to matter more than most cheesemakers expect going in. A few members have told us they keep a running list of which sample got the best reaction each week, just so the rotation isn't guesswork by September.

Pricing is the other habit worth copying, and it's less about the number than how you say it. Members who do well at markets tend to state their prices out loud, unprompted, before anyone asks. "That's eighteen dollars a pound, and it's worth tasting first" does more work than a price tag alone, because it signals confidence rather than apology. If you've ever caught yourself explaining a price defensively — "well, it takes six months to age" — that's usually a sign you haven't decided the price is fair yet yourself. Settle that in the quiet of your own kitchen table, and the market conversation gets a lot shorter.

## Where this gets taught properly

Our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course covers this ground in more depth than a blog post can — stand layout, sampling logistics that stay within your local health rules, how to build a repeat-customer list without a point-of-sale system built for it, and how to think through a market's true hourly return once you count the drive, the setup, and the teardown. It runs as a cohort a few times a year, and members who've taken it tend to say the pricing-out-loud habit alone was worth the enrollment.

It's also a course that pairs well with a second look at your own numbers. A stand that feels busy isn't automatically a stand that's profitable once travel time and unsold product are counted honestly, and the course spends real time on that math rather than treating a good crowd as proof enough on its own.

If your market season still has six or eight Saturdays left in it, there's time to test one change before the season ends rather than waiting until next spring to try it fresh. Pick the thing on your table that's been static the longest, and move it.
