Interstate cheese shipment has been a recurring topic in our advocacy work for years, usually
tied to labeling harmonization or licensing reciprocity between states. This year it's tied to
something newer: testing requirements for lactating dairy cattle that move across state lines,
put in place as part of a broader response to a livestock health concern that's affected herds
in multiple regions this year. For members who ship milk, cultures, or animals across a state
border, that testing requirement is now simply part of doing business — and members are telling
us it's adding real time and cost to routine movements that used to be straightforward.

We want to be careful about what we are and aren't saying here. This is a herd-health and food
safety measure, not a controversy, and our position isn't that testing is wrong. It's that the
logistics of compliance — where samples get collected, how fast results come back, who pays for
delays while a shipment waits on paperwork — have landed hardest on exactly the members with the
least slack in their schedule: small farmstead operations without a dedicated compliance staffer
and without the buffer time to absorb a two-day hold on a truck.

## What members are telling us

One farmstead member who ships across a state line every few weeks described the change bluntly:
"It's not that I object to the testing. It's that nobody built in time for the result to come
back before the truck was supposed to leave." Another member, a distributor who moves product
through several states, said scheduling has become the real cost. "The test itself takes an
afternoon. Working it into a shipping schedule that was already tight has taken most of the fall
to figure out."

These aren't complaints about the underlying goal of the rule. They're complaints about
implementation — the gap between what a testing requirement looks like on paper and what it
actually costs a small operation to build into a weekly schedule, especially heading into the
holiday season when shipment volumes are climbing and nobody has spare days to lose to a delay.

## What we're asking for, and what this doesn't cover

Our Membership & Outreach Committee has been compiling specific timing and cost data from
members affected by the new requirements, and we're using it to push for two things in
conversations with the relevant state agriculture offices: faster standard turnaround windows
for routine test results, and clearer advance notice when a testing rule changes so members
aren't caught reworking a shipping schedule with a week's notice. Neither ask changes the
underlying safety requirement. Both are aimed at making it survivable for a member without a
compliance department.

If your operation has run into scheduling friction, cost surprises, or unclear guidance around
interstate testing this fall, we want to hear about it specifically — dates, states involved,
and what the delay actually cost you in product or time. That kind of detail is what turns a
general complaint into something a regulator can act on.

To be clear, this post is about interstate movement of milk and cattle, not about food safety
practices inside your own creamery or cave. If you have questions about how any testing or
recordkeeping requirement applies to your specific operation, that's a conversation for your
state's food safety regulator, not a blog post. Our Food Safety Committee can help you find the
right point of contact if you're not sure where to start.

Members who want to add their experience to what we're bringing to regulators can reach the
Membership & Outreach Committee through the advocacy page on the member portal.
