With this year's regional competitions starting to post their entry windows, our office has been fielding a steady run of questions from members about how someone actually becomes a judge - not a taster with strong opinions, but a credentialed judge whose scoresheet counts toward an official placing. The short answer is that tasting well and judging well aren't the same skill, and our Competition Judge Accreditation is built around that distinction.

## Palate isn't the hard part

Most members who come to us wanting judge accreditation already have a trained palate - years behind a counter or in a cave will do that on its own. What the accreditation actually tests is something narrower: can you score a wheel against a fixed category standard, consistently, even when your personal taste runs the other way? A judge who happens to love an aggressive, funky washed-rind style has to score a mild one fairly against its own category's standard, not against a competing style they'd rather be eating.

A recent academic review of taste-panel scoring across official cheese competitions made a point worth repeating here: judge disagreement usually isn't about whose palate is "better," it's about inconsistent application of the same criteria from one entry to the next. That's exactly what our written and tasting exam is built to catch. Candidates score a flight of wheels independently, then compare their scores against a panel of accredited judges scoring the same flight. Consistent scoring - not necessarily agreement with every panel member, but internal consistency and defensible reasoning on the write-up - is what moves a candidate through. Someone can be an excellent, discerning taster and still fail their first attempt because their reasoning on paper doesn't hold up under a category standard, not because their palate was wrong.

The accreditation also covers competition mechanics that have nothing to do with tasting - chain-of-custody rules for entries, conflict-of-interest disclosure when a judge knows an entrant, and how to write feedback that's useful to a competitor without contradicting the numeric score. A judge who scores a wheel low but writes vague, encouraging comments creates exactly the kind of confusing feedback our Awards & Competition Committee hears about most often from members after results are posted. Learning to write a scoresheet comment that matches the number is, in practice, half of what separates an accredited judge from an enthusiastic volunteer. Candidates also sit in on a live competition as a shadow judge before their own scores count toward a result, watching how a panel resolves disagreements and handles a questionable entry - one that may have been mislabeled by category, for instance. That shadowing requirement tends to be the part candidates find most useful in hindsight, even though it can feel like a slow step at the time.

## Who this is actually for

We get asked whether Competition Judge Accreditation is worth pursuing for a member who has no interest in judging competitions themselves. Generally, no - it's a specific credential for a specific role, not a broader stamp of tasting expertise, and members looking for that kind of general credential are usually better served starting with our Sensory Evaluation Certificate instead. But for members who already volunteer at regional judgings informally and want their scoresheets to count officially, accreditation is the difference between tasting alongside the panel and actually being on it.

Application windows for this cycle's accreditation cohort open through our member portal, and our Awards & Competition Committee can walk any interested member through prerequisites before they apply.
