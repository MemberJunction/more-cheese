Every cave has a logbook, and every logbook eventually changes hands. At Alpine Crest Fromagerie, that handoff happened faster than planned when the affineur who'd kept the cave notes for eleven years retired ahead of schedule last fall, leaving an apprentice barely two years into the trade holding a decade of handwriting she hadn't written herself.

"The notes weren't hard to read," she told us. "They were hard to trust. He'd shorthand things — 'turned early, felt right' — and I didn't have the years behind me yet to know what 'felt right' meant with my own hands." Rather than start a new log from scratch, she spent her first few months cross-referencing his old entries against what she was seeing in the cave in real time, building her own judgment against his as a check.

## Working toward the credential, not just the job

She's now partway through the federation's Advanced Affinage Certificate, which she started specifically because of the handoff, not in spite of it. "I could have just kept doing the job without it," she said. "But I wanted something that told me, independently of his notes, whether I was actually reading a wheel correctly — not just copying what worked for him." The certificate's practical component asks candidates to track and defend affinage decisions on wheels they didn't start, which turned out to map almost exactly onto the situation she was already in.

That's not a coincidence the credential was built to catch, but it's a real overlap worth naming for any member in a similar spot: if you've inherited a cave, a program, or a set of standards from someone who isn't there to explain their shorthand anymore, the Advanced Affinage Certificate is designed to test whether your read on a wheel holds up without them, not just whether you can follow their notes. Affinage as a discipline is fundamentally a set of judgment calls layered on top of a few fixed variables — temperature, humidity, air movement, time — and two affineurs can make defensibly different calls on the same wheel. The certificate doesn't try to collapse that into a single right answer; it asks candidates to show their reasoning and stand behind it, which is a different and, she says, harder skill than following someone else's shorthand.

## What's changed since

Eleven years of entries is a lot to sit with, and she says the biggest shift isn't technical — it's confidence in overriding a note when her own read disagrees with it. "For the first few months I followed his instructions even when something felt off, because I assumed he knew better," she said. "Now I write down when I disagree with an old entry and why, and about half the time I turn out to be right. That's new. It wasn't there at the start."

Winter humidity has been the harder half of the job this year regardless of whose notes she's reading, and she's been leaning on other members' cave logs — shared informally, not through any formal channel — to sanity-check her own readings against caves built differently than hers. She's also started keeping a second notebook alongside the inherited one: not a replacement, but a running list of the handful of house rules she's decided to keep exactly as written, because she's tested them enough times to trust them on their own terms rather than out of deference to whoever wrote them down first.

She's not sure yet what she'll do with the original logbook once her own has caught up to it in years. Her instinct is to keep it rather than retire it, less as a reference and more as a record of a cave's temperament before it was hers to read. "It's not really instructions anymore," she said. "It's more like knowing what the room used to say before I learned to hear it myself."

If you're managing a cave inherited from someone else, or handing yours off to someone who'll inherit it soon, the Advanced Affinage Certificate is open to members with prior affinage coursework, and the Education Committee can walk you through the practical component before you commit to it.
