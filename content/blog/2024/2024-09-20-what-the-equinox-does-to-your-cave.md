This week's equinox is a bigger deal for a cave than most people outside affinage would guess. Ambient humidity outdoors starts dropping across most of the country right around now, and if your ventilation pulls any outside air at all, that shift shows up inside before it shows up in your logbook — usually as a rind that's drying half a point faster than it was in August, on an aging schedule you haven't touched at all.

We hear from members every fall who assume they did something wrong partway through affinage, when really the room moved under them while their routine stayed the same. It is worth treating the seasonal changeover as its own small checkpoint rather than something you only notice three weeks in, staring at a rind you're suddenly worried about.

## Why the room drifts before the wheel tells you

Most cheeses age best somewhere between about eighty and ninety-five percent relative humidity, with the exact number set by style — a hard natural-rind cheese wants it on the drier end of that range, while a bloomy or washed-rind wheel wants it firmly on the humid end. The reason a fall changeover catches people off guard is that a cave loses moisture to the surrounding air continuously, and the rate depends on how humid that surrounding air is. When outdoor humidity drops with the season, a cave that draws on outside air at all will drift toward drier conditions even if nothing about your equipment has changed. Too dry, and a rind hardens and can crack, shedding more weight than the recipe expects; the fix is almost never a different make, it's a different setpoint for the season.

## A short changeover checklist

Pull your humidity log from the same week last year, if you keep one, and compare it to this week. A gap of two or three points between last September and this one is normal and just reflects a slightly different outside air pattern year to year; a gap of five points or more is worth a look at your equipment, not your cheese. Check whether your misting or humidification system is still running on a summer schedule — a lot of members set theirs once in June and don't revisit it until a wheel tells them something's wrong, which is later than you'd like to find out, and further into a batch than you'd like to be correcting for.

If you're aging anything long — a clothbound cheddar, an alpine-style wheel past its fourth month — this is also a sensible week to walk the cave with a flashlight at rind height rather than trusting the digital readout alone. Sensors drift, corners of a cave dry faster than the center, and the wheels closest to a door or a fan are usually the first to show a problem. None of this is a crisis; it is routine maintenance that's easy to skip precisely because nothing looks wrong yet, and the equinox is as good a prompt as any to do it on a schedule rather than by accident.
