Bob Kowalski joined the federation in 2013 as a regional sales director for a dairy supply distributor, and thirteen years is long enough to see an employer through more than one season of change. The most recent one arrived last year, when the distributor he'd spent his career at was acquired. We asked him what actually shifts for a member when the ownership above them does, and what stays exactly the same.

"The truck routes didn't change. The customers didn't change. My job didn't really change," he said. "What changed is who signs off on things, and how long it takes to get an answer to a question that used to take one phone call."

## The parts that moved, and the parts that didn't

Kowalski has spent his career on the distribution side of specialty cheese, which means his working life is built around relationships more than around any single make or recipe. Those relationships, he said, are exactly what didn't transfer automatically in an acquisition. "A creamery that's ordered from me for nine years doesn't care whose logo is on the invoice. They care whether I still pick up the phone and whether the order still shows up on time. That part was on me to protect, not on the new ownership."

What did change, in his account, was the rhythm of decisions that used to be simple. Pricing exceptions, credit terms for a smaller account, the kind of judgment call a regional director used to make on the spot — those now route through a larger organization with its own approval chain. "I'm not saying it's worse," he said. "I'm saying it's slower, and slower matters when a creamery calls you on a Tuesday needing an answer before their Thursday delivery."

Why he stayed a federation member through it is a fair question, too.

Membership renewal is usually the first thing to get deprioritized when a member's employer goes through a transition like this — a new parent company doesn't always understand what a trade association membership is buying them, and dues can look like an easy line to cut during an integration. Kowalski's kept renewing anyway, and he was candid about why: it wasn't loyalty to the federation in the abstract, it was that the professional network built up over thirteen years turned out to be more valuable during the transition, not less.

"When you're the person who has to explain a new ownership structure to twenty long-standing accounts, you want to be talking to other people who've done that," he said. "Committee contacts, other reps I've known for a decade — that's who helped me figure out how to have those conversations, not anybody at the new parent company."

His engagement with events has thinned out some over the past year, which he attributes plainly to bandwidth rather than to any loss of interest. "I used to make it to more of the regional workshops. Right now I'm rebuilding internal relationships at a bigger company, and that's eaten the time I used to spend on federation events. I still read every newsletter. I just haven't been in a room in a while."

## What he'd tell another member going through the same thing

Asked what advice he'd give a member whose employer gets acquired, Kowalski didn't hesitate. "Don't let the membership lapse while you're distracted with the integration — that's exactly the moment you'll need the network most, and it's the easiest moment to let it slip because nobody's asking you to renew, they're asking you to fill out onboarding paperwork for the new parent company instead."

He also had a word for anyone assuming a longtime member who's gone quiet on events has checked out. "I haven't gone anywhere. I'm just busier proving to a new leadership chain that the relationships I built over thirteen years are worth keeping, not replacing."
