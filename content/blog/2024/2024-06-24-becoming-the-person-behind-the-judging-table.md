Every year in the three or four weeks before the Annual Conference, our inbox fills with a version of the same question: how do you actually become a judge? With this year's flagship competition three weeks out in Des Moines, it's a good week to answer it properly, because the honest version of the answer is longer than "sign up at the door."

Competition Judge Accreditation is the credential, and it sits apart from our other six in one important way: it isn't primarily about your own cheese. The other paths — Cheese Foundations, Food Safety & HACCP, Certified Cheesemonger, Certified Cheese Professional, Sensory Evaluation, Advanced Affinage — all build toward doing something better in your own cave or behind your own counter. Judge accreditation builds toward evaluating someone else's work fairly, consistently, and defensibly, which turns out to be a different skill than making good cheese yourself.

## What the credential actually tests

The coursework leans hard on calibration. A candidate taking our Judging & Competition Preparation course spends real time scoring the same wheels alongside a panel of experienced judges and comparing notes, not to see who's "right" but to see where individual palates drift from the group consensus and why. Sweetness tolerance, salt sensitivity, and even how long someone lets a paste sit on the tongue before deciding all shift a score in ways a new judge doesn't expect until they see their own numbers land three points off the panel average.

The written portion covers category standards in more depth than most members expect — what actually separates a flawed rind from a rind that's simply young, what textural defects are disqualifying versus merely note-worthy, and how to write feedback a competitor can use instead of feedback that just restates the score. That last piece gets the most attention in review, because a harsh score with no usable explanation is close to worthless to the person who receives it.

## Why it takes a season, not a weekend

Candidates complete a supervised apprenticeship round before full accreditation — sitting alongside an accredited judge at an actual competition, scoring in parallel, and reviewing the gap between their scores and the panel's. Most members need two apprenticeship rounds before their scoring settles into a consistent range. That's the part that surprises people who assumed a strong palate alone would carry them through. It's also, by design, not a credential you can complete the week before conference: the realistic timeline runs a full cycle, from coursework this summer to an apprenticeship round this fall or winter to full accreditation in time for next year's flagship. Members hoping to judge at this year's competition in three weeks should treat this cycle as the one to start, not the one to finish.

For members who've already completed Advanced Affinage, the judging pathway is a natural next step — a lot of the sensory vocabulary overlaps, and affineurs tend to have more calibrated palates going in than the coursework assumes as a baseline. For everyone else, Sensory Evaluation is the credential we recommend completing first; it isn't a formal prerequisite, but candidates who skip it tend to need a third apprenticeship round instead of two.

None of this is meant to talk anyone out of it. Competitions run on volunteer judges, and the program only works because members keep choosing to do the unglamorous calibration work behind it. If you're weighing whether to start this summer, the Judging & Competition Preparation course opens for enrollment through the member portal, and the Awards & Competition Committee is the place to ask questions about apprenticeship placement for the fall regional circuit.
