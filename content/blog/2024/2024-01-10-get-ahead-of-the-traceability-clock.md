A federal watchdog report published this month found that the food-safety agency behind a major traceability recordkeeping rule still hasn't finished documenting how it plans to enforce it, even though the compliance clock has been running since the rule was finalized a little over a year ago. Members keep asking us whether that rule touches them. The honest answer: probably not directly, since it targets a specific list of higher-risk foods and includes exemptions for many small producers and direct-to-consumer sales. But the exemption boundaries are exactly the kind of thing worth checking now, not assuming.

Here's the shape of it. The rule adds recordkeeping requirements — who you got a lot from, who you shipped it to, and when — for foods on a designated list, layered on top of whatever your state and the general food-safety rules already require. Certain soft cheeses appear on versions of that list depending on how they're made and sold, which means the question "does this apply to me" has a different answer for a raw-milk washed rind sold wholesale than for a pasteurized fresh cheese sold at a Saturday market.

## Why the watchdog report matters to a small creamery

You might reasonably ask why a federal oversight office's opinion about an agency's internal planning matters to somebody with nine cows and a cave. It matters because an agency without a finished enforcement plan tends to lean harder on guidance documents and informal outreach in the meantime, and those documents are where the real, practical answers to "does this apply to me" tend to show up before the formal rule text gets any clearer. Watching for that guidance now, rather than after an inspector asks about it, is the cheap insurance here.

It also means the compliance date itself is worth treating as a moving target rather than a fixed one. Regulatory timelines that depend on an unfinished internal plan have a way of shifting, and members who build their systems assuming the current date won't move are setting themselves up for a scramble either way — whether it holds or slips.

## Why January is the useful month regardless

Nobody wants to rebuild their lot-tracking system in a hurry. If you already keep a make sheet with batch numbers, you're most of the way to compliant recordkeeping regardless of whether the rule technically applies to you — and a clean lot record is the single most useful thing you can hand a state inspector or a wholesale buyer's quality team, rule or no rule. January, with the holiday rush behind you and the cave quieter than it was three weeks ago, is a good month to sit down with last year's records and see where they'd actually hold up under a real request.

Our Listeria Control in Small Creameries course spends a session on lot tracking that's worth attending even if your interest is general food safety rather than this specific rule. The instructors walk through what a usable batch record looks like on paper versus in a spreadsheet, and what a recall actually requires you to produce on short notice — which is usually more detail than most first-time entrants expect.

We're not going to tell you whether your specific operation is exempt from the traceability rule itself. That determination depends on your production volume, your sales channels, and the exact form of your product, and it deserves a real read of the rule text or a conversation with your state dairy inspector, not a blog post's best guess. What we can tell you is that the exemption thresholds are worth rereading if your business has grown since you last checked them. A creamery that added wholesale accounts this year may have crossed a line it wasn't near two years ago.

If you want a walkthrough of what "additional traceability records" actually means in practice, the Food Safety Committee is planning a webinar on it later this quarter. Watch the member portal for a date, and in the meantime, a clean make sheet is never wasted effort — watchdog report or not.
