We heard a version of this story from several members this week, enough that it's worth telling as a composite rather than crediting any one creamery. A farmstead cheesemaker in her second year of running her own cave flew home from the Des Moines conference on Thursday, was back at the vat by Friday morning, and spent the weekend catching up on everything that doesn't pause just because its owner was three states away.

"The cave doesn't know you went to a conference," she told us, only half joking. Her affinage room had held steady while she was gone — she'd trusted an employee to run the twice-daily checks — but the backlog was still real: orders to confirm, a wheel that needed turning attention she'd been tracking from her phone between sessions, and a stack of notes from four days of panels that felt urgent on the plane home and slightly less urgent by Monday.

## What actually made it into practice

Out of everything she wrote down in Iowa, she said two things survived first contact with a real week. The first was a small change to how she logs cave temperature swings, something she picked up informally from another attendee over lunch rather than from a scheduled session — proof, she said, that the hallway conversations are sometimes worth more than the agenda. The second was a decision to finally enroll in Humidity & Airflow Control this fall, a course she'd been meaning to take for two years and kept deferring because summer is never a convenient time to add a class on top of a full production schedule.

Everything else — the pricing panel, the trade updates, most of the vendor conversations — she filed away as useful context rather than anything requiring immediate action. That's not a knock on the conference. It's a fairly typical ratio: most members who go home with three or four pages of notes end up acting on one or two lines of them within the first month, and the rest becomes background knowledge that pays off later, sometimes a year later, when a related decision comes up and the memory surfaces on its own.

Timing mattered too. This is also the stretch when farm-level risk-coverage sales windows for the coming year are open, a detail that came up more than once at the conference and that she said she almost let slip past her while she was catching up on cave logs. Whether or not a given member uses that coverage, it's the kind of deadline that's easy to lose track of during exactly the week a conference backlog is competing for attention.

## If you're still digesting your own notes

If you came home from Des Moines with a notebook full of half-formed plans, you're not behind. The recordings of the general sessions are up on the member portal now if you want to revisit a panel you only half-absorbed live, and there's no rule that says the ideas worth keeping have to get sorted out in the first week back. Give it a month, see what's still nagging at you, and act on that shortlist — it's usually shorter, and more useful, than the one you wrote on the plane home.

For anyone weighing whether a fall course is worth the schedule squeeze, our composite cheesemaker's answer was simple: she said she wished she'd stopped deferring it two years ago instead of one, and that the actual enrollment took less time than the conversation she'd been having with herself about whether to do it.
