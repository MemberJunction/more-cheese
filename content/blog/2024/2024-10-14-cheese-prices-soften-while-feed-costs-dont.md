Late last month, the federal government's monthly outlook actually raised its wholesale dairy price forecast for the rest of the year, on the back of stronger-than-expected block and barrel prices through August and September, even as it trimmed the year's production estimate slightly. A few weeks later, the follow-up read cooled again toward year-end. If you sell wholesale, that back-and-forth is probably more familiar than any single headline number: this fall's forecasts have moved twice in two months, and neither move was dramatic on its own.

What hasn't moved twice, or wobbled, or cooled: feed costs. Premium alfalfa hay kept climbing through the same stretch, and that's the number that actually determines whether a good milk-price month turns into a good margin month. A wholesale forecast can rise a little and still leave you worse off than last quarter if the input side of the ledger moved more than the output side did.

## What the gap means for your worksheet

This is the exact situation the federal margin-coverage program exists to backstop, and it's worth pulling up your enrollment level this week rather than waiting for a January reconciliation. Several members have told us they only check their margin calculation when a payment shows up; by then the quarter that mattered is already closed. If you haven't revisited your feed-cost assumptions since spring, they're stale, and stale feed assumptions make every other number downstream look better than it is.

For retail and specialty producers who don't touch the federal margin program directly, the read-through is simpler and just as real: your supply costs are not necessarily moving in the same direction as your wholesale price, and they're not moving at the same pace either. That's not a reason to panic-reprice a case overnight, but it is a reason to run the arithmetic before your fall list prices lock in for the holiday season.

## A choppy wholesale market isn't a soft market for everyone

None of this means demand is weak. Specialty and artisan channels have kept growing through the current boom, and several members reported their best fall pre-orders in years even while wholesale forecasts bounced around. A choppy wholesale number mostly describes what's happening in the bulk and commodity tiers; a well-differentiated wedge with a story behind it tends to have more room to hold its price than a block does, because the customer buying it isn't shopping strictly on a per-pound comparison.

If your creamery sells across both channels — a wholesale cheddar program alongside a retail specialty line — this is the month to look at them separately rather than as one blended average. The commodity side may need a tighter watch on cost this quarter; the specialty side may have more pricing room than a blended spreadsheet would suggest.

**Next step:** if you haven't reviewed your feed-cost inputs against your enrolled coverage level this quarter, do it this week, before the next monthly update makes the comparison moot.
