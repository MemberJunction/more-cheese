This is the week our phones start ringing about rinds going dry. Cold air simply holds less moisture than warm air, and once that cold air gets pulled indoors and heated to keep a workspace comfortable, its relative humidity drops even further. Your cave doesn't know or care that it's January outside; it just registers a slow, steady pull toward dryness that wasn't there in October.

The fix isn't complicated in principle, but it's easy to get backwards. A pan of water with a partial cover, adjusted by how much of the opening you leave exposed, is still one of the most reliable low-tech humidity boosts for a small room, and it costs nothing to try before you spend money on equipment. A basin of salted water does something similar and is worth a look if your cave runs a few degrees warmer than most affinage spaces call for. For anyone running more than a spare fridge or a converted closet, a humidifier with distilled water and a digital hygrometer you actually check daily is the more dependable setup, because it responds to what your room is doing rather than what you assume it's doing.

## Match the fix to the cheese, not just the room

Your target humidity depends heavily on what's aging. Hard, aged wheels generally want less moisture in the air than bloomy or washed-rind cheeses, which can call for real airflow paired with high relative humidity to avoid a slimy rind. That pairing — airflow plus humidity, not humidity alone — is where a lot of home setups go wrong. Bump the humidity without adding circulation and you risk pooling moisture right at the rind surface instead of even coverage. An aging mat or a simple bamboo board that lets air move underneath the cheese solves more of this than people expect from something so basic.

One member working through her Certified Cheese Professional coursework told us she didn't take humidity swings seriously until her second winter in a new space. "My first fall in that cave, everything was fine, and I figured I understood the room," she said. "Then January came around and I watched three wheels crack before I connected it to the heating vent I'd installed over the summer." She moved her hygrometer twice before finding the actual problem: the vent was drying the air near the door, not the cave as a whole, so her readings from the middle of the room were misleading her.

That's the broader lesson worth carrying into this stretch of winter: one hygrometer reading isn't the same as knowing your room. If you've got the space, check humidity in more than one spot, especially near doors, vents, or anything that generates its own heat. Track what you find for a couple of weeks rather than reacting to a single low reading, since seasonal swings tend to settle into a pattern you can plan around instead of firefight.
