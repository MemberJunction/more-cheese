The federal government's monthly supply-and-demand report landed this week, and if you only skim the headline number, it looks quiet. The all-milk price forecast is holding close to where it's been all fall, and the herd expansion everyone expected hasn't shown up in the numbers the way analysts predicted a year ago. Read past the headline, though, and there's a shift worth passing along to anyone in your shop who prices the case.

## Milk steady, cheese and butter softer

The forecast for cheese and butter prices came down slightly for the rest of the year, while whey firmed a little. For members selling Class III-priced product, that's a mixed signal: your raw milk cost isn't spiking, but the finished-product prices you're competing against at wholesale have a bit less room under them than they did in August. If you've been holding a price point on a bestselling wheel since summer, this is a reasonable week to check whether your supplier's list price has actually moved, rather than assuming it hasn't.

Slower growth in milk production per cow also showed up in the revised numbers. That's not dramatic on its own, but combined with modest herd growth, it suggests buyers may be doing a little more competing for milk supply in some regions over the next two quarters — worth knowing if your creamery is negotiating a new milk contract this fall rather than rolling over last year's terms.

## Imports are the other half of the story

The report also flagged rising cheese and butter imports through the rest of the year. That's the piece that affects retail members more than producers: a fuller import pipeline usually means more competition on the shelf for imported-style cheeses, particularly in categories where a domestic maker and an import sit side by side in the case. If you carry both, it's worth watching your import supplier's pricing over the next few weeks rather than assuming your domestic cost structure tells the whole story.

None of this points to anything urgent. It's the kind of report that rewards a five-minute read with your buyer or your general manager, not a strategy meeting. The federal agriculture department's monthly report is public and free, and we flag the dairy sections for members every month when it drops.

Members who don't touch a milk contract directly sometimes skip these reports entirely, and we understand why — the language is written for economists, not for the person behind a cheese counter. But the translation is usually simple: a softer cheese and butter forecast against a steady milk price means your wholesale cost base is calm while your competitive pricing environment gets slightly more crowded. That's a very different situation from a spike in raw milk costs, and it calls for a different response. Watching your competitors' shelf prices matters more this quarter than watching your own input costs.

For producers, the signal is narrower but sharper. If milk supply tightens even slightly relative to demand in your region — which slower per-cow production growth can do even without a change in cow numbers — buyers may start offering slightly better terms to lock in supply before winter. It's worth a phone call to your usual buyer to ask whether anything's shifted on their end, even if nothing has changed on yours.
