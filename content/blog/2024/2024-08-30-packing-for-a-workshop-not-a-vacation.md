The member we spoke with this week runs quality for a mid-sized creamery and flew out this morning for Workshop: Curd Food Safety, which opens today. She didn't pack it the way she'd pack for a conference. Along with her clothes, her suitcase had a folder of her plant's own curd-handling records — the ones she's not entirely sure hold up.

"I wanted something to compare against," she told us. "It's one thing to hear how curd handling is supposed to go in a training room. It's another to sit with your own log next to someone else's and see where yours gets vague." She'd flagged three pages in her plant's food-safety plan before she left, sections she wrote two years ago that read fine in isolation and less fine now that she's had two new hires touch the vat since.

## Why she chose the trip over sending someone else

She's not new to food safety; she's been the person who owns it at her plant for six years and has sent junior staff to shorter trainings before. This one she wanted for herself. Her reasoning was practical: the workshop's calibration exercises only work if the person sitting in the room has the authority to change the plant's actual procedure afterward. Sending a floor supervisor gets you notes. Going yourself gets you a rewritten section of your HACCP plan before the flight home.

She's also treating the trip as the trip, not a stopover — no extra meetings bolted on, no side errands. That's advice we've heard from other members who've done the same workshop: the material moves fast enough over two days that splitting your attention costs you more than it saves.

There's a cost-conscious reason she made the trip a priority this year rather than deferring it, too. Milk has been tight and cheese prices have been running unusually strong this year, which is good news for her plant's margin but bad news if a food-safety lapse ever forces a recall in a market where every wheel is already spoken for. "We can't afford a mistake this year the way we maybe could when supply was looser and we had product sitting around," she said. "There's less slack to absorb a bad batch."

The first morning, by her account, was mostly recalibrating what "clean" actually means at the curd stage — not the textbook definition, but the version that survives contact with an actual vat on an actual production morning. She said the instructor ran the room through a flight of sample logs from real plants, herself included after she volunteered hers, and had the group flag which entries were vague enough to be useless during an actual investigation. Her own two-year-old entries didn't fare well.

By the second day the group had moved into small teams building out a swab schedule from scratch, defending each choice to the room the way she'd expect to defend it to an inspector. She said it was the first time in years anyone had pushed back hard enough on her reasoning that she'd actually changed her mind about a step in her own process, rather than just nodding along.

## What she's bringing home

Beyond the folder of her own records, she's hoping to leave with a clearer answer to one specific question that's been nagging her plant for a season: whether their current swab schedule around the curd table is actually catching the risk window, or just satisfying the calendar. That's a question a training room full of other quality managers, all comparing notes on their own caves and vats, tends to answer better than a manual does on its own.

If you're weighing whether a workshop like this is worth your own seat rather than a delegate's, her answer was simple: send yourself when the outcome needs to be a decision, not just a summary. Members with questions about upcoming Education Committee workshops can reach the committee directly for the fall schedule.
