Sofia Marchetti joined the federation a little over two weeks ago and has already enrolled in
her first credential track. That's faster than most new members move, and when we asked her
about it, her answer was almost embarrassingly simple: she'd been meaning to join for two
years and finally just did it.

Marchetti is a cheesemonger at Marchetti's Salumeria & Formaggio, where she's spent most of
her time behind the counter building the shop's cheddar and aged-cow-milk selection. She's
working toward the Certified Cheese Professional credential, which is one of the more
involved paths in the federation's certification ladder — it covers production knowledge,
sensory evaluation, and business fundamentals, not just counter skills.

## Why now, and why the deep end

"I'd been telling customers things for years that I was pretty sure were right," she said.
"Pretty sure isn't good enough once you've been doing this a while. I wanted the actual
answer, not my best guess."

That instinct — that expertise built entirely on the counter eventually needs to be checked
against something more rigorous — is common among newer members who arrive already several
years into the trade rather than brand new to it. Marchetti isn't new to cheese. She's new to
having a credential attached to what she already knows, and to finding out where the gaps in
that knowledge actually are.

She picked the full Certified Cheese Professional track rather than starting with something
narrower, and she's candid that it's more than she expected. "The sensory module alone is
humbling. I thought I had a good palate. I have an untrained good palate, which is a different
thing." She's since restructured how she tastes product for the shop's own buying decisions,
adding a written note to every sample rather than trusting memory — a habit she picked up
directly from the coursework.

## What the shop gets out of it

Marchetti says the credential work has already changed conversations at the counter, mostly
in small ways: she's more precise about why a wheel tastes the way it does, and she's started
pointing regular customers toward specialty and artisan styles she wouldn't have confidently
recommended a few months ago. Specialty cheese has been one of the fastest-growing segments in
the category over the past couple of years, and Marchetti thinks a counter staff that can
actually explain what's different about an artisan wheel — not just that it costs more — is
part of what turns a curious customer into a repeat one.

Her advice to anyone who's been working the trade for years without ever formalizing it: don't
treat a credential as something you earn once you're already an expert. "I joined thinking I'd
spend a year just reading before I signed up for anything. I'm glad I didn't wait. You learn
faster once you're actually enrolled and something is due."

If you're a member sitting on years of counter or production experience without a credential
to show for it, the certification pages on the member portal lay out all seven paths and what
each one actually covers. Marchetti's suggestion, for what it's worth, is to pick the one that
scares you a little rather than the one that feels safest.
