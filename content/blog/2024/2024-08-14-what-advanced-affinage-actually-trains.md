Of the seven credentials we administer, the Advanced Affinage Certificate gets the fewest
questions at the help desk and, member for member, the most repeat enrollments from people who
already hold it and want the refresher. That's not a coincidence. Affinage is one of the few
parts of this trade where "I've been doing it for years" and "I can explain why it worked" are
genuinely different skills, and the credential tests the second one.

## What it actually covers

The certificate isn't a tasting exam. It's built around the judgment calls an affineur makes
between the vat and the sale: reading a rind for what it's telling you about the paste underneath,
adjusting humidity and airflow in response rather than on a fixed schedule, and knowing when a
wheel that looks "off" is actually right on time. Trade coverage of specialty production this
year has described the category as still growing and still evolving in what it covers, with more
producers treating maturation as a specialty worth naming on the label rather than a background
step — which is part of why we've seen enrollment in this credential grow steadily since it
launched.

Candidates work through case scenarios covering the major style families — washed rind, bloomy,
blue-veined, and hard aged — because the judgment involved differs meaningfully across them. A
washed-rind problem and a hard-cheese problem rarely look alike, and the exam is built to catch
candidates who've only ever really worked one.

That style-by-style structure isn't a quirk of ours. Trade coverage of affinage this summer
described it plainly: refinement, in the French sense, covers everything that happens to a cheese
between the moment it's fully formed and the moment it's ready — which is a wide, style-dependent
stretch of judgment calls, not a single skill. Our exam is built around that same idea: a
candidate isn't asked whether a wheel is "good," but whether it's doing what that style, at that
age, in that cave, is supposed to be doing.

Two sections trip up more candidates than any others. The first is airflow reasoning — not
"what humidity number should the cave read" but "why is this particular wheel drying faster on
one side than the other, and what does that tell you about your rack placement." The second is
recovery planning: given a wheel that's already developed a problem, candidates have to propose a
realistic path back rather than simply diagnosing what went wrong. Diagnosis is the easy half.

We've also added more scenario variety in the last couple of cohorts, partly in response to
feedback that early versions of the exam leaned too hard on cow's-milk hard cheeses. Sheep- and
goat-milk affinage behaves differently enough that a credential claiming to test "advanced
affinage" broadly needs to actually cover it, and current candidates should expect at least one
scenario built around a non-cow milk.

## Who it's for, and who should wait

This one is not a starting credential. We recommend candidates hold the Cheese Foundations
Certificate and have logged meaningful cave time — your own or under someone else's supervision —
before sitting for it. If you're newer to the trade and reading this thinking it sounds like the
right next step, it might be, but the Sensory Evaluation Certificate or Food Safety & HACCP
Certificate are better places to start building toward it.

For members who already run a cave and have for years, this credential is less about proving you
can do the job and more about having a shared vocabulary — with inspectors, with judges, with
newer hires you're training. A few members have told us the biggest value wasn't the studying, it
was finally having language for a decision they'd been making on instinct for a decade.

It's also worth saying plainly what this credential is not. It isn't a food-safety certification,
and passing it doesn't substitute for the inspection and documentation requirements that come with
running a cave commercially — that's what the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate covers, and the two
are meant to sit alongside each other rather than one standing in for the other. Members who hold
both tell us they use them for different conversations: the affinage credential when they're
talking about craft, the food-safety one when they're talking about compliance.

If you're weighing whether now is the year, the credential page has the current prerequisites and
the next sitting dates, and the Education Committee takes questions from anyone still deciding
whether they're ready.
