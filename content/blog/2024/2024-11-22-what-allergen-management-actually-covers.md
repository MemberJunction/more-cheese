Gift boxes are one of the more overlooked food-safety questions of the holiday season, and it's a question a lot of members only run into once a year, which is exactly why it trips people up. A single-cheese order is straightforward: one product, one label, one set of allergen facts you already have on file. A holiday box with five or six cheeses from three different producers, assembled by hand at your own counter, is a different problem entirely — and it's the specific scenario our Allergen Management course was built to walk members through.

## Why assembly is where the risk actually sits

Most members already have solid allergen documentation for what they make or what they buy wholesale — that part tends to be handled at the production or purchasing stage, with paperwork that's been reviewed and filed. The gap shows up downstream, at the point where a staff member is hand-building a gift box on a counter that also handled a walnut-crusted cheese an hour earlier, or wrapping a nut-free selection next to a tray of items that aren't. Cross-contact at assembly is a different risk category than an ingredient in a recipe, and it's the one holiday-season staff — who are often seasonal hires without a full season of training behind them — are least likely to have internalized.

The course spends real time on the practical version of this: how to sequence assembly so allergen-containing items are handled last or on a separate surface, what an honest "may contain" disclosure looks like on a box built from multiple suppliers' products, and how to train a seasonal hire to ask the right question before they improvise a substitution because you ran out of one cheese mid-shift. That last scenario is more common than members expect, and it's exactly the moment where an allergen mistake tends to happen — someone swaps in a different wheel to fill a box, without checking whether it changes what's on the label.

## Where labeling gets genuinely complicated

A pre-built, packaged gift box is legally a food product with its own labeling requirements, which surprises some members who've been assembling boxes casually for years without treating them that way. If you're building boxes ahead of time for retail sale rather than assembling to order in front of a customer, the ingredient and allergen labeling obligations are closer to what applies to any packaged food product than to a simple point-of-sale transaction. The course walks through that distinction directly, because it's the piece members are most likely to get wrong not from carelessness but from not realizing the rule applied to them at all.

None of this is meant to make holiday gift boxes sound more dangerous than they are — done well, they're one of the most profitable and popular things a lot of members sell this time of year, and specialty and artisan cheese sales have been climbing steadily as a category. The point of the course isn't to slow that down. It's to make sure the systems around it — labeling, staff training, physical separation at assembly — are built before the volume hits, not improvised in the first busy week of December.

Allergen Management runs as a short, self-paced online course, and members who complete it before the holiday rush tend to tell us it paid for itself the first time a seasonal hire asked a question they already knew how to answer.
