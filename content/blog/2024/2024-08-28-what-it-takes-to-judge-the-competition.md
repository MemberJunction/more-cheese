Of the seven credentials we offer, Competition Judge Accreditation gets the fewest applicants and, cohort for cohort, probably the most second-guessing. Members ask about it every fall as entry deadlines for the year's categories start circulating, and the honest answer is that it isn't a credential you add to a resume in passing. It's a credential built for people who are willing to be wrong in front of a room.

The pathway starts with a prerequisite most applicants underestimate: documented tasting experience across a real spread of categories, not just the style you make or sell every day. A cheesemonger who's spent a career on soft-ripened and washed-rind wheels still has to show calibrated exposure to aged cheddars, blues, and alpine styles before the accreditation panel will consider the application. That's deliberate. A judge who only knows one corner of the case tends to score the rest of the room by the wrong yardstick.

## Where the training gets uncomfortable

The core of the coursework is calibration — small panels tasting the same flight and reconciling wildly different first impressions into a defensible score, out loud, with a mentor judge pushing back on soft reasoning. It is not a comfortable few sessions. Members who've been through it describe the moment a mentor asks "why a 7 and not an 8" and expects an answer rooted in the standard, not in personal preference, as the point where the credential either clicks or doesn't. A handful of applicants each cohort withdraw at that stage, and we'd rather that happen in training than at a competition table.

There's a written component too — familiarity with our published judging standards by category, and enough command of off-flavor vocabulary to write a comment sheet a competitor can actually use. A vague "not quite right" doesn't help a cheesemaker fix anything. "Ammoniated on the rind, underripe at the paste" does.

Part of what makes the accreditation coursework demanding is that our judging model pairs two roles at the table, and applicants have to be comfortable in either seat. One judge works from a technical standpoint, starting a wheel at full marks and deducting for identifiable flaws and defects. The other works the aesthetic side, starting from zero and building the score up for standout qualities in a category — texture, balance, the kind of finish that makes a judge want a second bite. Training walks applicants through both roles because a judge who's only ever scored from one direction tends to anchor high or low without noticing it, and the two-role system exists specifically to correct for that blind spot. Cohorts practice switching roles mid-flight so the habit doesn't calcify.

The accreditation also asks applicants to sit with feedback that goes the other direction — a mentor judge scoring the applicant's own comment sheets and pointing out where a note was vague enough that a cheesemaker couldn't act on it. That part surprises people who came in expecting the credential to be entirely about tasting. Writing a usable comment turns out to be its own skill, separate from having the right palate.

## If you're weighing it for this year

The accreditation exists because our Awards & Competition Committee needs a reliable bench of judges every entry season, not because it's meant to be a universal add-on credential. If you taste widely, you're comfortable defending a score under mild interrogation, and you can write feedback a stranger could use, it's worth the application. If none of that describes your strengths yet, our Sensory Evaluation Certificate is the better starting point — it builds the same calibrated-tasting muscle without the judging-panel pressure, and several current judges took it first. Either pathway starts with a conversation with the Awards & Competition Committee about which cohort has room this cycle.
