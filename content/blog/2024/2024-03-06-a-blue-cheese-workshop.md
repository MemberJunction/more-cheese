Tuesday's Workshop: Dairy Blue Styles filled its seats faster than most single-day sessions
do this time of year, and that tells you something about where members' attention has been.
Blue cheese isn't a small category anymore. Industry trade coverage this year has pointed to
sustained growth in artisan and specialty cheese broadly, with blue and veined styles picking
up more than their share of that shelf space, particularly at retail counters willing to carry
something with real character.

Growth like that creates its own problem, and it's the one the workshop actually spent most of
its time on: consistency. A blue that surprises the buyer in a good way once is a story. A
blue that surprises the buyer in a different way every batch is a return, and every returned
wheel is a conversation you don't want to have with an account you worked hard to land.

## Vein, not volume

The instructor's framing — repeated by half the room afterward — was that most young
producers chase a *stronger* blue before they've locked in a *consistent* one. Piercing
timing, needle spacing, and cave humidity all move the vein pattern independently of one
another, and changing more than one variable at a time makes it nearly impossible to tell
which adjustment actually caused the batch that came out right.

The exercise that got the most notes taken was a side-by-side of three wheels from the same
make, pierced at three different intervals and aged identically otherwise. The differences
were obvious once you'd tasted all three side by side — much less obvious if you'd only ever
tasted one of them alone, which is how most of us actually taste our own cheese day to day.

The second half of the session covered rind management on blue-veined wheels specifically,
which behaves differently from a washed rind or a natural rind and gets less coverage in
general affinage material. Several attendees told us afterward that this was the part they'd
actually come for.

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Regional festivals and marketplace events this spring
have reported strong turnout for artisan producers generally, and market research on the
category keeps flagging sustainability and small-batch craft as the two things buyers are
actively asking about at the counter. Blue cheese sits in an odd spot inside that trend — it's
both a heritage style with centuries of technique behind it and, done well, exactly the kind
of distinctive, small-batch product that's driving new interest in the category. Producers who
can talk knowledgeably about their process, not just their flavor profile, are the ones best
positioned to take advantage of that interest.

That's part of why the Events Committee scheduled this particular workshop for early spring
rather than waiting for the usual late-summer training window. Members told us during last
year's planning survey that they wanted technical sessions timed to when they're actually
building out a spring or summer product line, not after the decisions are already made.
