If your feed bill looked a little friendlier this month, you weren't imagining it. Corn, alfalfa hay, and soybean meal have all come down from where they sat a year ago, and the federal milk-to-feed margin reports we track for members have moved into more comfortable territory than we've seen in over a year. That's real breathing room. The question we're getting on the member line this week is what to actually do with it.

Our answer, most days: don't just let it sit as relief. Use it as a pricing window.

## Cost relief isn't the same as price relief

A lower feed bill changes your cost of production, but it doesn't change what a wheel is worth on a shelf, and it doesn't obligate you to pass every dollar of savings straight through to a buyer. Members who came through the last two years of feed-cost swings tell us the mistake was treating the margin like found money instead of like information. If your cost base just eased, that's the moment to revisit a make-cost worksheet you haven't touched since the numbers were worse — not to raise prices reflexively, but to see where you were pricing defensively and whether that's still necessary.

For creameries carrying wholesale accounts, this is also a fair moment to open a pricing conversation with a distributor rather than wait for them to open it with you. A buyer who watched your invoice climb through 2022 and 2023 will not be surprised that you're revisiting terms now that costs have moved the other way — and showing up with the number first, rather than reacting to a renegotiation request, tends to land better.

## Where the course comes in

This is exactly the gap our *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* course was built to close. It isn't a spreadsheet class. It walks through building a make-cost model that separates fixed costs (your cave, your labor, your equipment) from the ones that move with the market (milk, cultures, packaging), so that when feed costs shift — up or down — you know which lines in your price actually need to change and which ones don't. Members who've taken it tell us the biggest shift isn't the math, it's the habit of rebuilding the model quarterly instead of setting a price once and defending it for a year.

If you sell direct, the same logic applies at the counter. A shopper who's used to paying a certain amount for your wedge isn't tracking the feed-cost cycle, and you shouldn't expect them to. But you can use a softer cost quarter to protect your margin on a slower-moving line instead of chasing volume with a discount you didn't need to offer.

A caution on timing, though: feed costs move in cycles, and nobody on our Education Committee is going to tell you this window is permanent. Treat this quarter as a chance to rebuild your margin cushion, not as the new normal. If you locked in favorable milk contracts or feed contracts recently, factor that into your model too — the point of the exercise is to know your actual number, not to guess at it from a headline.
