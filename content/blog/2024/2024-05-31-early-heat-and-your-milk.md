Late May is early for heat-stress conversations in a lot of regions, but the dairy side of our membership has already started having them this year. Research on heat-stressed cattle has found measurable shifts in milk composition well before a herd looks visibly uncomfortable — protein and fat ratios drift, somatic cell counts can climb, and the milk's antioxidant capacity tends to fall as animals spend more energy managing body temperature instead of producing. None of that shows up on a farm tour. It shows up on your make sheet, usually as a coagulation that takes longer than it should or a curd that behaves differently than it did in March.

## The lag most cheesemakers don't expect

If you buy milk rather than milk your own herd, this is worth flagging to whoever supplies you, because the effect often has a delay. A hot stretch this week can still be showing up in components two or three weeks later, long after the weather itself has broken. Several of our farmstead members have told us their instinct is to blame the make process first — a starter culture issue, a pH miscalculation — when the actual variable that shifted was the milk arriving at the vat.

The practical response isn't dramatic. It's mostly about tightening your feedback loop: if you're logging make-day variables at all, add a note about recent weather alongside your usual pH and moisture readings, and compare batches across a stretch of hot days versus a cooler one. Patterns that look like inconsistency in isolation often resolve into a clear seasonal signal once you're looking at three or four weeks side by side.

A dairy-science review published this month puts numbers on the yield side of that lag: energy-corrected milk can fall roughly 3% for every degree a herd's temperature-humidity index climbs past the mid-70s, with protein concentration slipping right alongside it. For a mid-size herd, that kind of drop translates into real money for the dairy — but the number that should matter to a cheesemaker is the protein figure, not the volume one. A vat that's short on protein behaves differently no matter how much milk arrives, and it's the kind of shift that's easy to miss if you're only tracking whether the tanker showed up full.

## Where this fits in your training

Our milk quality and herd health coursework covers the mechanics here in more depth than we can in a blog post — how heat load translates into compositional change, what a buyer or cheesemaker can reasonably ask a supplier to track, and how to build early-summer variation into a make-sheet review rather than treating every off batch as its own mystery. It's aimed at both sides of the milk relationship, whether you're running the herd or just depending on someone who does.

It's also worth raising directly with your supplier if you haven't already. A short conversation now about how they're managing heat abatement — shade structures, fan placement, water access near the parlor — can save both of you a confusing quarter later, when a run of inconsistent batches gets blamed on the wrong variable. Suppliers who've already made this investment are usually glad to talk about it; it's a selling point for them too.

If your cave has already started feeling the difference this spring, you're not imagining it, and you're not alone.
