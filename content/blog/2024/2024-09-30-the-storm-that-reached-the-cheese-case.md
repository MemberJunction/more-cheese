Most weeks, this space covers the milk price and moves on. This week we can't do that without talking about the storm that tore through the southern Appalachians three days ago, because the two are no longer separate stories.

The historic flooding that followed the storm hit dairy country hard — barns down, milking parlors flooded, fencing gone, and hay and feed stores ruined right as the region headed into fall. In the hardest-hit counties, farms are reporting flattened fencing, contaminated wells, and cattle that had to be walked out of flooded pastures by hand before a single cow could be milked. Several members in our western North Carolina and eastern Tennessee footprint have called the office in the last few days, and the calls have a similar shape: not "can I still make cheese," but "what do I do with a cave I can't get power back to, and a herd that needs feed I can't source locally right now." One farmstead producer described losing a week of aging inventory to a cave that sat above 70 degrees for four days straight. That is not a footnote. That is a season's work.

It is early yet — most of the affected region is still without reliable power, and members are understandably more focused on the next 48 hours than on a blog post. But the pattern showing up in the first wave of calls is consistent enough that we think it is worth naming now rather than waiting for a tidier summary in a month. Small operations with a single cave and a single generator are the ones most exposed, because they don't have a second climate-controlled room to fall back on while the first one is repaired.

## What we're watching on the supply side

Separately from the storm, this month's federal crop report trimmed the national milk production estimate, citing softer per-cow growth rather than fewer cows. Cheese and butter prices have also eased since midsummer, which is part of why Class III forecasts came down even as whey firmed up. None of that is really about hurricanes — it is the ordinary rhythm of a dairy year — but it means the storm-affected region is losing production at a moment when the broader market has less room to absorb it quietly.

For members outside the affected states, the practical question is what this does to sourcing. If your supply chain runs through the Southeast — and for a meaningful share of our membership, it does, whether that is a distributor's regional hub or a specific farmstead account — expect some substitutions and some delays over the next several weeks. Ask your supplier now rather than in November, when everyone else asks too. If you buy from a small producer in the affected footprint, a short, direct note this week — not a form email — goes a long way; several members have told us that hearing from a buyer before the buyer needs anything mattered more than any discount would have.

## What the federation can actually do

We are not a disaster relief agency, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. What we can do: point members toward federal and state agricultural disaster assistance programs, connect an affected creamery with a distributor willing to hold shelf space during a rebuild, and put people who've been through equipment loss before in touch with people going through it now. If your operation was affected and you haven't called us, call us. If you're in a position to hold a slot in your case for a storm-affected producer this winter, tell your buyer that too — it matters more than a sympathetic headline.

We're also hearing, unsurprisingly, that fencing and feed are the more urgent problem for some members than the cave itself; a herd that can't be fed properly this week won't be producing quality milk in November regardless of how well the aging room comes back online. If your operation has hay or feed capacity to spare and you're willing to sell or lend it into the affected region, tell us — we'd rather make that connection directly than watch someone spend a week searching for it.

We'll have more specifics as members report their own numbers back to us. In the meantime, if you're in the affected footprint, our Membership & Outreach Committee is the first stop for anyone who needs a human being rather than a form.
