Two things have been true about specialty cheese for a while now, and this month both got a little more visible. First, money keeps finding its way toward small, well-run creameries and the shops that carry them — new packaging deals, new distribution partnerships, and a general sense among buyers that artisan cheese is worth betting on. Second, the federal price forecasts that shape everyone's spring budgeting nudged upward again this month, with the projected all-milk price for the year edging closer to $21 per hundredweight even as production forecasts for 2024 and 2025 got trimmed. Neither of those is a headline your case needs by itself. Together, they're worth a conversation.

We've heard from more than one member this winter that the phone is ringing differently than it did two years ago — distributors calling to ask about capacity rather than the other way around, retailers wanting to lock in allocations before summer rather than scrambling in June. "We spent years chasing shelf space," one longtime creamery member told us recently. "Now we're the ones deciding which accounts we can actually serve." That's a good problem, but it's still a problem: capacity decisions made under pressure tend to get made badly.

## What the price forecasts actually change

A higher all-milk forecast sounds like good news, and mostly it is. But if you're a small producer buying milk rather than milking your own herd, or a cheesemaker whose margin depends on the spread between what you pay for milk and what your wheels sell for, an upward price forecast is a reason to redo your make-cost math now, not in April. The gap between "milk costs more" and "cheese should cost more too" is where a lot of undercharged spring contracts get signed. If you haven't already, pull last year's cost sheet and check whether your current wholesale pricing still reflects this year's inputs.

The trimmed production forecasts matter here too. Less supply growth than expected usually means firmer prices further out, which is one more reason not to lock a full year of wholesale pricing on today's numbers alone. Build in a review point at midyear if your contracts allow it.

## What the investor interest means for you

You don't need outside capital to benefit from a market where outside capital is paying attention. Buyers who are courting well-capitalized producers are, by extension, courting the category — more case space, more marketing dollars aimed at "specialty" and "artisan" as selling points, more retail buyers whose job now explicitly includes finding new cheese to feature. That's an opening for members who aren't raising capital at all, provided your samples, your sell sheet, and your story are ready when a buyer calls.

It's also a moment to be a little skeptical of your own optimism. A rising tide lifts a lot of boats, including some that were built for calmer water. If a new account wants volume commitments larger than your cave or your staffing can comfortably support, it's fair to say so and propose a ramp instead. Losing a small piece of a deal now beats losing the whole relationship in August when you can't deliver.
