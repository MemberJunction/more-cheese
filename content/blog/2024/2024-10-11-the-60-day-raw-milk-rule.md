Federal rules have allowed raw-milk cheese to move across state lines since the late 1980s, on one condition: the wheel has to be aged a minimum of 60 days and labeled as unpasteurized before it ships. It's a narrow exception inside a much broader ban on interstate raw milk, and it's the reason a farmstead maker in one state can sell an aged raw-milk cheddar to a shop three states away at all. This week, one of our members spent an afternoon explaining exactly how that 60-day line works — and doesn't — to a small legislative panel weighing state-level changes that could affect it.

## Why the aging clock matters more than the recipe

Our member makes a raw-milk tomme that she ages closer to 90 days, well past the federal floor, because her own testing showed her cave's flora needed the extra time to develop a safe, stable rind. She told the panel that the 60-day number isn't a magic line where risk disappears — it's a floor, not a target, and makers who treat it as a target are taking on more risk than the rule assumes they will. That distinction is exactly the kind of nuance that gets lost when aging rules get discussed in the abstract instead of in front of people who actually run the coolers.

Her point to the panel: state-level proposals that would either loosen or tighten the interstate standard need to grapple with the fact that "60 days" was never meant to be read as "safe at 60 days." It was meant to give makers room to build a program around, with margin. Members who ship interstate should be building their own aging protocols well past the floor, documenting them, and being ready to explain why — because that documentation is what protects a shipment if a regulator ever asks questions after the fact.

The panel's own briefing materials, she told us afterward, treated the federal floor almost like a light switch — compliant on day 60, not compliant on day 59 — with no room for the biological reality that different milk, different cultures, and different cave conditions all reach a stable, safe rind on different timelines. Her written comments asked the panel to consider requiring makers to document their own validated aging standard alongside the federal minimum, rather than treating the federal number as sufficient evidence on its own.

## What we're asking members to do

If your creamery ships raw-milk cheese across state lines, or you're weighing whether to start, a few things are worth doing before your next batch:

1. **Write down your own aging standard**, separate from the federal floor, and keep dated records of when a batch crossed it. If you're already past 60 days as a house policy, that's worth stating explicitly rather than assuming it's obvious from your labels.
2. **Watch your own state's legislative calendar.** Interstate shipment rules interact with state-level raw milk law in ways that vary a lot by state, and a change in your home state can affect what you're allowed to ship out, not just what you're allowed to sell locally.
3. **Keep your batch records organized before you're asked for them**, not after. A regulator or a buyer's auditor asking "how do you know this batch is safe to ship" wants a dated log, not a verbal assurance.

Our Advocacy Committee tracks state-level raw-milk proposals and can flag ones relevant to your state before a hearing date, the way we did for the member above. If you'd be willing to offer testimony the way she did — even in writing, even briefly — reach out to the committee. Panels remember specifics from people who actually run a cave far more than they remember position papers, and we'd rather have five members' real numbers than one polished statement with none.
