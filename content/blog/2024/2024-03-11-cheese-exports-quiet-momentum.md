For most of our members, "export" has meant one pallet a year, maybe two, going to a distributor
who happens to have an overseas account. That's starting to change, and not because any single
producer decided to become an exporter. It's changing because the buyers did.

Federal trade circulars have tracked a steady climb in the share of domestic cheese production
that leaves the country — a low single-digit share as recently as a few years ago, trending
toward something closer to one wheel in twelve today. Mexico, Central America, and South America
have been the biggest movers, with importers there developing an appetite for styles that used
to be a strictly domestic conversation: aged cheddars, washed-rind wheels, the kind of thing a
regional distributor would once have called "too particular to ship."

## What this means if you've never filled out an export form

None of this requires a producer to suddenly become a logistics company. It does mean the
paperwork stops being optional trivia. A shipment that crosses a border needs the right country-
of-origin documentation, the right certificate of analysis, and — depending on the destination —
a health certificate that your state's agriculture office has to sign before the truck leaves the
lot. Get any one of those wrong and the load sits at a port while your buyer's chef substitutes
something else for the tasting menu.

This is exactly the gap our Export Documentation Essentials course was built to close. It's not
a trade-law seminar; it's a walkthrough of the actual forms, in the order a real shipment hits
them, taught by people who have had a truck held at a border for a missing signature and would
rather you didn't repeat it. If your buyer has so much as mentioned "maybe a container next
quarter," that's the signal to get someone on your team through it before the conversation gets
serious.

## The part that isn't really new

The underlying reason buyers are reaching further for cheese isn't exotic: demand for
distinctive, small-batch product has outpaced what any single region can supply, and a
buyer with a menu to fill will go find it. That's the same story that's been playing out
domestically for years, just with a longer supply line attached. The caves, the affinage, the
culture blends — all of that travels the same way it always has. What's different is who's
asking for the wheel at the other end of the truck.

We'd also note, because members ask: none of this changes anything about domestic pricing or
your existing wholesale relationships. Export demand is additive for most of the producers we
talk to, not a replacement for the accounts you already have. Treat it as a new column on the
spreadsheet, not a new spreadsheet.

If your business is even loosely considering a first shipment abroad this year, two things are
worth doing before you say yes to a buyer: register for the next Export Documentation Essentials
cohort, and call a member of the Membership & Outreach Committee, who can point you to another
member who's already made the first shipment and lived to tell you what they'd do differently.
