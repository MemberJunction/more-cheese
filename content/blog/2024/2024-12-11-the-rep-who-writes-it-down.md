Every distribution rep has a route. Not every rep has a notebook for it. A member who covers a five-state territory for a mid-sized specialty distributor told us this fall that she's kept the same running log for six years — one page per account, updated after every visit, no exceptions. Humidity reading if the producer mentioned one. What was moving off the shelf. Whether the last batch of a particular wheel tasted the way it should. She calls it boring. Her accounts call it the reason they pick up the phone when she calls back.

It's a small thing that adds up. A route rep sees a lot of caves in a year, and it's easy for one to blur into the next — especially heading into a December when every account's order is bigger and more urgent than the last one. Export showcases and international trade events this fall have made the case that American specialty cheese is a harder sell abroad on reputation alone; the same is true closer to home. Trust with a buyer or a wholesale account gets built account by account, visit by visit, not by a category-wide reputation. Her notebook means that when a producer says "it's not aging the way it did last year," she can actually check that against what she wrote down instead of relying on memory. It's also, though she'd never put it this way, a form of quality control that has nothing to do with a lab: a second set of eyes tracking a cave over time, from someone with reason to notice if something's off before it becomes a claim. National milk production has stayed steadier than a lot of forecasters expected heading into winter, which is good news for supply — but steady supply doesn't tell a producer anything about how their own cave is holding up week to week. That's the gap her notebook quietly fills.

## Why it matters more in a strong year

Sponsorship and event attendance have both been healthy this cycle, and a lot of members tell us wholesale relationships feel steadier than they have in a few years. That's good news, but a steady market also means less friction to catch problems early — nobody's renegotiating terms out of desperation, so a quiet drift in quality or delivery timing can go unmentioned for longer than it should. A rep who's actually tracking the account, visit over visit, is one of the better early-warning systems a producer has, and it's not something a distributor can write into a contract. It comes from someone deciding the job is worth doing carefully.

She told us the habit started almost by accident — she couldn't remember which of two accounts had asked about a delivery schedule change, wrote it down so she wouldn't get it wrong again, and just kept going. Six years later it's thick enough that she's started a second volume. She's not looking to turn it into a system or sell anyone on the method. It's just how she does the job.

If you're on either side of a distribution relationship — producer or rep — this is a low-cost habit worth borrowing. A running note per account, even a short one, turns "I think this changed" into "here's what changed and when," which is a much easier conversation to have with a supplier or a buyer. It also makes the relationship less dependent on any one person's memory, which matters if a route ever changes hands.

We don't run a formal program around this kind of member story, but if you've got a small habit that's quietly made your side of the business work better, we'd like to hear about it. Reach out through the Membership & Outreach Committee — spotlights like this one usually start with someone mentioning something in passing that turns out to be worth writing down.
