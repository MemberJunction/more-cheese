A version of this question comes into our advocacy inbox every few months, and it came in twice this week: can a member ship a raw-milk cheese to a customer in another state? The short federal answer hasn't moved in decades. The longer answer — the one members actually need — depends on the cheese, the state, and increasingly on enforcement discretion that isn't written down anywhere a small producer can look it up.

## What's actually on the books

Federal rule prohibits raw, unpasteurized milk from moving in interstate commerce, full stop, and has since a rule change decades ago made pasteurization mandatory for anything shipped across a state line. That rule carries one narrow, long-standing exception: a raw-milk cheese aged a minimum of sixty days and clearly labeled as unpasteurized is allowed to cross state lines. In practice, a raw-milk product that pushes past that specific standard — a washed-rind style aged shorter than sixty days, a small-batch experiment that doesn't map cleanly onto the exception — sits in a much grayer zone the moment it leaves the state where it was made.

States add another layer on top of the federal floor. A meaningful share of them permit some form of raw-milk sale within their own borders, under wildly different permit, testing, and labeling requirements; others ban it outright even for products a neighboring state would license without objection, and the rules shift often enough that a table printed last year can already be out of date. A member operating in a permissive state can find themselves fully compliant at home and still unable to say with confidence whether a wholesale account one state over exposes them to a real problem.

## Why "nobody enforces it" isn't good advice, and what to do instead

Several members have told us, correctly, that interstate raw-milk shipment happens constantly with little visible enforcement. We'd rather not build advocacy guidance on the assumption that quiet tolerance today means quiet tolerance indefinitely. Enforcement discretion is not the same thing as a rule change, and a member who has structured wholesale relationships around an assumption of low enforcement risk is exposed the moment that assumption stops holding — whether because of a recall investigation tied to another producer entirely, a competitor complaint, or simply a change in agency priorities. Public health data on raw-milk illness outbreaks has also been part of the conversation regulators are having, and it's not an argument our advocacy work can just wave away.

That's the gap our work is aimed at closing this year under the "Interstate cheese shipment" banner: not looser food safety standards, but a clearer, written path for raw-milk cheeses that already meet the sixty-day aging standard to move across state lines without each member having to guess at enforcement posture. We're not asking anyone to rewrite the pasteurization requirement — we're asking for a documented, predictable process for the products that already qualify for the existing exception, so members aren't relying on quiet non-enforcement to run a compliant business.

In the meantime, if you sell or plan to sell a raw-milk product to an out-of-state account, get your own state's permit and testing paperwork airtight first, and confirm your aging and labeling meet the federal sixty-day standard before you ship anything across a line. Our Food Safety Committee can walk you through what that record needs to include, and our advocacy volunteers want to hear directly from members who've hit a real wall trying to ship a raw-milk cheese out of state — specific stories are what make testimony persuasive, and general frustration alone rarely moves a committee.
