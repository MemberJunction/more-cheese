If you make or sell raw-milk cheese, you've probably heard some version of "they're looking at the aging rule again" at every gathering this spring. It's true, in the sense that it's true most springs — the federal food-safety regulator periodically revisits the science behind the requirement that raw-milk cheese age a minimum number of days before sale, and this is one of those periods. It is not true in the sense of an imminent change. Here's what we actually know, and why it's still worth your attention.

## What's actually under review

The aging requirement exists because time, salt, and the drop in pH during ripening reduce pathogen risk in a cheese that was never pasteurized. The rule was written decades ago, and the argument for revisiting it runs in both directions: some newer research suggests certain raw-milk styles carry more residual risk than the original rule assumed, and other research suggests well-controlled small producers with strong swab-and-test programs can demonstrate safety well before the current minimum. Both arguments are being aired in the current review, and neither has landed as a proposed rule yet — this is still the fact-gathering stage.

Our Food Safety Committee has been tracking the docket since it reopened and will flag the moment a formal comment period opens. Nothing is currently open for public comment; if you see a petition asking for signatures on a specific proposed text, check with the committee before signing — this cycle, more than one email has circulated describing a rule that doesn't exist yet.

## Why we're asking members to get their records in order now, not later

Whatever comes out of this review, any producer relying on raw-milk exemptions or extended-aging arguments will be in a stronger position with clean, dated records: swab maps, cave temperature and humidity logs, batch-level aging timelines. That's true regardless of which direction the rule eventually moves, and it's the same documentation habit our Food Safety Committee has been recommending for years for entirely separate reasons — insurance, wholesale contracts, and your own peace of mind during a recall scare.

A few members who testified in past comment periods on adjacent rules have told us the biggest regret wasn't what they said, it was not having the paper trail to back it up when a regulator asked a follow-up question. A well-kept logbook does more for you in that moment than a strong opinion does. It's also worth remembering that a review like this one moves on its own clock — it can sit in the fact-gathering stage for a year or more before anything resembling a proposed rule text appears, and members who spend that whole stretch anxious rather than organized end up no better prepared when the window finally opens.

If you're newer to raw-milk production and haven't been through a comment period before, ask a longer-tenured member what it actually looked like the last time a similar rule was on the table. The shape is usually the same: an announcement, a defined window (often 60 or 90 days), a docket number, and a strong preference among regulators for comments that cite specific data over comments that state a position. Members who plan to submit testimony do better starting a draft early and refining it than writing the whole thing the week the window closes.

Get on the Food Safety Committee's mailing list if you aren't already — that's where the actual comment-period announcement will land first, well ahead of any general federation update. In the meantime, the practical thing every raw-milk producer can do is tighten up their own records: dated batch logs, calibrated thermometers, a swab-testing cadence you can actually produce evidence of. When the comment window opens, the members who show up with numbers do better than the members who show up with opinions.
