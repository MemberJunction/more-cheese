A month ago, we previewed Webinar: Digital Storefronts 2024 and promised it would cover packaging, listings, and fulfillment costs rather than website design. Now that a full holiday order cycle has started for several members, we asked a handful of attendees what actually changed in their operation — and the answers were more specific, and more useful, than we expected.

Almost nobody mentioned their website. The change members kept bringing up was packaging: two members separately told us they'd switched insulated-box suppliers after the session's discussion of what actually survives a three-day truck route, and both said it was the first shipment in months that didn't arrive with a customer complaint about a softened rind. That's a small operational fix, but it's the kind of fix that only gets made once someone hears a specific number for how long a given box configuration holds temperature, rather than a general instruction to "pack it cold."

## Demand is still the easy part

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Cheese continues to show up in retail as a category that's gaining ground rather than holding steady, with shoppers reaching for it as an everyday item and retailers expanding rather than trimming their cheese sections. That's a fair proxy for what members selling direct-to-consumer are also seeing: interest in ordering online isn't the bottleneck. What breaks is almost always somewhere between the order and the doorstep — a box that isn't rated for the actual transit time, a listing that oversells consistency a handmade wheel can't promise batch to batch, or a fulfillment cost nobody built into the sticker price.

A few members told us the listing-language piece of the session mattered more than they expected going in. Being explicit that a wheel will vary slightly from batch to batch — rather than implying a factory-consistent product — turned out to reduce complaint volume more than any packaging change did, because customers who are told to expect variation rarely complain about it, and customers who aren't told sometimes do.

## What we're carrying into the next session

We're folding these reports back into how we run the next storefront-adjacent session, including the farm shop retail webinar coming up next month. If a topic works better as a specific number — how many hours a given insulated box holds temperature, what a realistic fulfillment margin looks like on a six-ounce wedge — than as general advice, we'd rather build the session around the number.

If you attended Digital Storefronts 2024 and made a change worth sharing, tell us — we're collecting a few more of these before the next Events Committee planning meeting, and the plainest, most specific feedback is the kind that ends up shaping the next agenda.

That specificity matters more for direct-to-consumer cheese than it does for most shipped goods, because the product itself is unusually unforgiving. A reefer truck and a walk-in cooler both hold temperature; a small parcel carrier's van does not, reliably, and a wheel that spends an afternoon on a loading dock in warm weather can arrive fine on the outside and wrong on the inside. Members shipping smaller volumes than a wholesale account don't have the leverage to demand a dedicated cold-chain carrier, which is exactly why the packaging conversation from the webinar landed as hard as it did — the box is doing work that the carrier isn't guaranteed to do for you.
