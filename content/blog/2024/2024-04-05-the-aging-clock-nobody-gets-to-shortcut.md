Our raw-milk members know one number cold: hard cheese made from unpasteurized milk can be sold across state lines if it's aged at least sixty days. It's one of the oldest rules on the books for this segment of the trade, it hasn't moved in decades, and every time a broader federal traceability framework gets revised for other reasons, we get a wave of calls asking whether this time is the time it finally changes. It isn't, as far as anything currently in front of regulators shows us, but the calls themselves tell us something worth acting on: members don't have a reliable way to find out quickly when an adjacent rule shifts in a way that touches their aging room.

## Why the margin matters more than the rule

Sixty days is a floor, not a target, and most of our raw-milk members already build in a buffer, aging past the minimum so a slow week in the cave or a hiccup in recordkeeping doesn't put a wheel on the wrong side of the line. That buffer isn't free. Extra weeks in the cave mean extra shelf space tied up, extra carrying cost, and for a small room, a real ceiling on how much product can be in the pipeline at once. Members with tighter caves have told the Food Safety Committee that the buffer they build in is often larger than it needs to be, simply because they'd rather over-age than risk a compliance question they didn't see coming.

That's the gap we've been focused on closing this year, not the sixty-day number itself, which nothing currently on the table proposes to touch, but the lag between when a federal agency signals it's reviewing dairy traceability recordkeeping and when that signal reaches a member who's deciding how much buffer to build into this month's aging schedule. A clearer, faster notice path doesn't change what the rule requires. It changes how precisely a small creamery can plan around it instead of padding every hold period out of caution.

Our advocacy work here isn't a general comment in support of raw-milk cheese, which regulators hear plenty of already. It's a narrower, more useful ask: that any future rulemaking touching recordkeeping for aged dairy products include a defined notice period before it takes effect, long enough for a member running a sixty-plus day aging cycle to adjust a batch already in the cave rather than discovering a new requirement after the fact. That kind of ask tends to land better than a broad objection, because it doesn't argue with the substance of a rule, it argues for the runway to comply with it.

## How to add your voice

If your operation ages raw-milk cheese past the sixty-day minimum and keeps records of exactly how you track that hold period, batch by batch, the Food Safety Committee wants to hear from you. A specific account of what your recordkeeping actually looks like, and how much lead time you'd realistically need to adjust it, carries more weight in what we file than a general statement of support ever does.

Members who want the fuller picture of what's required around aging and documentation should start with our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate, which covers hold-period recordkeeping in the kind of detail this conversation depends on. Reach out to the committee directly if you'd like to add your operation's numbers to the file we're building.
