The Advanced Affinage Certificate has been one of our steadier credentials for years — the
kind of course a member takes a season or two into running their own cave, once the daily
guesswork starts to wear thin. This summer, enrollment moved faster than usual, and the
timing isn't a coincidence. Much of the country is on track for one of its hottest Julys on
record, with several regions already logging their warmest stretches in decades. A cave that
held steady through an ordinary summer is getting tested this year, and members are noticing.

The credential covers what its name suggests — humidity control, temperature drift, mold
ecology, and the judgment calls that separate a rind you're proud of from one you're managing
damage on. What it doesn't cover, and what we want to be clear about, is anything to do with
whether a given batch is safe to sell. That's a food-safety and regulatory question, not a
craft one, and members with concerns about a specific lot should go through their own quality
plan and, where required, their state inspector — not a certificate course.

## What members are actually asking about

Committee volunteers who staff our education inbox say the questions this month cluster
around two things: how much a humidity swing of a few points actually matters over a full
aging cycle, and whether a cooling upgrade is worth the cost for a cave that's only
struggled during a handful of weeks a year. Neither has a one-size answer — it depends on
your rind style, your throughput, and how much margin you have for a bad batch — but the
course walks through a framework for thinking about both, using real cave logs from
members who've agreed to share their data anonymously.

One instructor, a longtime affineur who's taught the course's cave-monitoring module for
several cohorts now, told us she's reworked a chunk of the syllabus this year to spend more
time on short, sharp heat events rather than steady seasonal drift. "The caves that struggle
aren't usually the ones that are warm all summer," she said. "They're the ones that swing
hard for four days and then swing back before anyone's adjusted anything." That's a
different kind of monitoring discipline than most new cheesemakers are taught, and it's
become the part of the course members ask the most follow-up questions about. It also tracks
with what industry coverage has been saying about this year's specialty cheese season more
broadly — makers describing a summer of sharper swings and less predictable conditions than
usual, rather than one long steady heat.

## If you're deciding whether to enroll

The Advanced Affinage Certificate runs as a cohort course a few times a year, and this
summer's session has more members mid-buildout — new caves, new coolers, new setups —
than usual, several of whom told us the heat pushed them to finally sign up rather than
keep troubleshooting alone. If your cave has had a rough few weeks and you've been meaning
to formalize what you already do by instinct, this is a reasonable season to do it. Details
and the next cohort's start date are on the certifications page of the member portal, and
our Education Committee is happy to talk through whether the timing makes sense for your
setup before you commit.
