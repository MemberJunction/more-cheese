A member who buys for a specialty counter outside Milwaukee told us this week that her whole fall case plan got thrown out over one Tuesday tasting. She'd ordered against last year's list — the washed-rinds that moved well in October, the same three cheddars she reorders every autumn without thinking twice. Then she tasted the new lot from one of her regular suppliers and it wasn't the same cheese. Milder, less funk on the rind, a noticeably different make entirely under a familiar label.

"I almost just put it out anyway," she said. "Nobody would have complained. But it wasn't what I told my customers it would be." She spent the afternoon retasting everything on order and rebuilding the case around what was actually in front of her, not what the spreadsheet said should be there. By the time she was done, two of her four fall anchors had been swapped for wheels she hadn't planned to carry at all.

## Why this keeps happening

It's a small story, but it's one we hear some version of every fall. Cheese seasonality itself is real and worth respecting — several of the wheels members lean on this time of year are only made during a narrow window, aged on a schedule tied to when cows come off pasture or go onto hay, and released once a year on their own calendar. A buyer who orders from memory instead of checking what's actually in that year's release is going to be surprised, sooner or later, by a cheese that tastes different from the one she remembers, even with an unchanged label on the box.

For a buyer, that means the annual reorder-from-memory habit is getting less reliable, not more, as more producers scale up or bring in a new hand at the vat. Tasting blind, or at least tasting fresh before committing shelf space, isn't extra diligence anymore — it's close to required. Several of our retail members have told us they've started keeping a running note on each SKU: not just "customers like this" but "make style and release timing as of this date," so a shift doesn't sneak past them a second time.

## What we'd tell you to check

If you buy for a case rather than a single household, this is a good week to do what our Milwaukee member did on a smaller scale: pull your standing fall order and actually retaste it against last year's notes before it goes on the shelf under an unchanged sign. It costs an afternoon. It saves you from a customer telling you a favorite "isn't right" when the truth is the cheese changed and your case didn't catch up with it.

If you're building tasting habits for a newer staff member, our sensory evaluation coursework is a reasonable place to start. It won't replace years behind a counter, but it gives someone a working vocabulary for "this changed" instead of a shrug and a guess.

Our Milwaukee member put it more bluntly when we asked what she'd tell a newer buyer: taste the cheese in front of you, not the cheese in your notes. It sounds obvious until the afternoon before a delivery truck arrives, when it is much easier to just trust last year's paperwork and move on to the next line item.
