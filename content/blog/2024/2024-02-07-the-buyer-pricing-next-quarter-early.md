Most of the wholesale contracts a specialty cheese buyer signs in a given year get negotiated close to when the product ships. Not hers. A member who buys for a small regional grocery chain in the upper Midwest told us she's spent the last three winters locking her spring contracts in early February, weeks before most of her counterparts even start calling suppliers. "By the time everyone else is negotiating, I've already got my numbers," she said. "I'd rather be early and slightly wrong than late and definitely wrong." She's also been paying attention to a broader shift she's read about in trade coverage this winter: more shoppers cooking at home and treating a good specialty cheese as an affordable indulgence, which gives her more confidence to hold her retail pricing steady even in a year when the wholesale number moves.

Her reasoning is straightforward once you hear it. Milk price forecasts come out early in the year, and while they're never exact, the direction is usually more reliable than the decimal. This year's early read came in only slightly softer than last year's, with feed costs expected to ease at the same time — a combination that tends to hold supplier pricing steadier than the headline number alone would suggest. Rather than wait to see whether that holds, she uses the forecast as a starting point for conversations with her regular suppliers — three small creameries she's worked with for going on six years, plus one newer relationship she picked up at a competition two years ago.

## Why she buys ahead of the case, not for it

"I'm not pricing for what's on the shelf right now," she told us. "I'm pricing for what I'll need in April, which means I have to guess at what April's going to cost before anyone else has bothered to guess." That habit came out of a rough spring a few years back, when a supplier she'd waited too long to lock in called with a number well above what she'd budgeted, two weeks before a big seasonal order was due. She's built her calendar around not repeating that call.

It isn't a perfect system. She admitted that in years when the forecast moves a lot after February, she sometimes ends up slightly overpaying relative to where the market lands by summer. But she'd rather absorb a small, predictable overpayment than a large, unpredictable one. "A rind you can plan around beats a rind that surprises you," she said, which is about as close as she gets to a joke on this subject — she's plainly happier talking margin than making puns.

The habit has changed how she talks to her suppliers, too. Instead of a single annual negotiation, she now has a running conversation with each of them that starts every January and firms up by early February. "They know I'm coming with a number in hand, not asking them to set one for me," she said. "It makes the actual call shorter, and I think it makes them trust the relationship more, because I'm not disappearing for eleven months and reappearing with an ultimatum."

## What other buyers can take from it

Not every member has the standing order volume to negotiate contracts this far ahead, and she's the first to say her approach assumes a fairly stable relationship with suppliers who already know her buying patterns. But the underlying habit — treating the year's first price signals as a planning tool rather than something to wait out — scales down fine. Even a buyer placing a much smaller order can ask a supplier for an early-season conversation instead of a last-minute one.
