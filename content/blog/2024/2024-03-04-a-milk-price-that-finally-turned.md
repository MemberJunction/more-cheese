The federal milk price report that landed at the start of March showed the base Class I
price up for a second straight month, though still running below where it sat three years
ago for this point in the calendar. Feed costs, meanwhile, have been flat to slightly lower
since January. Neither number is dramatic by itself. Together, they're the closest thing to
good news the margin side of this business has had since the fall, and members have been
asking us what to make of it.

If you sell fluid milk or a wholesale cheese line priced off that federal report, you already
felt this in your own numbers. If you're a small creamery buying milk on contract, or you run
mostly off your own herd, the read-through takes a bit more translating — which is what a
market update in a federation newsletter is supposed to do.

## What's actually moving

Regional dairy economists have been describing the broader 2024 picture as markets "waiting
on demand" rather than actively recovering — prices have firmed, but the move has been
gradual and uneven by region, not a clean turnaround. That matches what we're hearing from
members directly: a Class I number that's better than January, a feed bill that isn't
climbing, and a general sense of relief rather than confidence.

Layer specialty cheese demand on top of that and the picture gets more interesting. Sales in
the artisan and specialty tier have kept growing steadily over the past year or two, and
that's the wedge you want to be standing in if input costs are easing at the same time
demand keeps climbing. It's the rare stretch where both sides of the ledger move your way at
once — even if, as the regional outlook notes, nobody's calling it a full recovery yet.

That doesn't mean margins are comfortable. Several members who called the office this month
are still carrying higher winter energy costs into their spring cost projections, and feed
prices, while calmer, haven't fallen back to where they sat before the last two years of
volatility. "Better" and "good" are different words, and this month sits squarely in
"better."

## What to do with it

If you've been putting off a price review because the market felt too unsettled to commit to
numbers, this is a reasonable month to run one. A milk price recovering off a multi-year low,
paired with steady specialty demand growth, is about as stable a footing as this category has
offered in a while — stable enough to plan a spring price sheet against, even with room built
in to adjust again by summer.

If you buy milk rather than produce it, ask your supplier where their contract sits relative
to the federal base and whether that gap has narrowed or widened since January. It's a
five-minute conversation, and it tells you whether the recovery we're describing is actually
reaching your invoice or just the headline number.
