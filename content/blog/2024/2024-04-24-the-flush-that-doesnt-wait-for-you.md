If you keep dairy goats, this is the week the math changes. Kidding runs early spring, and by late April most herds are deep into what producers call the flush — the stretch when milk volume climbs fast and the composition shifts with it, richer in fat and carrying the flavor of whatever the does have been grazing on. It's the best milk of the year for cheesemaking, and it does not wait around for anyone's schedule.

That's the part new goat-dairy members underestimate. Cow milk supply is comparatively steady across the year, so a make schedule built around it tolerates some slack. Goat milk doesn't offer the same grace. A herd in full flush can outpace a small creamery's vat capacity within a couple of weeks, and the flavor profile that makes April and May milk so good for fresh chèvre and bloomy small formats is itself a moving target — it changes as the pasture does.

## Why the window is short and why that's the point

Members who've worked a full kidding season describe the same arc: a slow start while does freshen, a sharp climb as lactation kicks in across the herd, a peak that might last six to eight weeks, and then a gradual taper as milk volume and richness both settle. Miss the peak and you've missed the year's best raw material — there's no saving it for later, because "later" is a different milk.

That compressed timeline is exactly why fresh and lightly aged formats dominate spring goat cheese. Chèvre, small bloomy wheels, and quick-turn logs let a creamery convert flush milk into finished product before the volume outpaces storage or the composition drifts past what a recipe was tuned for. Anything that requires months in a cave is fighting the calendar twice — once to make it, once to hold inventory through a season that's already moving fast underneath you.

## Where to actually learn this

We hear from a few members every spring who are cheesemaking-competent on cow's milk and genuinely caught off guard by how differently goat milk behaves — different fat globule structure, different set behavior, different tolerance for a make schedule that's running behind. If that's you, our course "Goat & Sheep Milk Cheesemaking" is built around exactly that gap. It's not a beginner cheesemaking course; it assumes you can already run a vat and spends its time on what's specific to small-ruminant milk: composition swings across the lactation curve, how to plan a fresh-format production line around a volume spike, and where the usual cow-milk instincts will actively mislead you.

Sheep's milk runs on a related but later calendar in most regions, which is its own trap for members who keep both species. It's tempting to treat a sheep make sheet as a goat make sheet with the numbers nudged, and this same course spends real time on why that substitution fails — the higher solids content in sheep's milk changes set time, yield, and moisture retention in ways that don't scale linearly from a goat-milk baseline. Members who've tried to wing it usually end up with a paste that's either too dry or won't drain evenly, and they don't find out until the wheels are weeks into the cave.

If you're mid-flush right now and reading this between batches, our practical suggestion is smaller and more immediate: don't let this week be the one you finally get around to writing down your make sheet variations. The flush is short, your notes from it are the only thing that carries into next year's flush, and next April you will not remember which batch got the extra ten minutes of draining. A dated line in a notebook now is worth more than a confident memory later.
