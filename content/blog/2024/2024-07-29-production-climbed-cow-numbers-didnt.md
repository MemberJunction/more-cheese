The federal production numbers that came out for July told two stories at once, and members have been asking us which one to believe. Cheese output rose again month over month, keeping pace with a market that has been hungry for it all year. At the same time, the national dairy herd kept shrinking, a few thousand head at a time, and the milk behind that cheese is coming from fewer cows than it was twelve months ago.

Both things are true, and neither one cancels the other out. Yields per cow have been climbing steadily enough to offset a smaller herd, which is a different kind of resilience than the industry has leaned on in past cycles. For a small or mid-sized creamery, that mostly shows up as a supply side that feels calmer than the headlines suggest — milk is available, and the price you're paying for it hasn't been the problem this summer.

## Where the margin actually sits

The number worth writing down is the milk-to-feed margin, which came in well above where it stood a year ago. Feed costs — corn, alfalfa, soybean meal — have all eased since last summer, and that eased pressure is doing more for a lot of members' bottom lines than any change on the retail side. If your make sheet has felt a little less punishing lately, this is most of why.

That doesn't mean every input has softened at the same rate, or that every region is seeing it the same way. A farmstead operation buying its own feed locally may not feel the national average the way a wholesale buyer of finished milk does. If your July numbers don't match what we're describing here, that gap is worth a conversation with your own accountant before you assume the market has turned in your favor across the board.

There's also a pricing-formula conversation running underneath all of this that's easy to miss if you only watch the monthly headline numbers. Regulators have been weighing changes to how federal milk orders set the price a processor pays for raw milk, and any change there eventually works its way down to what a small creamery pays for its own supply. It's a slower-moving story than a single month's margin, but it's the one that will still matter after this particular heat wave and this particular feed-cost dip have both faded from memory.

## What this means for the case

For members selling into a specialty case, the practical read is: supply has been steady enough that you shouldn't be scrambling for product, and the cost side has given many makers a bit of room to hold pricing rather than chase it upward. That's a rare combination, and it's worth using — whether that means locking in a supplier agreement for the fall or finally testing a price point you've been nervous about.

It's also a good stretch to build a small cushion rather than spend the whole margin the moment it shows up. Feed prices have eased before and firmed back up before, sometimes inside the same calendar year, and members who treat a good month as the new normal tend to be the ones caught flat-footed when it isn't. If you haven't already, this is a reasonable week to sit down with whoever handles your books and ask what a return to last year's feed costs would do to your plans for the fall.

We'll keep watching the herd-size trend, because a shrinking base of cows eventually catches up with a market even when yields are climbing. Export demand has also been picking up for cheese and a few other dairy categories, which is good news for the market as a whole but one more reason not to assume today's calm supply holds indefinitely. For now, treat the calm as real but not permanent, and use it while it lasts. If your committee liaison or regional rep hasn't heard from you this quarter, a short note on how your own numbers compare to the national picture helps us represent the membership accurately the next time this data comes up in a policy conversation.
