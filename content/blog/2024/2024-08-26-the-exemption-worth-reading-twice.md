Every time a "small-producer exemption" shows up in a federal food-safety rule, we hear from members who assume it means less paperwork. It rarely does, and this month we've been fielding enough of those calls that it's worth laying out plainly what the exemption actually covers and where it stops.

The exemption thresholds are built around annual sales, phased in on a schedule that pushes compliance deadlines further out the smaller the operation. That part is real relief for a farmstead creamery counting every dollar. What doesn't change, exemption or not, is the expectation that a facility making a product with a known pathogen history — and soft and semi-soft cheese sits squarely in that category — still runs a hazard analysis and keeps records that would hold up if a health department ever asked to see them. The exemption trims the paperwork format, not the underlying obligation to know your risks and manage them.

## What we're asking for

Our advocacy work this cycle has centered on a narrower ask: written guidance, in plain language, on what a "qualified exemption" does and doesn't cover for a dairy processor specifically, rather than generic produce-farm guidance that a lot of members have told us doesn't map cleanly onto a cave or a make room. We've filed comments making that case, and a handful of members have added their own letters describing exactly where the produce-focused language broke down for their operation. If you've hit that same wall — guidance written for a tomato farm that you had to reinterpret for a cheese vat — we want that example. Specifics move a comment file further than a general complaint does.

We're also watching the compliance calendar closely, since the staggered deadlines by farm size mean two neighboring creameries can be on different clocks depending on a few thousand dollars of annual sales. That's created some confusion at the workshop level, and it's part of why our Food Safety Committee keeps a running FAQ rather than a one-time explainer.

Regulators have shown recently that dairy-specific carve-outs do move, just slowly and one product category at a time — a narrow exemption affecting a single class of dairy product cleared earlier this year after a multi-year comment process, which is roughly the pace we expect for anything touching cheese. That timeline is frustrating if you're the member waiting on clarity for your own plant, but it's also the argument we make internally for staying in the file rather than treating one comment period as the whole campaign. The agencies that write this guidance revisit it in pieces, and a well-documented member example can sit useful for years before it becomes the basis for the next narrow fix.

There's a second layer past the sales-threshold question that catches even experienced operators: qualifying for reduced preventive-control requirements is not automatic. It typically requires an affirmative filing confirming your sales figures and your continued compliance with the baseline good-manufacturing-practice rules that never go away regardless of size. Members have told us they assumed the exemption applied itself once they crossed under a revenue line, then found out at an audit that the filing was overdue or never submitted. That's an easy, avoidable problem, and it's the kind of thing our Food Safety Committee's FAQ tries to flag before it becomes a finding.

## What to do this week

If your facility believes it qualifies for an exemption, don't treat that as the end of the conversation with your food-safety plan — treat it as the start of documenting why you still do the hazard analysis anyway. A short written record now is worth more than a reconstructed one under a deadline later. Members with questions about where their operation sits on the compliance timeline, or who want their exemption story added to our advocacy file, should reach the Food Safety Committee directly; specific, dated examples are what carry weight when we're at the table arguing for clearer guidance.
