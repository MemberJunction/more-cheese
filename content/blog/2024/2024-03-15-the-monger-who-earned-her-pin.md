A member who manages a specialty cheese counter in the upper Midwest told us she almost didn't
sit for her Certified Cheesemonger exam this winter, not because she doubted her palate, but
because she assumed the test would be mostly tasting. It wasn't. "I could tell you the paste,
the rind, the region on a wheel blind," she said. "What I had to actually study was shelf life,
allergen labeling, and how to talk a customer out of a cheese that's wrong for their dinner
without making them feel bad about asking."

That's a fair summary of what the credential is built to test. It's less a tasting panel and
more a working exam for someone who stands at a counter all day: product knowledge, yes, but
also food safety basics, storage and handling, and the retail judgment that keeps a case moving
without wasting product. Cheese trade coverage on the broader certified-cheese-professional
track this year has noted just how small the pool of people who hold any formal cheese
credential still is — which tracks with what our own numbers show. Most counters run on
tenure and tasting notes passed down informally, not on a documented credential.

## Three years of cases before the test

She'd worked the counter for three years before she felt ready, and most of that prep wasn't
formal study — it was the accumulated habit of writing down what she tasted, what sold, and what
came back. "I kept a notebook of every complaint and every compliment for two years before I
even looked at the exam outline," she said. "By the time I studied the material, half of it was
just naming things I already knew from getting yelled at about a runny brie in July."

The exam itself, she said, rewarded exactly that kind of counter-floor knowledge over
textbook memorization. Questions about how a cheese behaves as it warms on a cheese board,
what actually causes ammonia smell in a washed rind that's tipped past its window, how to read
a delivery invoice against what actually showed up in the box — those came easier than the
straight taxonomy questions about milk types and aging classifications, which she'd underrated
in her prep.

## What she'd tell a newer hire

Her advice to anyone behind her own counter now: start the notebook on day one, not year two.
"You don't remember the details of a bad batch six months later. You remember that it was bad."
She also points newer staff toward our Sensory Evaluation Certificate as a lower-stakes first
step — a way to build the vocabulary and habit of structured tasting notes before taking on
something as comprehensive as the full Certified Cheesemonger track.

If you manage a counter and have staff who taste well but have never put it into words on
paper, that's the gap worth closing first. The Education Committee keeps a running list of
upcoming certification prep sessions; a call to them is a reasonable next step before anyone on
your team signs up cold.
