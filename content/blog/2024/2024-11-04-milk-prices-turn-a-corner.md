If you've been watching the all-milk price with a slightly grim expression for most of this
year, this month should feel like a break. Trade coverage tracking the national figures has
started noting a modest step up heading into next year, and members who sell wholesale are
already seeing the early edge of it in their fall contracts. Nobody is calling it a boom.
Several members are calling it, cautiously, "the first quarter in a while that didn't feel like
treading water."

Cheese is doing a lot of the work. Weekly trade reporting on per-capita cheese consumption has
been pointing at steady growth through the year, and demand tends to get stronger from here —
retailers build out their gift and holiday cases through November, and specialty counters lean
into that window harder than almost any other stretch of the year. A member who runs a small
retail case in the upper Midwest told us she's already reordered twice this month for cheeses she
doesn't usually restock until December. "People are buying ahead this year," she said. "I don't
know if that's optimism or just habit, but I'm not arguing with it."

## Why the herd numbers matter as much as the price, and what we're watching next

The national milking herd has grown slightly compared to a year ago — not a dramatic swing, but
enough that supply has stayed ahead of demand for most of the year, which is part of why prices
softened in the first place. That combination of a slightly bigger herd and steadily improving
milk-per-cow output is exactly the kind of thing that keeps a recovering price from running away
in either direction. For members planning purchases or locking in supply agreements, that's
useful context: this isn't a shortage-driven bump, it's demand catching up to a supply base
that's been comfortable all year.

For farmstead members milking their own herd, the math is more personal than a national index.
Feed costs have eased somewhat from their peak, which helps, but input costs generally don't
retreat as fast as they climbed. A modest price recovery is welcome. It is not, on its own,
enough to undo a rough first half for anyone who was closer to the margin.

Two things are worth flagging for members making case or production plans over the next several
weeks. First, cheese sales tend to concentrate hard in a short window before the holidays, which
means members selling direct or through a retail partner should be thinking now about inventory
timing, not in early December when the shelf is already being built. Second, milk movement and
testing requirements for lactating dairy cattle have tightened over the course of this year in
several parts of the country, and that has real implications for anyone shipping milk or
cultures across state lines — more on that in a separate post this week, since it's worth its
own space rather than a paragraph here.

None of this is a forecast members should treat as a guarantee. Price recoveries in dairy have a
way of stalling out on nobody's schedule but the market's own. But for members who've spent most
of this year watching a soft price and wondering when it would turn, this is the first month in
a while where the numbers are moving in the right direction, even if only by a little.

If you're weighing a supply contract or a wholesale price adjustment for the new year, the
Membership & Outreach Committee can point you toward members who've been through a similar
negotiation recently — sometimes the most useful data point isn't the national index, it's
someone who signed a deal last month.
