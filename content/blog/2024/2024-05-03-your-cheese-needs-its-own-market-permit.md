Farmers market season is opening across a lot of the country this week, and every spring we hear from the same kind of member: someone who's sold cheese wholesale or out of their own shop for years, decides to add a market stand, and assumes the paperwork will be roughly the same as what a neighboring jam or bread vendor deals with. It usually isn't, and the gap catches people at exactly the wrong moment — a week before opening day, not a month before.

Most states run some version of a cottage food law that lets home producers sell certain low-risk items — baked goods, jams, dry mixes — with a light registration and no commercial kitchen inspection. Several states expanded those laws again this year, and a few state legislatures had cottage food bills moving through committee again this spring. Dairy products are almost never on that list. Cheese, like meat and most perishable animal products, is treated as a higher-risk category everywhere we've checked, which means a separate license, a separate inspection, and often separate labeling rules from whatever your neighbor's stand is operating under.

## What actually trips members up

The most common surprise isn't the license itself — most of our members already hold a proper dairy processing license for their regular sales channel. It's whether that existing license and its labeling actually covers a market stand specifically. Some states require a distinct retail or direct-sale endorsement layered on top of your processing license before you can sell the same cheese out of a folding table instead of your own shop or through a distributor. If your license was written around wholesale or a fixed retail location, don't assume it silently extends to a mobile setup without checking.

The second surprise is temperature control in the field. A shop case holds temperature reliably; a cooler in a tent on an 80-degree Saturday does not, and a market inspector who shows up unannounced is checking exactly that. Members who've done this for years tell us the fix is boring but effective: a thermometer you actually read and log every hour you're set up, not just one sitting in the cooler for show.

The third surprise, and the one that catches even experienced members, is that the market itself has its own rulebook layered on top of the state's. A market manager can require proof of your dairy license before they'll hand you a stall, ask for a certificate of insurance you didn't think to renew, or set its own rules about what can be sampled versus what can only be sold pre-wrapped. None of that is a food safety regulation in the legal sense, but it can shut down your first Saturday just as fast as a state inspector can. Call the market before you show up with a truck full of wheels, not after.

A handful of states have been actively updating their cottage food rules again this year, mostly around labeling requirements and what sales channels are allowed — a reminder that the boundary between "homemade food" and "regulated dairy product" isn't static even where you've operated for years. If your state touched its cottage food law recently, it's worth a quick check on whether the changes brush up against your category at all, even if dairy usually sits outside that law entirely. We'd rather members ask us a question that turns out to be unnecessary than skip the question and find out at the market gate.
