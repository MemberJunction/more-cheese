Of the seven credentials the federation offers, the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate has the shortest validity window: two years, against five or more for most of the others. Members occasionally ask us why, usually right after they've discovered their own certificate lapsed without them noticing. The short answer is that food-safety practice moves faster than the rest of the craft, and a credential that doesn't keep pace with it isn't doing its job.

## Why two years and not five

A credential like Advanced Affinage can reasonably hold its value for half a decade — the physics of a cave don't change much. Food safety is different. Sampling protocols get revised, recall procedures get tightened after other companies' incidents, and the guidance around environmental monitoring in a small processing space has shifted meaningfully even in the past couple of years. A trade-press recall notice earlier this year was a plain reminder of why this matters in practice: a listeria-related recall widened after investigators found more affected product than the initial notice covered, which is the exact scenario a current environmental monitoring plan is built to catch earlier. Members holding an outdated HACCP certificate aren't necessarily doing anything wrong day to day, but they're more likely to be working from monitoring assumptions that predate a protocol update they never saw.

This is also the credential most directly tied to the industry's oldest ongoing food-safety debate: the 60-day aging requirement for cheeses made from unpasteurized milk. That rule has been argued over by researchers and regulators for more than a decade, with real disagreement over how consistently it eliminates pathogens across different cheese styles and moisture levels. We're not in a position to settle that argument, and this credential doesn't ask members to take a side in it. What it does ask is that anyone working with raw-milk-adjacent production understands the current state of that guidance well enough to build a real hazard analysis around it, rather than relying on an inherited assumption about what "sixty days" actually guarantees.

## What recertification actually involves

Recertifying isn't a full retake of the original coursework. It's a shorter review module covering what's changed since your last cycle, plus a current hazard-analysis exercise specific to your own production setup — not a generic worksheet, but one built around your actual layout, your actual product lines, and where your last audit (internal or third-party) flagged soft spots. Members tell us this part is more useful than the original certification exam, if only because by year two or four you have real incidents and near-misses to reason from instead of hypotheticals.

If your certificate lapses before you recertify, you're not locked out of anything immediately, but a lapsed HACCP credential is the kind of thing a wholesale buyer or a retail account sometimes asks about directly, and "it expired a few months ago and I haven't gotten to it" is a worse answer to have ready than it needs to be.

The practical suggestion is a two-minute one: check your certificate's expiration in your member portal profile before the year turns over, not after. Members who let it lapse over the holidays tend to also be the members busiest with holiday-volume production, which is exactly the wrong time to be scheduling a recertification module around everything else on the calendar. If you're within six months of expiration, our Food Safety Committee can tell you what's changed in the review module since your last cycle so you know roughly how much time to set aside.
