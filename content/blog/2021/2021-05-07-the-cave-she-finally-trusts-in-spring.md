Spring is the season that breaks new affineurs, and one member — a small farmstead producer in the upper Midwest, three years into running her own aging room — told us this was the first one that didn't. Not because the weather cooperated. It didn't. Because she'd finally stopped treating every humidity swing as an emergency.

Her first two springs went the way most people's do: outdoor temperatures climb faster than a below-grade cave can track, humidity drifts with them, and a producer used to a stable winter cave suddenly watches rind development go sideways on a batch she did everything right on. "I used to open the door and panic," she told us. "Now I open the door, write down the number, and wait to see if it's still moving in three hours before I touch anything."

## What changed wasn't the cave

The physical space didn't change — same dehumidifier, same shelving, same below-grade room she's had since she started. What changed is that she now logs humidity and temperature twice a day instead of checking only when something looks off, and she keeps last spring's numbers next to this spring's on the same sheet. That comparison turned out to matter more than any single reading. A 4% humidity drift in isolation looks alarming. The same drift, next to a note that says "this happened at the same point last year and corrected itself in four days," is just weather.

She credits part of the shift to a workshop she took last year — not one built specifically around spring transitions, but one on defect diagnosis that pushed her to separate "this looks wrong" from "this is going to stay wrong." That distinction is most of what a first-year affineur is missing, and it's not something you get from a single reading. It comes from having enough of your own data to know what normal drift looks like in your specific room, in your specific season, which nobody else's chart can tell you.

## What this spring looked like for her

This year, with the same seasonal swing hitting her cave in April, she made one small process change and left the rest alone: she moved her most humidity-sensitive wheels — a bloomy-rind batch that's punished her before — to the shelf nearest the dehumidifier intake, where the swing is smaller, and left her hardier aged cheddar wheels on the shelves that drift more. Small adjustment, and it came from a season of trusting her own log over her own nerves.

The timing helped, too. Spring flush — the seasonal surge in milk volume and quality that comes as animals move onto fresh pasture — means she's making more cheese right now than at almost any other point in her year, with milk that behaves a little differently batch to batch. That's exactly the wrong moment to be improvising in the aging room: a bigger spring batch means a bigger loss if a shelf's worth of wheels turns on you, which is one more reason her steadier, log-first approach is paying off now rather than later.

She's also blunt about what didn't work in her first two years: guessing at fixes. A dehumidifier setting changed on a hunch, a shelf moved because a wheel "seemed off," a panic call to another member mid-afternoon. None of that is wrong exactly, but none of it built the kind of pattern recognition that only comes from comparing this week against the same week a year ago, in her own room, with her own wheels.

If you're heading into your first or second spring managing an aging room and the swings are rattling you, the fix usually isn't a better dehumidifier — it's a log you actually keep and actually compare. The **Listeria Control in Small Creameries** and defect-diagnosis coursework both build that habit early, and it pays off exactly at the moment you'd otherwise be guessing.
