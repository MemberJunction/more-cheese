Trade coverage this year keeps returning to the same theme: food processors of every size are struggling to hold onto staff, and dairy plants are not exempt. For a large operation, a departure means reassigning a line. For a small creamery, it can mean the one person who actually understood the food safety plan is suddenly gone, and whoever's left is holding a binder they've never had to defend to an inspector.

That's the scenario behind a noticeable uptick in enrollment this fall in our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate. It's not a new credential and it's not changed in scope, but members are telling us plainly why they're finally getting around to it: a hazard-analysis plan that exists only in one person's head is not a plan, it's a liability with a name attached, and this year has made that risk feel closer than it used to.

## What the credential actually covers

The certificate walks through the seven-step hazard analysis and critical control point framework as it applies specifically to dairy processing — identifying where contamination risk actually sits in a make and aging process, setting critical limits that make sense for cheese rather than being borrowed from a generic food-safety template, and building the monitoring and recordkeeping habits that let someone other than the plan's original author demonstrate it's being followed. That last part is the piece most small creameries skip when they write a plan themselves, and it's the piece an inspector asks about first.

It is not a substitute for your written food safety plan, and it does not certify your facility — it certifies that the person holding it understands how to build and maintain one. What it buys a small creamery is redundancy: a second or third person on staff who could pick the plan up and run it if the person who wrote it left tomorrow. Several members this fall have specifically enrolled a newer hire alongside their longest-tenured one, precisely so the knowledge doesn't live in one place.

A plan that only one person understands has a second problem beyond the obvious one. Inspectors don't just ask whether a plan exists — they ask whoever's on the floor that day to walk through a critical control point and explain why it's set where it is. A binder nobody but the departed employee ever opened doesn't hold up well under that kind of question, even when the plan itself was written correctly. Redundant training is as much about surviving an inspection as it is about surviving a resignation.

## Where to start if this is new

If your creamery has one person who "handles food safety" and no one else who could explain the plan to an inspector on short notice, that's the gap this credential is built to close. The certificate runs as a self-paced online course with a proctored exam at the end, and members have told us the biggest time cost isn't the material — it's finally writing down the plan they'd been running informally for years.

Members who've recently added a second certificate holder describe a similar sequence: the newer staffer shadows the aging or make process for a week with the plan open beside them, then walks the same process solo while the certificate holder checks their monitoring log against what they'd have written themselves. It's slower than assuming the plan speaks for itself, and it's the only method that's actually caught the gaps between what a plan says and what a floor crew has been doing out of habit. If staffing has been unsettled at your creamery this year, that's not a reason to put this off. It's the reason to move it up.
