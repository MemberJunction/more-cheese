If you've looked at a federal price announcement in the last few weeks and done a double take, you're not alone. Class III has been climbing steadily through the spring, and Class IV has followed it up. Neither number moves your make sheet by itself, but both are worth understanding before you set summer pricing.

The short version: cheese and dry whey prices have firmed up, and butter and nonfat dry milk have moved with them. Behind both is export demand that has been unusually strong for a spring quarter, with milk-solids-equivalent exports running well above where they sat a year ago. For members who sell primarily direct or wholesale within their own region, this can feel abstract. It isn't. Class III sets the floor a lot of buyers use when they think about what "fair" looks like on a wheel of cheddar or a truckle of Gouda, whether you sell into that system or not.

## What actually moves for a small producer

A rising Class III doesn't hand you a higher price. It changes what your buyers expect to pay, and it changes what your milk is worth if you're buying it in rather than milking your own herd. If you're a make-to-order creamery buying fluid milk, watch your input cost line before you watch the headline number — feed costs have been the more stubborn variable this year, and margin coverage can look tighter than the price chart suggests once you net the two against each other.

If you sell finished cheese wholesale, this is a reasonable moment to revisit your price list with a distributor or a buyer, especially if you haven't touched it since before the price run started. Bring your own cost numbers, not the federal announcement — a buyer who deals in commodity blocks and a creamery selling a hand-turned wheel are working from different economics, and conflating the two rarely goes well at the negotiating table.

Two numbers are worth tracking through the rest of the spring, and neither of them is the price announcement itself. The first is your own feed or fluid-milk cost, which has been the more volatile line this year and can erase a Class III gain faster than you'd expect. The second is whatever your primary distributor or largest wholesale account is doing with their own price list — if they're not adjusting in response to a firmer market, that's worth a conversation before you assume your invoice should move.

It's also worth remembering that a national price series describes a national commodity market, and most of our members aren't selling into that market directly. A hand-ladled fresh chèvre and a 40-pound block of cheddar respond to completely different pressures, even when the same federal number is technically "the" price for both. Use the headline as a signal that conditions are shifting, not as a number to copy onto your own price sheet.

## Where to take questions

We don't have wholesale pricing benchmarks to hand out — margins are specific to your operation and your buyers, and a number from us would do more harm than good. What we can point you to is the federation's market-data roundup, updated as new federal numbers post, and the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course, which walks through how to read a Class III move against your own cost sheet rather than against the headline. If your board or your accountant wants the underlying federal releases, the Membership & Outreach Committee can point you to where we've been pulling them from.

A firmer export number is good news in the aggregate. What it means at your particular counter is a smaller, more specific question, and it's worth working through before you print a new price list — ideally with your own cost figures open next to the announcement, not instead of them.
