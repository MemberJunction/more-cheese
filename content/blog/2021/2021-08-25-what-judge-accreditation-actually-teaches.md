Every year a handful of members ask us whether Competition Judge Accreditation is worth the time if they don't actually plan to judge anything. The honest answer is: usually yes, and for reasons that have little to do with sitting behind a scoring sheet.

The credential is built around calibration — teaching a group of tasters to describe and score the same wheel the same way, using a shared vocabulary instead of whatever adjectives come to mind first. That skill turns out to be just as useful on the production side of the counter as the judging side of it, and it's a large part of why the credential exists at all: a scoring system is only as good as the agreement behind it.

## What the credential actually covers

Accreditation walks candidates through the standard scoring framework used in our competition categories — appearance, texture, aroma, flavor, and finish, each scored against category-specific expectations rather than personal preference. A washed-rind entry isn't dinged for smelling like a washed rind; it's scored on whether it does the things a washed rind is supposed to do, cleanly and consistently. Candidates practice scoring flighted samples, writing feedback a maker can actually use, and — the part people underestimate — defending a score to fellow judges without getting defensive about it.

The framework itself splits roughly into two lenses candidates learn to hold at once: a technical read, which deducts for defects against a near-perfect baseline, and an aesthetic read, which credits what a wheel does well within its category. Most new candidates lean naturally toward one or the other. The coursework's real job is teaching you to run both passes deliberately instead of blending them into one gut reaction.

None of that requires you to want a seat on next year's panel. A cheesemaker who's been through the coursework tends to write a tighter, more useful make-day note. A cheesemonger who's been through it can tell a customer why one wheel outscored another instead of just liking one better. It's the same discipline the Sensory Evaluation Certificate builds toward, one level up.

## Who it tends to help most

We see three groups get the most out of it. Producers preparing to enter competition for the first time, who want to know what a judge is actually looking for before they submit a wheel. Retail and wholesale buyers who need a defensible way to explain a purchasing decision to a supplier. And members already active on our Awards & Competition Committee, for whom it's close to a prerequisite — you can volunteer as a steward without it, but you can't score.

It's not a light credential. Expect a real time commitment across several flighted tasting sessions plus a written component, not a single afternoon. Members who've come through it tend to say the calibration sessions — where your score gets compared directly against three other judges tasting the same sample — are the part that actually changes how they taste.

If you're weighing it, talk to the Awards & Competition Committee before you enroll — they can tell you whether the timing lines up with judging needs for next year's competition cycle, and whether a Sensory Evaluation Certificate first would smooth the on-ramp. Either way, it's one of the few credentials in our catalog that pays off whether or not you ever pick up a scoring sheet for real.
