Most of the calls we get this time of year are about the case — what's moving, what's not, how
to keep a wholesale account happy through a hot stretch. This week one member's call was about
the field instead. She runs a small farmstead operation in the upper Midwest, milks her own herd,
and has spent the last month watching alfalfa acreage in her county go from "tight" to "worrying"
as this summer's drought has settled in harder than anyone budgeted for in the spring.

## When the input, not the output, is the problem

Her creamery is a member of Golden Prairie Fromage's regional buying group, which has helped some
— shared trucking, shared bulk feed contracts — but shared contracts don't fix a shortage,
they just spread it around more fairly. Her feed bill is up meaningfully over last year, and
because her herd size is fixed by pasture capacity she can't already grow, the math she's now
running isn't "how do I sell more cheese," it's "how many wheels does this month's hay actually
buy me." That's an unfamiliar question for a maker who has spent most of her career thinking
about affinage and rind development rather than commodity forecasting.

What she's landed on for now is a leaner production calendar: fewer experimental batches, more of
her proven, reliably-selling wheels, and a harder look at which of her cultures and inputs have
crept in price without her noticing because they were never the line item she watched closely.
It's not a dramatic pivot, but it's a real one, and she's not alone in making it — feed and input
costs have been a recurring thread in Membership & Outreach Committee calls all summer, mostly
from members in drought-affected counties who are doing the same quiet arithmetic she is.

## What we'd ask of the rest of you

If your region hasn't been hit by this summer's dry conditions, it's worth checking in on a
member who's in one, especially if you buy from a shared distributor or belong to the same
regional group. A short conversation about where someone's actually feeling the squeeze — feed,
fuel, packaging, all three — tells us more than a market report does, and it helps the federation
make a better case when we're asking a lender or a state program to treat drought-driven cost
pressure as the real, documentable thing it is rather than a general complaint about a hard year.

She'll be at this month's Annual Conference, virtually, and has said she's happy to talk through
what the leaner calendar has looked like in practice if it's useful to another small operation
facing the same numbers. If that's you, find her in the small-creamery breakout room on the day,
or ask your regional contact for an introduction beforehand.

Her other piece of advice, offered half as a joke and half not, is to call your feed supplier
before you need to, not after. She started renegotiating her hay contract in June, before her
usual supplier's own shortage forced a price jump, and thinks the two weeks of lead time bought
her a meaningfully better rate than members who waited until their bins were closer to empty. It's
a small, unglamorous piece of business advice next to a make sheet or a cave log, but it's the
kind of thing that only comes from someone who's actually lived through the tight month, not from
a market report written about it after the fact. In the meantime, we're glad her count of wheels
going out the door is still climbing, even if the path to get there this year runs through the hay
barn first.
