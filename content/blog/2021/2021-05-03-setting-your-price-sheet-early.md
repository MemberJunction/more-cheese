If you buy milk instead of milking your own herd, you've probably noticed your invoice creeping up since February. Class III has strengthened three months running, and the run-up has a real driver: cheese demand has been firming as school cafeterias and restaurants reopen their doors, and export orders — Mexico in particular — came in stronger this spring than they had in a year. None of that shows up on your price sheet automatically. You have to put it there.

Members have been writing in with a version of the same question: do we raise prices now, while the market is moving, or wait and see if it settles back down? There isn't a universal answer, but there's a wrong way to decide it — by gut feel, a week before an invoice is due, with no paper trail. That's the exact gap our **Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese** course was built to close, and this is a good month to take it if you haven't already.

## What actually belongs in the calculation

A price sheet built on last year's input costs is a price sheet that's already wrong. The course walks through a margin model that separates three things people tend to blend together: your raw milk cost (which moves monthly and sometimes faster), your fixed production cost per wheel (which barely moves at all), and your target margin (which is a business decision, not a market fact). When milk costs rise the way they have this spring, the instinct is to raise the final price by the same percentage. That's usually wrong — it either eats your margin on the cheap-to-make items or prices you out of the market on the expensive ones, depending on which direction you round.

Instead, work the increase through per-wheel cost first, then decide separately whether your margin target still makes sense for each product line. A young, high-turnover cheese and a twelve-month aged wheel do not absorb a milk price increase the same way, because the aged wheel is also carrying months of storage and shrink that the young one isn't. If your price sheet treats them identically, you're guessing on one of the two.

## A note on timing, not just math

There's a second piece worth naming plainly: raising prices takes longer to land with wholesale accounts than it does at your own counter or farmers market stand. If you sell through a distributor, build in the lag — a price change you send this week may not reach the shelf for a full billing cycle. Members who've been burned by this tend to build a small buffer into their number rather than pricing exactly to the current month's cost, since the cost you're pricing against today may not be the cost you're actually paying by the time the new sheet takes effect.

None of this requires predicting where milk prices go next. It requires having a model that updates cleanly when they move, instead of a spreadsheet you're afraid to touch. If your last price review was more than two quarters ago, or if you've never separated raw cost from margin target in writing, that's the sign this course is for you this cycle rather than next one.
