Milk kept coming this spring, and the price of it kept giving a little ground. A federal supply-and-demand forecast released in mid-May raised its national milk-production estimate for the year on the strength of higher cow numbers, and pegged Class III — the benchmark most cheesemakers watch when they price a make sheet — a bit firmer than the month before, but still well off where futures markets had been pointing earlier in the spring. Since that report, Class III has drifted down further as the extra milk actually showed up in plants.

That combination — more milk, a softer Class III — usually means one thing for a small or mid-sized producer: your raw material got a little cheaper while your finished case stayed roughly where it was. If your creamery buys milk rather than milking its own herd, that is a margin opportunity worth checking against your actual invoices rather than assuming it based on the headline number. Regional basis has been unusually noisy this year, and what the national indicator does and what your co-op bills you can diverge by more than usual in a supply glut.

## What we're hearing from members

The read we're getting from wholesale accounts is more encouraging than the price chart alone suggests. Several members told us this week that order volumes finally look like a normal spring rather than a cautious one — cheese counters are restocking, and a couple of members who cut SKU counts last year are adding lines back for summer. One distributor member described it simply: "the case is full again, and people are buying what's in it." That is the useful signal underneath the futures noise: demand recovering roughly in step with a production rebound, rather than one racing ahead of the other.

None of this changes the basics of pricing a wheel. If your cost of production runs meaningfully above Class III, as it does for most farmstead and artisan operations, this month's softness is a reminder to re-check your margin against current input costs rather than last year's spreadsheet. Feed, packaging, and freight have all moved since you last built that model, even where the milk line looks friendlier.

A national production number is also an average of a lot of very different herds and a lot of very different milk contracts, so it tells you the direction of travel, not your number. Members on fixed-price contracts with their processor may not see this softness reach their bottom line at all this quarter; members buying on a spot or near-spot basis are the ones most likely to feel it directly, for better or worse depending on which side of the transaction they sit on.

## Where to take this next

If you haven't recalculated your cost per pound since early spring, now is a reasonable moment to do it — a soft Class III month is exactly when it's easiest to convince yourself your margin is better than it actually is. Members working through that math are welcome to bring it to the Membership & Outreach Committee's office hours, or to look at the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course when the next cohort opens.
