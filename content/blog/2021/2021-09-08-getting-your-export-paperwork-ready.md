If you've been following the cross-border dairy quota dispute that's been working through a formal review process this year, you already know the headline: a neighboring country's quota-allocation rules are being challenged, and a ruling is expected before the year is out. What you may not know is that the outcome matters a lot less to most of our members than the paperwork does.

We heard this from a handful of small producers this summer who tried to fill an export order for the first time and got stuck — not on tariffs, not on quota, but on a certificate of origin form filled out incorrectly, or a health certificate that didn't match the shipment's actual composition. One member described spending three days on the phone sorting out a single pallet because the invoice listed a cheese style the receiving country's customs office didn't recognize by that name. None of that was about trade policy. It was about a form.

## Why this matters more this year

Two things make 2021 a worse year than most to get this wrong. First, a lot of members are shipping smaller, more frequent orders than they used to — which we wrote about earlier this week — and each of those smaller shipments still needs its own complete paperwork. A mistake that used to cost you time on one large annual order now costs you time on six or eight smaller ones. Second, the paperwork itself keeps changing: a new certificate requirement for one overseas market takes effect later this month, and members who ship there told us they only found out about it because a customs broker mentioned it in passing. If you export at all, it's worth checking whether any of your regular destinations have a certificate update landing this fall before you assume last year's form is still the right one.

Export Documentation Essentials — one of our standing course offerings, currently running its Brook cohort — walks through the paperwork side specifically: certificates of origin, health and sanitary certificates, commercial invoices that match what customs actually expects to see, and how to handle a shipment that includes more than one cheese style or format. It's not a trade-policy briefing. It's built around the actual forms members have gotten wrong, including a few of the examples above.

A few concrete habits the course pushes hard, and that are worth adopting even before your enrollment date:

- **Match your invoice language to the receiving country's tariff nomenclature**, not your own marketing name for the cheese. A "farmstead washed-rind wheel" on your label can still need to be classified correctly on the form.
- **Keep a template on file for your most common shipment types** so you're not rebuilding a certificate from scratch every time, and so a smaller, more frequent order doesn't become a bigger time sink than a large one used to be.
- **Confirm your health certificate reflects your current facility inspection status**, not last year's. A lapsed inspection date is one of the most common reasons a shipment gets held.

## If you ship, or want to start

You don't need to be actively exporting to benefit from this course — several members have told us they took it before their first international order specifically so the first shipment wouldn't be the one where they learned all of this the hard way. If your creamery is weighing whether an export channel makes sense as wholesale volume domestically stays uneven, this is a reasonable place to start before you commit to a first shipment.

Enrollment details for the current cohort are on the courses page, and the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with a member who's shipped internationally if you want to talk through your specific situation before you sign up.
