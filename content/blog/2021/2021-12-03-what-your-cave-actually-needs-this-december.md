Every fall, we get a version of the same question from members running small aging rooms: their cheese is aging slower, their humidity readings have dropped, and they're not sure whether to intervene or just let winter do what winter does. The honest answer is both, and it depends on which of those two things is actually happening in your room.

Cold weather does two things to a small cave that aren't the same problem. It slows the biochemistry of aging, which is normal and mostly harmless — cheese sitting a week or two longer to hit the same texture isn't a defect, it's just a slower clock. But cold outside air is also drier air, and if your cave draws makeup air from outside or sits in an unheated space, your humidity can fall well below the range most aging cheeses want, well before your thermometer tells you anything is wrong. Hard natural-rind styles generally want somewhere around 80 to 85 percent relative humidity, while bloomy, washed-rind, and blue styles want it closer to 90 to 95. Rind cracking, uneven moisture loss, and a paste that dries out faster at the edges than the center are usually a humidity problem wearing a cold-weather disguise.

## Reading your room instead of your thermostat

The fix isn't complicated, but it does require checking more than temperature. If you're running a converted refrigerator or a wine-fridge setup, a hygrometer is worth more than a second thermometer this month — most small units hold temperature well and lose humidity fast, especially with frequent door openings during a busy pre-holiday production stretch. A shallow tray of water, a damp cloth over open shelving, or a small humidifier sized to the room are all standard fixes members have used successfully, and none of them require new equipment budgeted for next year. Larger rooms typically need something closer to several gallons of added water a week through the coldest stretch to hold steady; a small home-scale cave needs far less, but it needs it checked more often, since there's less air volume to buffer a swing.

Turning and inspection frequency matters more in winter, not less. A slower aging clock means more time for a small mold problem or a dry patch to establish itself before it shows up as a finished-product defect. If your usual schedule is a weekly turn, consider moving to twice a week through the coldest stretch, especially for anything with a natural or washed rind that depends on consistent moisture at the surface. Wood shelving, if you're using it, helps buffer these swings on its own, since it absorbs and releases moisture more evenly than metal or plastic racking.

## Where to take this further

This is exactly the ground covered in Cave Management, one of the federation's ongoing course offerings, which walks through humidity troubleshooting, seasonal adjustment, and the record-keeping habits that catch a drifting room before it becomes a batch problem. Affinage Fundamentals and Maturation Planning for Small Rooms cover adjacent ground if you're setting up a new space rather than tuning an existing one, and either is a reasonable place to start if you're building a cave from scratch this winter rather than adjusting one you already run.

If your cave is doing something you can't explain from temperature and humidity alone — an off smell, an unexpected mold, a rind behaving differently than it has in past winters — that's a Food Safety Committee question, not a guessing game. Reach out before you write off a batch; a lot of what looks like a lost wheel in December turns out to be a room that just needed a tray of water and a closer look.
