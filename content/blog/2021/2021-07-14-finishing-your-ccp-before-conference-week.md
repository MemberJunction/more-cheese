With the Annual Conference now a little over a week out, we've had a steady run of members
asking the same practical question: is there still time to finish the Certified Cheese
Professional credential before it? For a good number of you, yes — and it's worth knowing what
that credential actually covers before you decide whether closing it out this month is worth the
push.

## What the CCP actually tests

The Certified Cheese Professional designation is the federation's broadest credential, and
deliberately so. Where something like the Certified Cheesemonger badge is retail-facing and the
Advanced Affinage Certificate is narrowly about cave management, the CCP asks you to demonstrate
competence across production, sensory evaluation, food safety fundamentals, and basic business
practices — the full arc of what it takes to run a small cheese operation rather than one slice
of it. It's the credential most members describe afterward as "harder than I expected," usually
because the business and food-safety sections catch people who assumed the exam would be mostly
about tasting and technique.

That breadth is also why it tends to take longer than our other credentials. Most members who
finish it have come through it via one of the underlying courses first — Pricing & Margin for
Specialty Cheese covers a meaningful chunk of the business section, and Sensory Foundations feeds
directly into the tasting component. If you haven't touched either yet, this month's push
probably isn't realistic; better to plan for the credential this fall than to rush a section you
haven't built the foundation for.

If you do finish before the conference opens on July 20, we'll be recognizing new CCP holders
during the opening session, same as we did with new credential-earners last year over video. It's
a small thing, but members have told us it matters more than we initially assumed — a name read
out and a credential noted publicly carries weight for members who've spent a year working toward
it largely alone at a kitchen table or a cave bench, without a cohort in the room to mark the
moment with them.

To be included in that recognition, your final assessment needs to be submitted and graded before
the conference's opening block, which given current review turnaround means getting your
materials in by the end of this week at the latest. If you're close but not quite there, don't
force it — the fall cohort will have its own opening-session recognition slot, and a rushed
submission helps no one.

For members earlier in the process, the more useful move this week isn't racing toward July 20 —
it's picking which of the two feeder courses to start with based on where your actual gaps are.
If your make sheet is solid but your pricing worksheet makes you wince, start with the business
side. If you can hold a room's attention describing a paste's texture but couldn't defend a
minimum-margin calculation, the sensory work will come easier and the business section deserves
the head start. Either way, reach out to the Education Committee before you register; they can
tell you honestly whether your existing course history already covers ground you don't need to
repeat.
