Every member with a small aging room hits the same moment each September: the outside air
stops behaving the way it did all summer, and whatever settings got the cave through July
stop being enough. If you are running a converted basement or a single walk-in rather than a
fully engineered facility, this is the point in the year worth paying closest attention to,
because the swing between a warm afternoon and a cold night is exactly the kind of
instability that shows up on a rind two months later as an uneven bloom or a crack you
didn't put there on purpose.

Our Maturation Planning for Small Rooms course exists because this problem is common and
underdiscussed — most affinage writing assumes a room with enough thermal mass and
mechanical control that the outside season barely registers. A lot of our members' rooms
aren't that. If yours swings more than a couple of degrees between morning and evening right
now, that is useful information, not a problem to hide from a wheel.

Most guidance you'll find on aging conditions gives you a target and stops there: something
in the neighborhood of 50–55°F and 80–90% humidity for most aged styles, tighter for a
bloomy rind, a little drier for a hard natural rind. That target isn't wrong, but it assumes
you can hold it. The harder skill, and the one this time of year actually tests, is noticing
when your room is drifting away from that target before the cheese tells you with a cracked
rind or a slimy patch that wasn't there last week.

This is also, as it happens, an unusually warm September for a lot of the country, with
federal climate summaries tracking one of the warmest Septembers on record and afternoon
highs running well above normal across the Midwest and Northeast in the back half of this
very week. That matters for a small room more than it would for a large facility, because a
converted basement or single walk-in is borrowing more of its behavior from the outside air
than a fully mechanical room ever does. If your cave has felt harder to hold steady the last
few days, it isn't just you — the outside conditions actually have been more volatile than
usual this month.

## Three things worth checking this month

**Your humidity source, not just your reading.** A hygrometer will tell you the room read 85%
this morning. It won't tell you whether that number came from a stable source or from a
door that was open for twenty minutes while a delivery came through. As outside air gets
drier heading into fall, rooms that relied on ambient moisture in summer often need an active
source for the first time in months.

**Your turning schedule against your actual rind development,** not the calendar you wrote in
July. A wheel that was three weeks into a wash schedule when the season turned may need a
day or two of buffer either direction. Watch the rind, not the clock.

**Whether your rind culture is doing the work you think it is.** Biopreservation cultures on
a washed or natural rind are living populations, and a swing in room conditions changes how
fast they establish relative to whatever else is floating around your cave. Newer
biopreservation approaches on the market this year are aimed at exactly this
inconsistency — giving a protective culture a better foothold regardless of small swings in
the room — which tells you the industry considers this a real problem, not just a small-cave
one.

**Whether your record-keeping survives the transition.** The wheels going into the cave now
will come out well into winter, by which point the conditions that shaped their first weeks
will be a memory unless you wrote them down. A simple daily log of temperature, humidity, and
any door-open events pays for itself the day something comes out wrong and you need to work
backward.

None of this requires new equipment for most small operations — it requires paying closer
attention for about a month while the room finds its new baseline. If you want a structured
walk-through rather than figuring it out solo, the course above runs through exactly this
transition, and our Education Committee can point you toward the next scheduled cohort if the
current one has already filled.
