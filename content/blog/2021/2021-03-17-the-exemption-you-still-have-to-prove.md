Small-producer exemptions under federal food-safety rules get talked about as if they're a status you either have or don't. In practice, several members have told us this spring, an exemption is closer to a claim you have to be ready to back up, with sales records and a clear sense of which threshold you're under, than a box you check once and forget.

The federation has heard from a handful of small creameries this month who assumed their exemption was settled once and permanent. It isn't. Thresholds are evaluated on a rolling basis, tied to average annual sales over a multi-year window, and a good year can move a small producer across a line they'd stopped watching. The Food Safety Committee has fielded enough of these questions that we think it's worth restating plainly: if you're relying on an exemption, keep the sales documentation that supports it in the same file as your other food-safety records, updated at the same cadence.

## Why this isn't just paperwork

It's tempting to treat exemption status as an administrative detail, separate from the actual work of making safe cheese. Members who've been through an inspection tell us that's the wrong way to think about it. An inspector who shows up expecting to verify an exemption and finds no supporting documentation doesn't just note a gap — they may start asking questions about the rest of your record-keeping, on the theory that if this file is thin, others might be too. The exemption claim is often the first thing reviewed, which makes it a bad place to be unprepared.

This is a case where the record you'd want to keep for your own sake — a running log of what you sold and to whom — happens to be exactly the record a regulator wants to see, in close to the format they want it in. Members who've built that habit for their own planning purposes report that the exemption conversation during an inspection is short, because there's nothing to reconstruct on the spot.

It's worth being plain about one more thing: an exemption from routine inspection is not the same as an exemption from liability. If something goes wrong with a batch and a customer gets sick, an exempt small producer can still face the same legal and financial exposure as a fully inspected plant — the exemption changes who checks your paperwork in advance, not who can hold you responsible after the fact. Members carrying only the coverage that came bundled with a farm policy have told us they were surprised to learn how thin that protection actually was once they looked closely. A conversation with an insurance broker who understands food production, alongside the exemption paperwork, is cheap compared to the alternative.

Some of our most active members in this space are the ones who've testified at hearings on raw-milk and interstate shipment rules over the years, and they'll tell you the same thing from the advocacy side: regulators are generally more sympathetic to small producers who can show they take food safety seriously on their own terms, exemption or not. A thin file undercuts that credibility even when nothing has gone wrong.

## What we're doing about it

The federation's Food Safety & HACCP Certificate program now includes a short module specifically on small-producer exemption thresholds and how to document them, added after enough members flagged the gap. It's not a substitute for reading the underlying rule yourself, but it gives a plain-language walkthrough of what the sales-threshold math actually requires, where the common documentation mistakes show up, and what a reasonable liability-insurance conversation looks like for an operation your size.

If you're not sure whether your operation still qualifies for the exemption you've been claiming, or you've grown past a threshold without updating your file, the Food Safety Committee can point you to the certificate module and to members who've recently been through the recalculation themselves. It's a better time to check than during an inspector's visit.
