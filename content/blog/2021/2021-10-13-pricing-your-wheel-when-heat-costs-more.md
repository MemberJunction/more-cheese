Monday's post covered the heating bill. This one is about what to do with it once it's on your desk. Members finishing our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course this fall have been running a version of the same exercise: a price sheet built back in the spring, before this winter's propane and natural gas numbers were known, tested against what it actually costs to hold a wheel through a full aging cycle right now.

The instinct for a lot of small creameries is to absorb the increase and revisit pricing next year. That instinct is understandable and, for a cost this size, usually wrong. An aging room's energy draw isn't a rounding error against your other inputs — for a washed-rind or a long-aged wheel sitting in conditioned space for months, energy can be a meaningful share of what that wheel actually costs you to produce, and a cost that's risen by double digits deserves to show up in what you charge for it.

## Separating the wheel's real carrying cost from its milk cost

The course's core exercise is deceptively simple: take your existing price sheet and split it into what the milk costs, what the labor costs, and what the room costs — the last one being everything you spend keeping the cave at the temperature and humidity a given style needs for as long as it needs it. Most members have never separated that third number out before. Once it's isolated, it's easy to see which items on your list are most exposed to this fall's energy jump: a two-week fresh cheese barely notices a heating bill, a twelve-month wheel notices quite a lot. If your price sheet charges every style the same margin regardless of how long it sits in the cave, this is the year that assumption starts costing you specifically on your longest-aged products.

## What to actually change, and how to tell a buyer

The course doesn't tell members to raise every price by the same percentage — that flattens the very difference the exercise is meant to reveal. Instead, work out a carrying cost per wheel per week of aging, apply it to each style's actual time in the cave, and let the increase land where the cost actually falls. For most small producers that means a modest adjustment on quick-turn items and a real one on anything held past ninety days. It also means the adjustment should happen on a schedule you control — at your next price sheet update, not mid-invoice with a wholesale account that's expecting a number they already agreed to.

That's the arithmetic. The harder conversation isn't with your own spreadsheet, it's with the buyer who's used to last spring's number. The course spends real time on this because it's where members most often flinch and hold a price too long out of not wanting an awkward call. The framing that's worked for other members isn't an apology — it's a plain statement of what changed and why: your carrying cost went up by a specific, defensible amount, on a specific set of styles, for a reason the buyer can look up themselves if they want to. Buyers who've been paying attention to their own utility bills tend to find that easier to accept than a vague "costs are up everywhere," because it's a number instead of a mood.

Timing the conversation matters too. Raising the price on an order that's already been quoted or half-shipped reads as a scramble, and it can look worse to a wholesale account than the actual dollar amount does. Members who've handled this well this fall gave notice at the start of a pricing period rather than mid-cycle, gave the buyer the same carrying-cost breakdown they'd worked out for themselves, and treated the long-aged styles differently from the fresh ones in the same conversation — which matches the buyer's own sense that a twelve-month wheel and a two-week one are not the same product to carry.

If you haven't run this exercise since before this fall's energy numbers landed, the next Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese cohort is a good place to do it properly, worksheet in hand rather than in your head. Members who've been through it this year say the number that surprised them wasn't the size of the increase — it was how long they'd been pricing their longest-aged wheels as if the room they mature in were free.
