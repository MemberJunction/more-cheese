Shoppers love the idea of a cheese board and hate building one. That gap is exactly where a lot of members' fourth-quarter sales are coming from this year, and it's worth understanding why before you set your own counter for November.

Cheese has had a good year at retail generally, and a lot of that growth is coming from people trading up rather than buying more. What's changed is how they want it handed to them. A shopper who used to buy three wedges and improvise is now more likely to buy a pre-composed board or a kit that tells them exactly what to pair and how much to cut. If your case is still organized purely by milk type or country of origin, you may be making a confident shopper's job easy and an anxious one's job hard — and this time of year, the anxious ones outnumber the confident ones.

## What the counter course is teaching this fall

The current run of Counter Culture: Retailing has spent real time on exactly this problem, and a few pieces of it are worth passing along even if you're not enrolled:

**Compose in threes, with a reason for each choice.** A hard aged cheese, a bloomy or washed rind, and something blue or funky covers the range most shoppers are looking for without overwhelming them. Write the reason on the card: "goes with the sparkling wine you already bought" does more work than "nutty and buttery."

**Price the board, not the pieces.** A composed board at a clear price point sells faster than the same three wedges sitting separately with three tags a shopper has to add up in their head. You're not discounting anything — you're removing a decision.

**Staff a sample plate near the case on weekends.** Members running small tasting stations report it's the single biggest driver of board sales this quarter, more than signage or price. People buy what they've already tasted.

## The part that isn't about cheese at all

None of this works if your supply is unpredictable. A board recipe that calls for a specific wedge from a specific producer falls apart the week that producer is backordered. Members who've had the smoothest fourth quarter so far are the ones who built boards around categories — "a firm alpine-style," "a soft-ripened" — rather than single SKUs, so a substitution doesn't mean redesigning the whole card.

If storytelling is more your gap than logistics, Storytelling & Provenance in Marketing runs a parallel track on writing the kind of pairing notes and provenance copy that actually gets read at the counter, rather than skimmed past. Both courses are open for enrollment now, and either one is a reasonable use of a slow Tuesday before the season gets busy.

The same season is also when the international competition calendar peaks, and members who've entered wheels into judging in past years tell us the timing works in their favor at the counter. A ribbon or a strong showing gives you a specific, credible story to put on a board card or a shelf talker, and it's a different kind of claim than the vague "award-winning" language shoppers have learned to tune out. If you entered anything this cycle, now is the moment to use the result, not file it away.

Before you lock in your holiday board lineup, walk your own case the way a first-time shopper would. Can they tell, at a glance, what a board is for — a gift, a party, a quiet Tuesday at home? If every board looks the same regardless of occasion, you're asking the shopper to do work your signage should be doing. A ten-minute walkthrough this week can save you a slower December than you'd like.
