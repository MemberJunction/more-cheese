Two members called the office this week with the same chart and opposite worries. One had
seen cheese-based Class III pricing come off its spring highs and wanted to know if it was
time to hold back on a wholesale order. The other had seen the export numbers — dairy
shipments abroad running well ahead of last year's pace — and wanted to know if it was time
to expand. Both were reading the same market. Neither was wrong.

Milk production has been climbing all spring, up mid-single digits over last year by some
counts, and more milk on the ground tends to soften the cheese-based pricing benchmarks that
set the floor under a lot of wholesale contracts. That's the piece our first caller was
feeling. At the same time, export demand for U.S. dairy has stayed strong enough to absorb a
good share of that extra volume, which is the piece our second caller was watching. Both
things are true at once, and that's most of why this spring's cheese case has felt harder to
read than usual.

## What this means for your pricing

If your contracts reference Class III as a benchmark, expect some softness through the
summer unless production growth slows. That's not a reason to panic — it's a reason to know
which of your customers are on index pricing and which are on a fixed sheet, and to have that
conversation before your next delivery, not after. If you sell direct or through a
distributor relationship without a formal index, you have more room to hold your price where
it is; just be ready to explain why if a buyer brings you last month's mailbox price.

Export strength is the more interesting long-term signal for smaller producers, even if it
doesn't show up on your invoice directly. A market that's absorbing extra domestic volume
overseas is a market with less downward pressure on you next quarter. If you've been curious
about export documentation and haven't had the bandwidth to look into it, this is a
reasonable season to start — our **Export Documentation Essentials** course walks through
what a first shipment actually requires, from health certificates to the paperwork your
freight forwarder will ask for twice.

There's a smaller, more practical wrinkle underneath both numbers: butter and dry whey have
actually posted stronger gains than cheese this spring, which is unusual enough to be worth
noting if you make anything besides a straight table cheese. A creamery selling both fluid
whey byproduct and finished wheels may find the byproduct side of the ledger looking healthier
than the cheese side for a stretch, and that's worth factoring into how you price a
whey-cream skim or a butter run this summer rather than treating it as a footnote to the
cheese number.

## Where to take this next

We don't publish a house price forecast — that's not our job, and anyone who tells a
cheesemaker with confidence what next month's mailbox price will be is guessing along with
everyone else. What we can do is keep flagging the two threads: production growth on one
side, export demand on the other. Watch both, and if your contract language only mentions
one of them, that's worth a second read before you sign the next one.
