For most of this year, national milk production has been running comfortably ahead of last
year's pace — up close to three percent through the first seven months, by most counts. That's
the kind of number that keeps cheese cases full and buyers a little relaxed about supply. Over
the past few weeks, though, members in several regions have been telling us the growth has
cooled off, and the federal production reports members forward to us back that up. It isn't a
dramatic drop. It's a slowdown, and slowdowns move markets in their own quiet way.

Herd size hasn't shrunk, and per-cow output hasn't collapsed — this reads more like the ordinary
late-summer plateau than anything structural. Heat has played a part in some regions, feed costs
have played a part in others, and a milk price that spent much of the spring lower than producers
wanted has made a few marginal decisions for people who were already thinking about culling.
Whatever the mix of causes locally, the net effect nationally is the same: a little less milk
showing up at the plant than the spring numbers would have predicted. The most recent national
weekly dairy market report we've seen put the federal order uniform price a little below the
previous month but still slightly ahead of last year, and margin-support coverage for
August firmed up from where it sat earlier in the summer as milk prices ticked up and feed
costs eased off their highs — a small signal, but one pointing the same direction as what
members are telling us.

## What that does to the case

Less milk on a slower-growing base tends to firm prices, and that's the pattern several members
selling into wholesale channels have described over the last two weeks — cheese moving a bit
easier, buyers a bit less willing to wait for a better number. If you've had a wholesale account
go quiet on negotiating and just place the order instead, this is probably why. It's a welcome
change after a spring where a lot of members were pricing defensively, but it's worth remembering
that a slowdown in production growth is not the same thing as a shortage, and prices that move up
on thinner supply can move back just as fast if a few large operations decide to push output in
response.

For members setting a fall price sheet, the useful move right now is the boring one: check your
own cost basis against where your prices actually sit, rather than chasing the market number
itself. A firmer wholesale tone is good news, but your margin lives in the gap between your input
costs and your price, not in the headline direction of Class III. If you haven't revisited your
price sheet since spring, this is a reasonable week to do it.

## Where this could go by winter

Nobody on our side is forecasting a shortage, and we'd be cautious of anyone who tells you they
know exactly where the fall number lands. What we can say is that the two things worth watching
are feed costs, which have stayed elevated enough to matter for anyone still deciding on herd
size, and how much of this slowdown is seasonal versus a genuine shift in producer behavior. The
Membership & Outreach Committee has been collecting informal notes from members on regional
conditions all summer; if you've got a read on your own area that doesn't match what we've
described here, we'd like to hear it — that kind of ground-level detail is exactly what a
national number can't tell us.
