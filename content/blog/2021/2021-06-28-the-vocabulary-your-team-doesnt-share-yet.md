A member called us last month with a small, specific complaint: her assistant kept
flagging a batch as "fine" right before she caught a flaw the assistant hadn't noticed
at all. Not a training gap in the usual sense — the assistant had a good palate. The
problem was that "fine" meant something different to each of them, and neither had ever
had to define it out loud. That gap is exactly what Sensory Foundations, one of our
shorter and less-requested courses, is built to close.

## Tasting is not the hard part

Most people who work around cheese all day already taste well. What they haven't
necessarily built is a shared vocabulary — a set of words that mean the same specific
thing to everyone on a team, so that "sharp" or "grassy" or "a little flat" carries the
same information whether it comes from the head cheesemaker or the newest hire on the
line. Sensory Foundations spends less time training your palate and more time training
your language: structured tasting sequences, a defect glossary members actually use on
the floor, and practice describing a cheese in terms specific enough that someone who
wasn't in the room could act on the note.

That distinction matters most in exactly the situation our member described. A team of
two or three, all with reasonable palates, can still miss things simply because nobody
agreed in advance on what a specific word is supposed to flag. "Fine" is not a
tasting note. "Slightly bitter on the finish, sharper than yesterday's batch" is one,
and it's the kind of sentence the course drills until it becomes automatic rather than
effortful.

## Where this pays off first

The most immediate return we hear about isn't in the tasting room at all — it's in
handoffs. A production log that says "batch 14 tasted a little off" tells the next
shift almost nothing useful. A log that names a specific defect, at a specific stage,
in language the whole team has practiced together, turns a vague worry into something
someone can actually act on before the wheel goes any further. For small operations
running with one or two people wearing every hat, that shared shorthand does a lot of
the work a formal quality department would otherwise have to do.

It also sets a foundation, deliberately, for members who go on toward the Sensory
Evaluation Certificate or a spot on a competition judging panel later. You don't need
those ambitions to get value out of the course — most members who take it are simply
trying to keep their own team consistent — but if you do have them, this is the
starting point rather than a detour from it.

The next cohort is forming now. If your team has ever disagreed about what "off"
means, or you've caught yourself translating one taster's notes into another's
vocabulary before you can act on them, this is a low-cost way to close that gap before
it costs you a batch. Reach out to the Education Committee for the current schedule.
