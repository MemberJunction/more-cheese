Class III settled in the mid-teens per hundredweight this month, and the federation has fielded more questions about that single number in the last two weeks than about anything else in the pricing world. That's understandable. It's also, on its own, not the number that tells you the most about where your year is headed.

The more useful number sits one step downstream: the margin between that milk price and what feed costs. A firmer Class III print is good news for a producer selling raw milk into the pool, but for the cheesemakers among our membership who are buying milk rather than milking cows, a rising benchmark shows up as a cost line before it shows up as anything else. If you've locked in supply contracts that reprice off Class III, this is the month to reread the contract language rather than assume last year's terms carried forward.

## What this means for a small operation

For members enrolled in the federal margin coverage program, the relevant question isn't "did the milk price go up," it's "did the margin between milk and feed widen or narrow." A rising milk price paired with flat feed costs widens the margin and reduces the odds of a support payment triggering — which is good for producers overall, but worth knowing before you set your own wholesale pricing for spring. Several members who make and sell have told us they were caught off guard last year by a margin that moved differently than the milk price alone suggested, because they were watching the wrong side of the ledger.

If you sell finished cheese rather than raw milk, the practical move is smaller than it sounds: check your own cost sheet against this month's benchmark before you quote spring wholesale pricing, rather than after. A one-point move in Class III doesn't always translate cleanly into your own cost of goods, and a few members have found their aging cheese contracts were priced against an assumption from last autumn that no longer held. It's a five-minute exercise that saves an uncomfortable renegotiation in June.

## A quieter number in the same report

Cheese stocks in cold storage came in a little lighter than the same point last year, which is one of the reasons the price has firmed rather than stalled. That's a market-wide figure, not a member-specific one, but it's worth knowing if you're deciding whether to hold inventory into a firmer market or move it now. Neither choice is obviously right; it depends on your own storage costs and how confident you are in the direction of the next report. A cave that's already full changes that math in a way a spreadsheet alone won't capture.

It would be easier, frankly, to just repeat the topline figure every month and move on. We keep pushing members toward their own cost sheets instead because the federation has seen how differently the same market month lands across our membership. A producer selling primarily wholesale into grocery accounts experiences a firming Class III very differently than a member running a farm stand or a weekend market table, where retail pricing tends to move on its own schedule regardless of what the federal order is doing. We've heard from a few newer members this spring who are still building out that kind of direct-sales side of their business, and for them the monthly milk-price release matters less day to day than it does for a larger wholesale operation — which is a good reminder that "the market" isn't one thing for everyone in this federation.

We'll keep tracking the monthly releases and flagging anything that looks like it changes the picture for small and mid-sized producers specifically, rather than just restating the topline number. If you want to talk through what a given month's report means for your own operation, the Membership & Outreach Committee can point you to a member who's walked through the same math recently.
