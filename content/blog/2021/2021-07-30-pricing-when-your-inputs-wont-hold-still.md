Specialty cheese has had a genuinely good run this year. Members selling into the specialty case have told us demand held up better than they expected, and categories like aged and flavored wedges have kept moving even as grocery budgets tightened elsewhere. That's the encouraging half of this week's post. The other half is that a good sales year doesn't automatically mean a good margin year, especially if your cost base has been climbing under you the whole time.

That tension is exactly what members have been bringing to Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese this cohort, and it's worth walking through the part of the course that applies most directly right now: building a price from your actual cost structure instead of last year's price plus a guess.

## Start from the make sheet, not the shelf

The course's core exercise has you build a per-wheel cost from the ground up: milk cost per hundredweight, cultures and coagulant, labor hours at the vat and in the cave, packaging, and a loss factor for trim and aging shrink. Most members who've been pricing off last year's number are surprised how much that total has moved once feed-driven milk costs and a tighter hay market get plugged back in. A wheel that penciled out fine in January can be quietly underpriced by midsummer if nothing about the price sheet has changed since then.

The fix the course teaches isn't a bigger flat increase across the board. It's rebuilding the per-wheel cost on a schedule — monthly is reasonable for anyone buying milk on an index, quarterly at minimum for everyone else — so a price change is a calculation you can point to, not a number you're hoping the buyer doesn't push back on. That distinction matters more than usual this year, given how many wholesale conversations are happening over video where a buyer can be pulling up a competitor's price in the same window you're talking in. If you caught Wednesday's post on the wholesale negotiation webinar, this is the homework that makes that conversation easier: you can't hold a price with confidence if you're not sure how you got there.

## Margin isn't the same question as demand

It's tempting to read strong specialty sales as permission to leave pricing alone, but the course is deliberately built to separate those two questions. Demand tells you whether people want the cheese. Margin tells you whether selling it is worth what it costs you right now, this month, at this feed price. A category having a good year doesn't insulate any one producer from a cost base that's moved since the last time they looked at it.

The next Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese cohort opens for enrollment shortly; members who complete it walk away with their own working cost template, not just the theory. If you make and sell your own cheese and haven't rebuilt your price sheet since before this year's feed costs took off, this is a reasonable week to put it on the list.
