Most of our member spotlights feature someone who's been in the trade for a decade or more. This
one is about a member who's been at it for less than two years, because the last twelve months
have been a genuinely strange time to be starting out.

She's a second-year apprentice at a small farmstead creamery in the upper Midwest, and until last
spring her entire education came from standing next to a head cheesemaker who'd been making the
same washed-rind style for eighteen years. Then the plant went to a skeleton crew, the head maker
started working staggered shifts to keep distance in a small make room, and the informal
mentorship that usually happens over a vat simply stopped happening as often. "I went from asking
a question every ten minutes to maybe getting fifteen minutes with him a day," she told us. "I had
to figure out where else that knowledge was going to come from."

## Four courses, one screen, a lot of pausing and rewinding

Where it came from, in her case, was three federation courses in eight months — Rind Development
& Washing, Allergen Management, and a Sensory Foundations cohort — plus a stack of recorded
webinars she worked through on her own schedule after close. She's quick to say the courses didn't
replace standing next to an experienced maker, but they filled a specific gap: the parts of the
craft that are usually explained once, quickly, in passing, and easy to miss if you're not looking
for them.

"The rind course was the one that actually changed what I do every day," she said. "I'd been
brushing on instinct, copying what I'd watched. Having someone walk through why you adjust the
frequency when the humidity swings, and actually show the mold progression on camera, week by
week — that's not something I would've picked up just by watching from across the room. I could
pause it and go check my own wheels against what I was looking at."

The format wasn't effortless. She described the first course as "a lot of pausing and rewinding,"
and said it took real discipline to work through course material after a full shift rather than
just going home. What made it stick, she said, was a habit of testing each week's material against
her own cave the same day she watched it — not letting the gap between lesson and practice get any
wider than it had to.

## What she'd tell a new apprentice this year

Her advice for members in a similar spot: don't try to replace your mentor with a course, replace
the specific gap your mentor doesn't have time to fill right now. "He still teaches me more in
five minutes than I'll get from any video," she said. "But five minutes a day wasn't enough on its
own this year, and the courses gave me somewhere else to put the questions I used to just save up
for him."

If you're an apprentice or a supervisor navigating a similarly thin mentorship year, the Education
Committee maintains a short list of course sequences built for exactly this — pairing a hands-on
apprenticeship with the specific technical modules that tend to fall through the cracks when
in-person time gets scarce.
