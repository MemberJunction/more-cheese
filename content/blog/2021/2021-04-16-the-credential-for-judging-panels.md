Registration opens this month for the Judging & Competition Preparation course, and every year it
brings in a wave of questions about the credential that sits behind it: Competition Judge
Accreditation. It's one of our smaller certificates by enrollment, and it works differently from
the others, so it's worth explaining what it's actually for.

## What it requires, and what it doesn't

Competition Judge Accreditation isn't a starting point — it's a next step. You need the Sensory
Evaluation Certificate first, which covers the vocabulary and scoring discipline judging depends
on, and then supervised experience on an actual panel before the accreditation is issued. There's
no written exam beyond that. The credential exists to certify that you can sit next to an
experienced judge, apply a shared scoring standard consistently, and defend a score out loud when
another judge disagrees with you — which, on most panels, happens more often than newcomers
expect.

Panels in our federation's competitions typically pair judges with two different jobs: one judge
scores against a checklist of technical flaws — cracked paste, uneven rind development, off
aromas — working down from a perfect score, while a second judge scores up from zero for qualities
that stand out in a positive way. Both perspectives get weighed together. New judges sometimes
assume the technical role is the "easier" one to train into, but members who've done both told us
the aesthetic side is actually harder to score consistently, because it asks you to separate what
you personally like from what the style is supposed to taste like.

## The part that surprises people: it lapses fast

Unlike most of our certificates, which run on a multi-year validity clock regardless of what
you do with them, Competition Judge Accreditation lapses quickly if you go a season or two without
actually judging. That's deliberate. The whole point of the credential is that your palate is
current on this year's styles and this year's flaws, and a judge who hasn't sat on a panel in three
years is being asked to score cheeses against a standard they haven't recently exercised. If
you've earned the accreditation before and haven't judged recently, check with the Awards &
Competition Committee before you assume it's still active.

The Judging & Competition Preparation course opens its next cohort next month, and it's built
specifically as the supervised-panel step for members who already hold the Sensory Evaluation
Certificate. It runs as a shorter, more concentrated format than most of our courses — closer to a
guided practicum than a lecture series — because the skill it's teaching is repetition under
feedback, not new material to memorize.

If judging has ever interested you, this is a reasonable year to try it: our flagship competition
is still virtual, which means panels are running in smaller, more frequent sessions rather than one
marathon weekend, and several judges have told us that format is actually easier to train into as
a first-timer. Contact the Awards & Competition Committee if you'd like to sit in as an observer
before you commit to a seat.
