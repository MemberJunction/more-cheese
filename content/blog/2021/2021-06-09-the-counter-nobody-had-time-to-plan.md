A lot of the cheese counters open right now were assembled under pressure — a curbside table
that became a permanent case, a farm stand fridge pressed into year-round service, a shop
floor rearranged three times for distancing before anyone had a chance to plan it properly.
That's not a criticism. It's just what the last year asked of people. But we've heard from
enough members lately that their counter "just kind of happened" that it seemed worth
revisiting the basics.

**Building a Cheese Counter** is one of our steadier course offerings, and this spring's
cohort filled faster than usual, mostly with members who already run a counter and want to
go back and fix what they built on the fly. The course covers case layout and traffic flow,
which sounds obvious until you watch a customer get stuck behind someone deciding on a
cheddar; portioning and shrink, which matters more than most new counter operators expect;
and label and signage requirements, which have a way of drifting out of compliance when
nobody's had time to check them since last spring.

Some of the pressure on counters right now is simply volume. Specialty perishables — the
cheese, deli, and bakery items sold by weight rather than barcode — have been one of the
faster-growing corners of the specialty food business this year, and cheese counters are
feeling that growth as foot traffic, not just as a nice sales chart. A case that was adequate
for last year's customer count can feel undersized fast when more people are stopping at the
counter and staying longer to ask questions. That's a good problem, but it's still a problem
if your layout wasn't built for it.

## The three things members ask about first

Temperature logging comes up in almost every cohort. If your case swaps between a curbside
setup and an indoor one, or if you added a second small fridge to handle overflow, your
logging routine needs to cover both — a case that reads fine at 9am and drifts by 2pm is a
food-safety problem before it's a quality one.

Signage is the second. A counter built in a hurry often ends up with handwritten cards that
never got updated when a supplier changed or a wheel got swapped for a similar one. That's a
small thing until a customer with an allergy takes your sign at its word.

Staffing the case is the third, and it's more of a training question than a layout one. A
counter person who can talk through three cheeses confidently sells more than a counter with
a better case and a nervous clerk. The course spends real time on this, not because it's
glamorous, but because members tell us it's the piece that actually moves revenue.
