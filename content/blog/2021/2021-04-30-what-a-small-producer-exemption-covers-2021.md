We've spent part of this month sorting through member letters on small-producer exemptions, and the same misunderstanding keeps coming up. A qualified exemption based on sales volume is not a food-safety exemption. It changes which paperwork you file and how a regulator verifies your practices. It does not change what your cave, your make room, or your aging schedule actually have to do to be safe. Several of the letters we've helped members send this spring make exactly that distinction, and it's worth restating here for anyone still confused about what they'd be signing up for.

The advocacy work our members have been doing centers on raw-milk aging rules and the federal small-producer exemption thresholds together, because the two interact in ways that aren't obvious from either rule on its own. A creamery small enough to qualify for an exemption from certain preventive-controls paperwork is not automatically exempt from the aging period a raw-milk cheese has to meet before sale. Members running under that exemption have told us the paperwork relief is real and helpful, but it has occasionally led newer producers to assume it covers more ground than it does — and a mistaken assumption about aging requirements is a much more expensive mistake than a paperwork gap.

Part of the confusion is structural rather than anyone's fault. Compliance dates for different pieces of federal food-safety rulemaking have rolled out on a staggered schedule over several years, with different thresholds and different start dates depending on farm size and product category. A producer who qualified for one exemption two years ago may find their obligations changed without anything about their own operation changing, simply because a compliance date they hadn't been tracking finally arrived. That's a reasonable thing to lose track of if you're running a two-person creamery, and it's exactly the kind of gap our members keep flagging in their letters.

## What our members are asking for

The federation's position, reflected in the letter campaign several committee volunteers have been circulating, is not that the exemption thresholds are wrong. It's that the guidance explaining what the exemption does and doesn't cover is too thin for a producer without a food-safety background to parse on their own. We've asked for a plain-language explainer to sit alongside the regulatory text — something a two-person creamery can read in ten minutes and come away knowing exactly which obligations still apply to them, including the aging-period requirement that doesn't go away just because the paperwork burden does.

If you've signed a petition or letter through the federation this spring, thank you — the volume of member responses is part of what makes this kind of request land with a regulator's staff rather than get filed. If you haven't and you operate under a small-producer exemption, we'd still like to hear from you directly, because the strongest evidence we can offer is a specific example of guidance that left you unsure what to do next. A short note describing exactly where you got stuck is more useful to us than a general complaint that the rules are confusing.

## Where this goes next

This is a slow-moving comment period, not a vote next week, so don't expect a quick resolution. Rulemaking on this timeline typically moves in months, not weeks, and the federal calendar for the next round of compliance milestones hasn't been finalized as far as we've been able to confirm. What we can promise is that we'll keep members updated as the guidance moves, and that the Food Safety Committee is the right place to route a specific compliance question in the meantime rather than guessing at what your exemption does and doesn't require. If your own aging schedule or record-keeping has never been reviewed against the current thresholds, that's a reasonable thing to put on this month's to-do list regardless of how the comment period resolves.
