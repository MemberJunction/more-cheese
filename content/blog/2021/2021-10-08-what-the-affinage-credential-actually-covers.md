We get more questions about the Advanced Affinage Certificate than any other credential we offer, and most of them boil down to the same thing: is this for someone running a cave already, or someone who wants to? The honest answer is both, but it assumes you have hands-on time logged before you start.

The credential builds on our Affinage Fundamentals coursework rather than replacing it. If you have not taken a foundations-level course — moisture management, rind development, the basics of cave environment — start there first. The advanced certificate assumes you already know how to read a wheel and spends its time on the harder judgment calls: recovering a wheel that has drifted off course, managing a mixed cave with several styles competing for the same humidity, and documenting your reasoning well enough that someone else could pick up where you left off.

## What the coursework actually asks of you

Unlike some of our shorter workshops, this one is built around a running log. You track a set of wheels over multiple weeks — moisture readings, rind observations, any interventions you make — and your final assessment is built from that log rather than a single exam. Instructors have told us the members who struggle most are not the ones with less experience; they are the ones who skip the documentation habit and try to reconstruct it from memory at the end.

This fall's cohort is running the same as our earlier virtual sessions have: live video check-ins with an instructor reviewing photos and readings from your own cave, rather than a shared classroom cave the way our in-person workshops used to run. Several members who took it skeptically last year told us afterward that reviewing someone else's log alongside their own turned out to be more useful than watching a demonstration would have been — you see the mistakes as well as the successes.

## Who it tends to suit

It is a good fit if you are already the person other staff ask when a wheel looks off, and you want the credential to back up judgment you have already built through trial and error. It is a poor fit if you are hoping it will substitute for time in a cave — there is no shortcut around the log, and no version of the assessment that skips it.

If you are weighing whether to enroll this cycle, the Education Committee can walk you through whether your current experience lines up with where the coursework starts. Foundations-level members interested in the pathway should ask about the next Affinage Fundamentals cohort before enrollment closes for the season.

A few misconceptions are worth clearing up before you enroll. The most common misconception is that the credential is primarily about identifying defects. It is really about intervention — knowing what to actually do about a wheel that has started to drift, and knowing when the right call is to leave it alone. Instructors have told us that members with strong diagnostic instincts sometimes struggle here at first, because naming a problem correctly is not the same skill as deciding whether, and how, to act on it.

The second misconception is about time commitment. Because the coursework runs over several weeks rather than a single intensive session, some members assume it is lighter than a weekend workshop. In practice it asks for a small amount of disciplined attention nearly every day — a few minutes of readings and notes — rather than a large block of hours all at once. Members who try to compress it into weekly catch-up sessions tend to find their logs thinner and their final assessment harder than it needed to be.

Finally, several members have asked whether the credential expires or needs to be renewed. It does not expire on its own, but we do recommend refreshing your log habits periodically, particularly if you go through a stretch without much active affinage work. A credential earned three years ago on wheels you no longer make is less useful to a hiring manager, or to you, than one backed by a log you kept up over time.

If any of this sounds like more structure than you are used to, that is by design — the format is built to produce a habit, not just a certificate.
