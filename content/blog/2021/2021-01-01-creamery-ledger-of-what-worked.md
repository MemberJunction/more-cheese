Most years, a small creamery's New Year's ritual is the same as anyone else's: clean the books,
set a modest goal, get back to the vats. This member did that too, but she also did something we
have not seen from many others — she kept an actual notebook, dated and numbered, of every change
she made to how the business ran this year, whether it worked or not.

It started, she told us, out of necessity rather than discipline. In April, with wholesale
accounts disappearing and a walk-in cooler filling up faster than the case emptied, she began
writing down what she tried each week so she would not repeat a bad idea twice. By December the
notebook had become something closer to a playbook.

## What made the list

The changes that stuck were not dramatic. She moved her farm stand's hours earlier, because
customers who used to stop after work started shopping before it instead. She started offering a
smaller weekly cheese box alongside her regular wholesale wheels, aimed at the households who
wanted something between "one wedge" and "a whole subscription." She dropped a slow-moving washed
rind from her regular rotation for the winter and replaced the case space with a faster-turning
young tomme, then wrote down the exact week she planned to bring the washed rind back once the
counter traffic recovered.

Not everything in the notebook is a success. She tried a Friday evening pickup window in the
fall, hoping to catch people on their way home, and it never drew more than two or three cars.
She crossed it off by November rather than let a bad idea quietly linger on the schedule out of
inertia. That willingness to kill something that was not working, and to say so plainly, is
rarer than it should be — a lot of small operations keep a failing program running for a full
year out of politeness to the idea.

## The part worth borrowing

What we would ask any member to take from this, heading into a new production year, is not the
specific tactics — her market, her customer base, and her cave are hers, not a template. It is
the habit of writing the change down the week you make it, with a date, rather than trying to
reconstruct in December what you actually did in April. Memory is generous to itself. A dated
line in a notebook is not.

If you are looking for a more structured version of the same habit, our Pricing & Margin for
Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly this kind of week-over-week tracking, applied to
your actual cost per wheel rather than a gut feeling about how the year went. It is not a
substitute for the notebook. It is closer to what the notebook becomes once you start comparing
years against each other instead of just weeks.

We do not know yet whether this year will look anything like the last one. Neither does she. But
she is starting it with a clearer record of what actually happened than most of us will manage,
and that seemed like the right note to open a new year on.
