A year of direct-to-consumer shipping turned a niche question into a live one for a lot of our members: what are you actually allowed to ship across a state line, and under whose rules? The honest answer, right now, is that it depends which two states are involved, and the patchwork was built for a much smaller volume of shipments than most creameries are now sending.

The Membership & Outreach Committee has heard this from enough members in the last quarter that we're treating it as a standing advocacy item, not a one-off question.

## Why this got harder, not easier, this year

Before 2020, interstate cheese shipment was mostly a wholesale question — a distributor's truck, a licensed route, paperwork somebody else handled. Direct-to-consumer shipping moves the compliance burden onto the producer, and the rules that apply were mostly written with that wholesale picture in mind. A raw-milk aged cheese that clears federal standards for interstate shipment can still run into a state-level restriction on the receiving end that has nothing to do with the cheese itself and everything to do with how that state treats out-of-state raw-milk products generally. A policy brief circulating among state agriculture staffers this winter made the same point in plainer terms: raw-milk product rules vary enough state to state that a producer can be fully compliant at home and still be in a gray area the moment a package crosses a border.

One small farmstead producer who shipped direct for the first time last spring told us the hardest part wasn't the paperwork — it was finding out which paperwork applied before, not after, a customer's order bounced. "I built my whole online store before I checked whether my own aged wheels were even legal to send to three of the states people were ordering from," she said. She's since built a checklist she's shared with her regional group, which is more use to other members than most of our official guidance had managed to be.

Our position, going into any comment period on this, is not that shipment rules should disappear — food-safety standards on interstate shipment exist for real reasons. What we're asking for is a single, clear reference point producers can check before they build a shipping page, rather than the current situation, where the honest answer to "can I ship this cheese to that state" is "call a lawyer or guess."

## How to help

If you shipped cheese across a state line in the last twelve months — successfully or not — we want a short account of it: which states, what got flagged if anything did, how long it took to sort out. These specifics are what make a comment letter land instead of reading like a general complaint. You can send yours through the member portal under Advocacy, or route it through your regional committee contact if that's easier.

If this is the first you're hearing that a rule like this might apply to you, that's useful information too — it tells us how far the awareness gap actually runs, which is its own kind of evidence, and it's exactly the sort of thing we want on file before any comment period on this moves further.
