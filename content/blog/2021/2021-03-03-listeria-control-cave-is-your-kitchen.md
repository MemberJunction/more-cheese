A year into remote coursework and skeleton-crew production schedules, we're hearing from a lot of members who wrote their environmental monitoring plan for a busier kitchen than the one they're actually running right now. Fewer staff, fewer batches, and — for some of you — a make room that's also doubling as packing, shipping, and occasionally the only warm space in the building. None of that makes Listeria control less important. If anything, a smaller, more improvised footprint is exactly where cross-contamination risk hides, because the boundaries between "clean" and "dirty" zones get blurred when one room is doing three jobs.

A surveillance report published this week noted that Listeria infections had ticked up across European monitoring data even as several other foodborne illnesses declined — a reminder that this isn't a pathogen that goes quiet just because everything else around it has slowed down. Our *Listeria Control in Small Creameries* course exists for this specific situation, and this cohort's enrollment has been some of the strongest we've seen since the course launched. The material doesn't assume a dedicated food-safety hire or a facility built to spec — it's aimed at the make-room-and-a-half operation where the person swabbing the drain is also the person who made this morning's batch and is also, quite possibly, packing yesterday's orders in the same hour.

## Three things worth checking this week, even if you're not enrolled

**Your drain and floor swab map is only useful if it matches your actual layout.** If you moved equipment, added a second sink, or started using a corner of the room for aging that used to be for packaging, your swab sites should have moved with it. A map drawn up before your operation changed shape is describing a kitchen that may not exist anymore, and testing the wrong corner gives you a false sense of confidence rather than useful information.

**Wet, low-traffic corners deserve more attention than they usually get, not less.** A humid environment with reduced foot traffic is exactly the profile Listeria persists in — the organism doesn't need volume, it needs a stable, damp niche and time to establish itself. If your cleaning schedule assumed a busier room, check whether "less used" areas are actually getting cleaned less, and whether that's actually the opposite of what your risk profile calls for.

**Environmental testing during a slow season is not optional.** It's tempting to scale monitoring down when you're making less cheese, but a resident environmental strain doesn't care how many batches you ran this month. If your testing frequency dropped along with your production volume, that's a gap the course spends real time on, including how to make the case internally for keeping a testing budget steady even when output isn't.

A related habit worth adopting even outside the course: separate your "makes cheese" hours from your "packs and ships cheese" hours as clearly as your space allows, even if that just means a different apron and a hand-wash between the two. It won't fix a genuinely undersized facility, but it closes one of the more common gaps we hear about on member calls.

## Where to go from here

If any of that sounds familiar, the course is a manageable weekly commitment and runs entirely online — no travel, no dedicating a full day away from the room. It counts toward the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate for members working through that credential, and several members have told us the environmental monitoring module alone was worth the enrollment.
