A quarterly legal briefing on federal dairy regulation held this spring was a good reminder of how slowly this machinery actually turns, running through a full quarter's worth of price-support, trade, and regulatory developments in a single hour. One long-pending cheese standard-of-identity change — whether a filtered-milk ingredient can be used in standardized cheese without triggering a labeling change — was first proposed back in 2005. It is still not final, having been through more than one further round of public comment since. A few members have asked us why a fairly narrow technical question has taken sixteen years and counting to settle, especially after one of our trading partners notified a brand-new national cheese standard this spring on a timeline measured in months, not decades.

The honest answer is that this is closer to the normal pace than the exception. A standard of identity, once set, tends to stay put for a generation, which is exactly why it's worth taking years to get one changed correctly rather than rushed — but it also means members shouldn't expect a quick resolution to anything filed through the same process, including ours.

That's the backdrop for two asks our Membership & Outreach and Standards Committees have kept open since last year: a clearer federal labeling standard for aged and washed-rind cheese categories, and a review of the raw-milk aging rule that sets the minimum hold time before a raw-milk cheese can be sold. Neither has moved this spring. Both are still open for member comment through the committees, and both are still worth your time even when the calendar makes it feel like nothing is happening.

## A member's view from the testimony chair

Few of our members have spent as many hours in front of a state committee microphone as one longtime farmstead owner from our raw-milk contingent, who has logged dozens of petition signatures, letters, and testimony appearances on aging and labeling rules over the years. His advice to newer members watching this cycle drag on: "You don't testify because you think it'll change the vote that week. You testify so the record has a farmer's name on it when someone finally reads it two years later." That's not an especially satisfying answer if you want results by Friday, but it matches what we've seen — the rules that eventually move are usually the ones with the longest paper trail of member input behind them.

If you make raw-milk cheese under the current 60-day aging rule, your practical exposure hasn't changed this week, and it isn't going to change quickly. That's not a reason to stop submitting comments and testimony when the committees ask — it's the reason the committees keep asking. A rule this old rarely gets revisited because of one good argument; it gets revisited because a decade of arguments accumulated somewhere a regulator eventually had to read them.

## What to do this month

If you want your name on that record, the Membership & Outreach Committee can walk you through how to file a comment tied to either the labeling-standards ask or the aging-rule ask, and can tell you which docket each one actually belongs to so your comment lands somewhere it will be read rather than somewhere it will be filed and forgotten.

It's a slower kind of advocacy than a single headline vote, and it rarely produces a moment worth putting in a newsletter. But it's the kind that has actually moved rules like this one before, and the committees keep a running list of exactly where each ask stands so you're not starting from zero if you've never filed a comment before. Bring a question to the next committee call, or send it in writing if a Tuesday afternoon doesn't work for your schedule — either way, it gets logged the same.
