Some rulemakings move in a straight line. This one hasn't. A federal proposal that would change how certain milk ingredients can be described on a cheese label has been open, in one form or another, for years without a final rule landing. It's the kind of slow-moving process that's easy for a small operation to lose track of, and exactly the kind the federation tries to keep an eye on so members don't have to check a federal docket themselves.

## What a standard of identity actually means

A standard of identity is a federal definition of what a product has to contain, and in what proportions, before it can legally use a given name. For cheese, that framework governs things like moisture content, milk type, and aging behind names like cheddar, mozzarella, and blue. Industry groups that track this area regularly point out that these definitions were mostly written decades ago and can lag behind new processing methods, which is part of why proposals to loosen or clarify them keep coming back around instead of resolving once and for all.

The rulemaking currently in motion is narrower than a rewrite of those core definitions — it mostly concerns how a specific milk ingredient can be used and described in standardized cheeses — but it runs through the same regulatory mechanism that would govern any broader change to a cheese name's definition later. For most members this has stayed background noise for years, something a food-law newsletter mentions and everyone moves past. It becomes very much foreground the moment a distributor's compliance team or a state inspector flags a label because the product doesn't fully match the name it's sold under.

## Why a small producer should still care

A larger operation can afford a food-law consultant who tracks every open docket as a matter of course. A two-person creamery usually can't, and often finds out about a change only when it surfaces during a distributor's compliance review, sometimes long after a rule has already taken effect. That gap is exactly why the federation keeps this docket on our watch list and flags the parts that touch smaller operations specifically, even during long stretches when nothing appears to be moving.

You don't need to draft anything today. Check whether your product's name or label touches milk-ingredient sourcing or composition in any way; if you're not sure, the Standards Committee can help you read the current docket language against your own spec sheet. When the rule does move toward a final version, a short comment describing your actual process will carry more weight than a form letter, because regulators are explicitly interested in how a rule lands on small-batch and farmstead production, not only large-scale manufacturing. We'll flag the next comment window the moment it opens, and the Standards Committee can add you to the notification list for this specific docket in the meantime.

None of this is the loudest story in dairy trade coverage right now, and that is precisely why it is easy to miss. A quiet docket doesn't stay quiet forever, and the members best positioned when it finally moves will be the ones who spent a few minutes now checking whether their own label is anywhere near the line. That's a smaller ask than it sounds — most members can answer it in the time it takes to reread their own ingredient statement.
