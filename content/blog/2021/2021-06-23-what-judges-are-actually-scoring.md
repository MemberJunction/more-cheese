A question we hear every entry season, usually from a member entering for the first time: what exactly are the judges looking at? It's a fair question, because a competition scorecard doesn't look anything like the checklist a home cook or even a working cheesemaker builds in their head. Judging & Competition Preparation, one of our standing course offerings, spends most of its time on exactly this gap.

The short version is that judges score a wheel against the standard for its own category, not against every other cheese in the room. A soft-ripened entry and an alpine-styles entry are never compared to each other; each gets scored against what a well-made example of that specific style should be. That distinction trips up a lot of first-time entrants, who assume "best cheese" is a single ladder everyone's climbing. It isn't. A technically flawless washed rind and a technically flawless blue-veined wheel can both take top marks in their categories in the same competition, and neither one "beat" the other.

## The categories judges actually work through

A scorecard typically moves through appearance, texture, aroma, and flavor, in roughly that order, and most first-time entrants underweight the first two. Appearance isn't about whether the cheese looks appetizing in a general sense — it's whether the rind, the paste, and the eye structure (if the style calls for eyes) match what's expected for that category. A cheddar with an uneven paste color reads as a make or storage inconsistency before a judge has tasted anything. Texture gets evaluated on the cut and on the mouthfeel separately, and a wheel that photographs beautifully can still lose points on a chalky or crumbly interior that a photo won't show.

Aroma and flavor come last, and this is where the course spends real time on vocabulary, because "good" and "bad" don't tell a judge — or you — anything useful. The coursework pushes entrants toward specific, repeatable language: is a washed-rind cheese's aroma barnyard-funky in a way that's appropriate to style, or is it a sign the wash schedule ran too long? Is a bitter note in an aged cheddar a natural product of extended aging, or a sign of an off culture balance? Judges are trained to separate "this isn't what I personally like" from "this deviates from the standard for the category," and the course teaches entrants to read their own cheese the same way before it ever leaves the creamery.

## Preparing an entry, not just making one

The practical half of the course is about the entry itself, separate from the cheese inside it. Wheels arrive damaged, underweight, or improperly labeled more often than members expect, and any of those problems can cost points or disqualify an entry before a judge tastes it. The course walks through packaging that survives shipping, labeling that matches the category description exactly, and timing your make so the wheel is at its intended point of maturity — not underdeveloped, not past its peak — on the day it's judged.

If you've been holding off on entering because you're not sure your cheese is "competition-ready," that's often exactly the wrong instinct. Judges are evaluating a specific style standard, not an abstract notion of excellence, and a well-executed, true-to-style wheel beats an ambitious one that doesn't quite hit its category. The course is open to any member regardless of experience, and past cohorts have included both first-time entrants and members preparing for our Competition Judge Accreditation credential themselves.
