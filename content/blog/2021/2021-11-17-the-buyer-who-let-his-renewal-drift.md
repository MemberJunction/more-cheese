Marcus Chen joined the federation last year, in the middle of a stretch when most of what we offered had moved to a screen. He's a specialty cheese buyer, the person who decides which wheels make it into a retail case and which don't, and when his membership came up for renewal this fall it sat unrenewed for a few weeks. Not lapsed — he hadn't turned off auto-renew so much as not gotten around to confirming it — just drifting in the kind of grace window a lot of members have passed through this year without quite noticing.

We bring this up not to single him out but because his account of what changed his mind is useful for anyone managing membership at their own organization, or wondering whether the reminder emails they're sending actually work.

## What the reminder emails didn't do

"I saw them," Marcus told us. "I didn't open them, because I already knew what was in them." A renewal notice that says nothing but *your membership is expiring* competes for attention with everything else in a buyer's inbox during a season when case space is the whole job. It wasn't until a course notification landed — a seat opening in a cohort of Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese — that he actually logged back into his account, and at that point renewing was one extra click before he could register.

That's a small thing, but it lines up with a pattern industry-wide membership research has documented this year: associations surveying their own churn have found that a lack of engagement, not a lack of perceived value, is the single biggest reason members don't renew — and the fix that's worked best isn't a better renewal letter, it's making sure members actually see what they're already entitled to before the notice arrives. Value shows up before renewal does. A membership that's mostly a line item doesn't get defended when the calendar is full. A membership attached to something a person is actively trying to get into — a course seat, a webinar registration, a discounted competition entry — gets renewed almost as an afterthought.

Marcus joined during a stretch when most of what the federation offered had already shifted to a screen — virtual sessions in place of a conference floor, a course catalog instead of a workshop tent. He says that made the membership easier to justify at the time, since a specialty buyer can watch a pricing webinar between calls in a way that's harder to do with a three-day trip. But it also made it easier to forget he'd joined at all once the initial reason for signing up — a specific course he wanted — was behind him. The renewal window arrived at a moment when nothing new was pulling him back in, and it sat.

## What this means if your own renewal is sitting there

If you've got a renewal notice you've been meaning to get to, the practical fix is the same one that worked for Marcus: don't try to force yourself to care about the renewal in the abstract. Go register for something you actually want — a course, next month's Judging Standards webinar, a committee call — and let the system remind you the two are connected. It usually is enough.

For anyone reading this from the other side of the desk — a committee lead, a volunteer coordinator, anyone watching your own roster for members who've gone quiet — the lesson generalizes past cheese buyers. A quiet member isn't necessarily gone. They're often just waiting for a reason to log back in that isn't the renewal notice itself, and this year in particular, with so much of our own programming still running virtual, that reason is usually one click away rather than a plane ticket away.

Marcus is registered for the next round of Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese, and his membership is current through next fall. If you're the one holding a renewal notice right now, our suggestion is the same one that worked for him: don't wait to feel motivated about the renewal in the abstract. Go register for whatever you were actually curious about, and let the system connect the two for you.
