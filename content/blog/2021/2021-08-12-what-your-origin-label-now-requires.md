A new federal rule on "Made in USA" labeling takes effect this week, and it's one of the few
recent regulatory changes we'd tell every member to read, not just the ones who ship across state
lines. The rule tightens what an unqualified Made in USA claim can mean on a label, a package
insert, or a website, and it gives the federal trade regulator the ability to seek real civil
penalties for violations for the first time. That's a meaningful shift from a rule that, until
now, was enforced mostly through warning letters.

The standard itself isn't new in substance — final assembly or processing has to happen in the
United States, and all or virtually all ingredients or components have to be made and sourced
domestically before you can put an unqualified claim on your label. What's new is how seriously
it's now enforced, and how broadly "label" is defined. It covers packaging, but it also covers
your website, your mail-order catalog, and anything else that carries the claim to a buyer.

## Where this catches small producers off guard

Most of our members aren't making a deliberate false claim; they're making an assumption that
hasn't been tested against this stricter standard. If your cheese is made from milk you produce
yourself, on equipment made domestically, with cultures and rennet sourced from wherever your
supplier happens to source them, you may not have all or virtually all of your inputs covered —
and a casual "made in the USA" sticker on your label could now be more exposure than it's
worth. This isn't a reason to panic. It's a reason to actually trace your inputs once, write down
what you find, and either qualify the claim honestly or drop it if it doesn't hold up.

A qualified claim — something like "Made in the USA with imported cultures" — is still allowed
and still carries real weight with buyers who care about provenance. Several members who sell
direct at farmers' markets have told us their customers respond just as well to an honest,
specific claim as to a blanket one, sometimes better, because it reads as more credible.

## What we're doing and what you can do

We've asked the Standards Committee to put together a plain-language checklist members can use to
audit their own labels against the new rule, and we expect to have it ready for the fall.
In the meantime, if your packaging carries any origin claim at all, it's worth a look this month
rather than waiting for a complaint to prompt it. The rule doesn't require you to remove a claim
you can support — it just requires you to be able to support it.

If you're not sure whether your current label holds up, the Membership & Outreach Committee can
point you toward the questions worth asking your suppliers, though for anything that touches your
specific legal exposure, that's a conversation for your own counsel rather than for us.

The timing is worth noting too. This rule lands in the same month a lot of our farmstead members
are finalizing fall packaging runs, ordering new labels, or updating a website ahead of the
holiday order season. It's a lot easier to fix an origin claim before a print run than after one,
and a label reprint is a real cost most small operations would rather absorb once than twice. If
you're placing a label order in the next few weeks anyway, treat this as the natural moment to
have the origin conversation with your supplier rather than a separate project to schedule later.

We'd also rather hear from members proactively than react to a complaint after the fact. If
you've already done the work of tracing your inputs and you're comfortable with your current
claim, that's worth documenting too — a dated note on file showing you checked is far more useful
six months from now than trying to reconstruct your reasoning after the fact. Keep it simple: a
short list of your major inputs, where each one comes from, and the date you checked, is enough
to show good faith if the question ever comes up.
