A piece of industry research that crossed our desk this fall has stuck with us: artisan and specialty producers are, on average, making fewer distinct types of cheese than they were a few years back, after a long run of expanding their lineups. The same research pointed to something else worth noting going into a new year — a majority of small producers now sell directly off their own website, a channel that barely existed for most of them a decade ago.

Put those two trends together and you get a shift worth sitting with over a quiet holiday week. If your shop makes three or four cheeses instead of eight, and a growing share of what you sell goes out through your own site rather than a wholesale account or a farmers' market table, the case for a documented credential behind your name changes. You're not relying on a buyer walking your case and tasting your way to trust. You're relying on a product page and a photo, and a credential is one of the few things that travels with that page instead of requiring the customer to have met you.

## Where a credential actually earns its keep

The Certified Cheese Professional designation is the one most members ask about when they're thinking past their own kitchen — it signals broad competence across handling, storage, sensory evaluation, and food safety basics, which is exactly the range a smaller catalog needs to cover well since you can't lean on breadth to cover for a weak spot. If your business is narrowing rather than expanding, that's not a step down; it's a case for depth, and depth is what the credential is built to certify.

For members further along, the Advanced Sensory Evaluation coursework pairs well with a narrower lineup for a different reason: fewer cheeses means more scrutiny on each one, and being able to describe precisely what's changed in a wheel from batch to batch — not just "it tastes different" — is a skill worth formalizing rather than trusting to memory. A member with a four-cheese catalog and a credential to back it up is making a much stronger claim to a wholesale buyer or an online customer than a member with the same four cheeses and no third-party standard behind them.

There's a retail-side version of this too, worth mentioning since it's easy to overlook. The Certified Cheesemonger credential exists for the people selling the cheese rather than making it, and a smaller, more curated case is exactly the kind of thing a trained counter person can sell well — a shorter list means more time to actually talk through each one with a customer instead of rattling off a case of thirty options nobody can hold in their head.

## A quiet week is a good week to start

Holiday week is genuinely light on committee and course activity, which makes it an unusually good window to look at the certification pathway pages and figure out which one actually matches where your business is headed, not where it was three years ago. We hear from members every January who wish they'd started the paperwork in December instead of waiting for a slower month that never quite arrives.

If you're not sure which credential fits your catalog, that's a normal place to be — most members aren't sure either, and the pathway pages are written for someone who hasn't decided yet, not someone who's already committed. Reach out to the Education Committee with a short description of what you actually make and sell; that's a five-minute conversation, not a commitment, and it'll save you from starting the wrong track in January and having to switch in March.
