A comment period on interstate cheese shipment closes at the end of this month, and it's easy to let a notice like that slide past you in a week already full of virtual everything. We'd rather you didn't. This is one of the rulemakings where a stack of specific, well-written member letters has moved language before, and the topic is squarely one our members have testified on for years.

## What's actually being proposed

Strip away the legal phrasing and the proposal does three things. It clarifies which small-batch shipments across state lines qualify for a lighter compliance path versus a full interstate license. It sets new documentation requirements for temperature logging in transit — a real cost for anyone shipping soft or washed-rind cheese without a dedicated reefer account. And it asks, in a fairly open way, whether the current sales threshold for the lightened path is still the right number three years on.

That third question is the one worth writing about. If you ship a modest volume across state lines and the current threshold works for you, say so with a number. If it's too low and it's pushing you into paperwork disproportionate to your volume, say that too, with your own shipment count from last year if you have it handy. Specific numbers from working creameries carry more weight in a comment record than general objections, however well argued.

## The temperature-logging piece deserves a plain read

We're not going to tell you this requirement is nothing — for a shop shipping in coolers rather than a dedicated cold chain, a documented logging step is a real change to how you pack a box. What we can tell you is what the draft language actually requires: a logged reading at pickup and at delivery, not continuous monitoring, and it applies to a defined set of high-moisture products, not everything in a case. Read the product list before you assume it touches your line. Members who ship primarily aged, low-moisture cheeses may find this section doesn't apply to them at all.

Individual comment letters count, and our Membership & Outreach Committee has a plain-language explainer and a letter template up for members this week — request it through your regional contact if you haven't already. A letter doesn't need to be long. Three sentences that say what you ship, how you ship it now, and what this specific change would cost or save you is worth more than a page of general support.

If you'd rather talk it through first, our next open advocacy call is a good place to ask questions before you write anything down. We've had members join those calls undecided on a rule and leave with a clear paragraph for their letter — that's exactly what the call is for.

One more thing worth saying plainly: we don't have a position telling you what to write. Our job here is to make sure the rule is understood correctly and that your specific situation — not a generic industry position — makes it into the file. A one-size complaint doesn't move a comment period. A hundred specific ones sometimes do.
