Federal milk pricing data has shown a steady climb this spring, and it's the kind of movement worth pausing on if you sell wholesale. Class III — the classification most cheese milk falls under — moved from roughly $15.75 in February to $17.67 in April, with figures for May pointing higher still. Class IV has followed a similar path, up from around $13.19 to $15.42 over the same stretch. Federal outlook data covering the four weeks into early May showed the pattern up close: 40-pound blocks of cheddar rose about four cents a pound over that stretch, with butter, nonfat dry milk, and dry whey all firming at the same time.

The reasons behind it are mostly demand, not supply shock. Cheese purchases through a federal food-box program set to wind down at the end of this month have kept demand elevated, and as more of the country moved back toward in-person dining and schooling this spring, food-service cheese orders picked up alongside it. At the same time, milk production nationally has been running well ahead of last year — April volumes were up something like 3.5% year over year, with May tracking higher still. So the price strength isn't coming from tight supply. It's coming from buyers wanting more cheese than usual, at the same time production keeps growing.

## What that combination means for you

For members who set wholesale prices off a milk-cost formula, a rising Class III number raises your input cost, which is straightforward enough to pass through if your contracts allow it. The less straightforward part is the production side: with milk supply running ahead of demand growth in most other categories, a lot of that extra milk has nowhere obvious to go once cheese demand levels off. That's usually the setup for a softer summer once a program like this one expires, not a promise of one — but it's worth planning your pricing calendar with both directions in mind rather than assuming May's number holds through August.

If you haven't looked at your own formula in a while, our course on pricing and margin for specialty cheese walks through building a cost-plus structure that flexes with a Class III move like this one without you having to renegotiate every contract by hand. It's a useful thing to have in place before a swing, not after — several members who took the course last fall told us it saved them a round of awkward calls with distributors this spring.

## The retail side is a little different

Members selling direct — at a counter, a farmers market stand, or online — have more room to hold a price through a short-term swing, since you're not locked into a standing wholesale contract. A few members told us they're watching the food-box program's end date closely, since a drop in that channel's demand could show up as more cheese looking for a retail home later this summer. If that's a trend that matters to your business, it's worth keeping an eye on through June rather than reacting after the fact.

We'll keep tracking the federal pricing reports and pass along anything that changes the picture meaningfully. In the meantime, if you want a second set of eyes on your own pricing formula, the Membership & Outreach Committee can point you toward a member who's been through this exercise recently.
