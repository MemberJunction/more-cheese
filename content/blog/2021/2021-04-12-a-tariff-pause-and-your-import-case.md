A few members have written in this month with a version of the same question: why did the landed
cost on a couple of their European lines suddenly drop, with no notice from the supplier? The
short answer is a trade dispute that has nothing to do with cheese finally easing off of cheese.

## What actually changed

In early March, the United States and the European Union agreed to a four-month suspension of the
retaliatory tariffs tied to a long-running dispute over aircraft subsidies. That dispute had
nothing to do with dairy, but cheese was one of the goods caught in the tariff lists both sides
built years ago, alongside wine, spirits, and a handful of other specialty foods. For as long as
that tariff line was active, a case of French or Italian cheese crossing into the country carried
an extra duty that had nothing to do with the cheese itself.

That duty is paused for now, not repealed. The suspension runs a fixed number of months from when
it took effect, and nothing in the announcement guarantees what happens after that. If your import
mix leans on a supplier in the affected countries, the sensible move is to treat this quarter's
lower landed cost as a temporary window rather than a new baseline — price accordingly, but don't
rebuild your whole case plan around it.

For members who distribute rather than retail, this is also a reasonable moment to ask your
distributor directly which of your SKUs were even affected. Not every European import sat on the
tariff list, and "cheese" as a tariff category is narrower than most people assume — it can hinge
on moisture content, packaging format, or country of origin in ways that aren't obvious from a
shelf label.

It's tempting to read a story about import tariffs and assume it only matters to members who carry
imported wheels. It doesn't. A lower landed cost on competing imports changes the shelf math for
domestic producers too, especially in categories — washed-rind, alpine-style, aged sheep's milk —
where an imported and a domestic option often sit a few feet apart in the same case. If your
retail accounts start asking for a price adjustment on domestic product because "the import got
cheaper," that's this tariff pause working its way downstream, and it's worth having an answer
ready before the question lands.

None of this changes the underlying production economics — feed costs, labor, and the price of
milk itself are moving on their own schedule this spring, tariff dispute or not. What's changed is
one narrow input on one slice of the market, for a window that has an end date already attached to
it.

## What to do with this

If tariffs are more than background noise for your business — you import regularly, or you compete
hard against imports on a specific style — it's worth a few minutes with your distributor rep this
month to understand exactly which lines moved and by how much. The Membership & Outreach Committee
can also point you to members further along the import supply chain who've been through tariff
swings like this one before and can talk through how they planned around the last one.
