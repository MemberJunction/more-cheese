If your distributor has been quoting softer numbers than they did in November, you are not imagining it. Cheese and butter both went into the new year sitting on fuller shelves than a year earlier, and the price has moved the way inventory usually moves it: down. Members who watched the Class III number fall by more than seven dollars a hundredweight between November and December told us they felt it first in wholesale negotiations, not at the register.

We want to walk through what that means for a small or mid-sized producer heading into February, because "the market is soft" is not the same thing as "your cheese is worth less."

## Why the surplus built

Milk production kept climbing through the back half of last year even as some retail and foodservice demand stayed uneven, and more milk with steadier demand is exactly the recipe for stocks to build. Cheese inventories closed December well above where they sat twelve months prior, and butter was up even more sharply. None of that is a verdict on quality or on any one creamery's book of business — it is a commodity-market story, and commodity cheese prices move differently than the specialty case most of our members compete in.

That distinction matters more than usual right now. A softening bulk price can actually widen the gap between commodity block cheese and the aged, named, story-driven wheels many of you sell direct or through specialty accounts. If your price has been anchored too closely to the commodity index, this is a good week to loosen that anchor and reprice around your own cost structure instead.

## What to check before you touch a price sheet

Start with your milk cost, not the futures board. If you buy from a co-op or a neighboring dairy, ask what they are actually being paid this month rather than assuming it tracks the headline number — spot arrangements can lag or lead the index by weeks. Then look at your aging and packaging costs, which have not gotten cheaper just because the milk side softened.

We also want to flag the federal risk-management tools that are open for enrollment right now for producers who ship fluid milk: margin coverage programs that pay out when the gap between milk price and feed cost narrows past a set threshold. They will not help a make room buying already-processed curd, but if any of your supply comes from your own herd, it is worth ten minutes with your local farm service office before the sign-up period closes. Several members who enrolled last spring told us it took the edge off exactly this kind of price swing.

A quieter kind of good news sits underneath all of this.

One thing worth saying plainly: fuller cheese stocks are not a crisis. They are the market doing what it does when supply runs a step ahead of demand, and demand has been the harder half of that equation to forecast for almost a year now. Members selling direct-to-consumer, through farmers' markets, or into specialty retail have generally told us their own order books look steadier than the bulk numbers would suggest — which is exactly the case for pricing against your own costs and your own customer base rather than the commodity print.
