We've had more emails than usual this week about a specific problem: a cave that was perfectly
well-behaved in June and is now doing something strange in the middle of an August that's put a
large share of the country under an excessive heat advisory. Rind slip that wasn't there before,
condensation where there shouldn't be any, a humidity reading that climbs overnight and won't
come back down on its own. None of it is mysterious once you look at what's actually changed,
which is the air outside the cave, not the cheese inside it.

Heat and humidity together put more strain on refrigeration and dehumidification equipment than
either does alone. A compressor that keeps up fine on a dry ninety-degree day can fall behind on
a humid one, because it's fighting moisture load on top of temperature, and heat indexes over
much of the country have been running well above the actual air temperature this week. If your
cave has felt like it's "working harder" this summer without you changing anything about your
setup, that's not your imagination — it's the equipment running closer to its limit than it does
the rest of the year.

## What to check before you touch the wheels

Before you start second-guessing your affinage schedule, check the boring things first. Door
seals are the usual culprit — a seal that was fine in a cooler spring can let in enough humid air
in August to push your whole room's reading up several points every time the door opens. Condensate
drains clog more in humid months and back up quietly until you notice a puddle where there
shouldn't be one. And a room that's slightly overcrowded copes fine most of the year but stops
coping once the ambient load goes up; if you added inventory this summer ahead of fall orders,
that's worth factoring in before you blame the wheels themselves.

Once the equipment side checks out, it's worth looking at whether your turning and washing
schedule needs a seasonal adjustment. A rind that develops faster in higher ambient humidity may
need a slightly shorter interval between washes for a few weeks, not a permanent change to your
recipe. Members who keep a simple daily log — temperature, humidity, what they did that day —
tend to catch this kind of drift a week or two before it becomes a problem you can see on the
wheel itself, which is a lot easier to fix than a problem you can taste.

## Where to go deeper

Our Humidity & Airflow Control course walks through cave mechanics in more detail than we can
cover here, including how to size a dehumidifier for your actual room rather than a rule of
thumb pulled from a forum post, and it's a useful one to take before next summer rather than
during it. If you're in the middle of a rind problem right now and want a second set of eyes
sooner than a course schedule allows, the Food Safety Committee has fielded a lot of these calls
this year and is a good place to start. Bring your humidity log if you have one; it tends to cut
the diagnosis time in half.

And if your cave is holding steady through this stretch, that's worth noting too — it's useful
information about your setup that's easy to forget once the weather turns milder again.
