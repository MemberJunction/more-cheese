Two numbers crossed our desk last week and they don't tell the same story. The federal all-milk price moved up again in October, continuing a run that's put farm-gate prices well ahead of where they sat a year ago. Cheese export volumes are on pace to top 400,000 metric tons for the year, the first time that line has ever been crossed, with Mexico and Central America both buying more than they did in 2020. On paper, this is a good fall for dairy.

Ask a member who ships wholesale and you'll hear the other half of it. A recurring theme at last week's supply-chain briefing covering the dairy and broader food sector was how much of that farm-gate gain is getting absorbed before it ever reaches a shop shelf. Container availability is tight, ocean freight is expensive when you can get it at all, and the paperboard and film that go into a retail cheese package aren't arriving on the schedule anyone budgeted for in January. If you make cheese for export or for a distributor two time zones away, you already know this in your bones. If you sell mostly at the counter or the farmers market, the same pressure is showing up as a longer wait on the boxes and wrap you order every quarter.

## What this means for pricing your fall case

We've heard from more than one small producer this month who raised prices on a wholesale line for the first time in two or three years, not because the cheese changed but because the case it ships in did. That's a reasonable response, and it's worth doing with real numbers rather than a guess. Pull your last three packaging invoices and compare the per-unit cost to what you were paying in the spring. If it's moved more than 10 or 15 percent, your wholesale pricing probably needs to move too, even on a wheel that's been priced the same way for years.

The export numbers matter here as a signal, not a target. Most ICF members aren't shipping internationally, but the same freight and container constraints touching those exporters are touching the trucking capacity that gets a domestic pallet from a Midwest creamery to a coastal distributor. When larger shippers are paying more to move product, smaller producers competing for the same limited truck slots feel it too, usually as a longer lead time before a slower rate hike.

## Watching the winter ahead

Nothing in this data suggests the tightness eases before the holiday season winds down, and several members have told us they're already building a longer cushion into December orders than they normally would. If you haven't looked at your packaging costs since spring, now's the week to do it — before the holiday order comes in and you're pricing against numbers that are already out of date.

If you want a second set of eyes on a wholesale price sheet, the Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding these questions all month and can point you to a member who's already worked through the same math.
