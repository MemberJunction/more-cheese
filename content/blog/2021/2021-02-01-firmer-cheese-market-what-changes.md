Members who watch the federal milk price announcements closely have probably already noticed it: the class benchmarks tied to cheese firmed up through January, continuing a recovery from the rough stretch last spring. That's welcome news after twelve months when the word "unprecedented" got worn out. It's also a good moment to talk about what a firmer benchmark does and doesn't mean for your own numbers.

## What actually moved

A benchmark tied to cheese and dry whey firming up mostly reflects two things: cheese has been moving, and buyers are willing to pay more for it. Restaurant closures hit food-service volume hard for a long stretch, and a lot of that demand simply migrated to retail cases and to-go orders instead — a decent proxy for why a home cook reaching for a block of cheddar mattered more to the market this winter than it might have in a normal year. Specialty cheese in particular held up better than the broader category through last year, which tracks with what a lot of our retail members told us directly: cases that leaned into interesting, higher-margin wedges fared better than ones that competed purely on volume.

For a wholesale supplier or a producer selling into distribution, a rising benchmark can put a floor under contracts that felt shaky last spring. For a direct-to-consumer creamery, it mostly shows up secondhand, in what your milk supplier is charging or in a co-op check that finally looks less alarming.

Neither of those things means your own retail price should move in lockstep. Members ask us this every time the benchmark shifts, and the honest answer is the same: a benchmark is an input to your cost structure, not an instruction for your price tag.

## Building it into your own math

A few practical steps we've walked members through this winter:

- Pull your last two quarters of milk cost per pound of finished cheese and compare it to your case price. If the gap has closed since fall, you have room; if it hasn't, you have a conversation to have with your supplier or your own price list.
- Don't confuse a firmer *benchmark* with firmer *demand for your specific wheel*. A rising Class III number says something about commodity cheddar and mozzarella volumes nationally. It says very little about whether your washed-rind release is moving.
- If you sell wholesale, ask your buyer whether their own contract references the benchmark directly, or whether it's negotiated separately. A lot of smaller accounts do the latter, which means the benchmark move may not touch your check at all until the next renegotiation.
- Watch your packaging and freight costs alongside the milk number. A few members told us those lines moved more than milk did last quarter, and a firmer cheese market can mask a supply cost creeping up somewhere else in the ledger.

We know a fair number of members spent last spring in survival math rather than growth math. A market that's stopped falling isn't the same as one that's roaring back, but it's a better place to plan from. A firmer benchmark is a tailwind, not a plan — the creameries that came out of last year in the best shape were the ones that kept their own cost sheet current the whole time, rather than waiting for the market to tell them what to charge.

If you want a second set of eyes on your own cost sheet, the Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding these one-off questions all winter — reach out and we'll point you to a member who's done the exercise recently. It's a fifteen-minute conversation for most people, and it's usually the fifteen minutes that gets put off longest.

None of this is happening in a vacuum, either. Other membership organizations have kept their own programming running through the winter rather than waiting out the market — a reminder that a slower calendar doesn't have to mean a quiet one. We'd rather members spend a January reviewing numbers with us than putting that conversation off until spring.
