A proposed federal labeling rule is open for public comment this month, and it's one we're actively asking members to weigh in on rather than leave to the trade groups with more Washington staff than we have. The proposal would standardize how aging claims, moisture-content disclosures, and pasteurization status are presented on cheese packaging — three data points that currently vary a fair amount from state to state and, in practice, from producer to producer.

The intent behind the proposal is reasonable on its face: a shopper should be able to compare an aging claim on one label against another without needing to know that one producer rounds up to the nearest month and another doesn't. The draft language, though, leans heavily on formats built with larger commercial operations in mind — specific font-size minimums, a required disclosure box in a fixed layout, and testing documentation requirements that assume a lab relationship most small creameries don't have in-house.

## Where the draft creates real friction

Our Standards Committee has been reviewing the draft alongside members who make and label cheese themselves, and the friction points are consistent. A fixed disclosure box eats label space that a small producer often can't spare on a compact wheel or wedge, especially anyone hand-labeling in small batches rather than running a commercial print run. The testing documentation language, as written, would require moisture-content verification on a schedule that makes sense for a high-volume operation shipping thousands of units but is a real cost burden for a farmstead producer making a few hundred wheels a season.

None of that means the underlying goal is wrong. Clearer, more comparable labels are good for buyers and good for members whose careful aging practices currently don't show up clearly against a competitor's looser claims. The concern is narrower: a rule written around commercial-scale assumptions, without a small-producer allowance, could push some members toward vaguer labeling rather than more precise labeling, simply because the precise version costs more to document than a small operation can absorb.

The comment we're drafting asks for two specific changes: a scaled documentation standard tied to annual production volume, similar to exemptions that already exist elsewhere in food-safety rulemaking, and a smaller allowable disclosure format for packages under a certain size. Neither change weakens the disclosure itself — a shopper still gets the same information — it just changes how a small producer is allowed to prove and print it.

## How to weigh in

If you make and label cheese yourself, your comment carries more weight than ours does, because it comes from someone the rule would actually apply to rather than an association describing the effect secondhand. The federal comment period runs through the end of this month, and our advocacy page on the member portal has a short template you can adapt with your own specifics — what you currently disclose, what a fixed-format box would cost you in label space, and what documentation you can realistically produce on your own timeline. Specific examples from a real operation are what a regulator remembers out of a stack of form letters, so don't worry about sounding polished; describe what the rule would actually mean for your label.

If you want to talk through your comment before submitting it, or you're not sure whether the proposal would affect your current labeling at all, the Standards Committee is holding office hours this week specifically to help members work through it.
