If you sell overseas, you have probably felt this already: the order is placed, the wheels
are ready, and the container that is supposed to carry them out of port is somewhere else,
because it was more profitable for a carrier to send it back empty than to fill it with
cheese. Members in our export-facing segment have been telling us the same story all summer,
and it has only gotten louder heading into fall.

The irony is that demand has rarely looked better. The global dairy auction that cleared
earlier this month showed cheddar prices up again and the broader index sitting roughly a
third above where it was a year ago, an eight-year seasonal high by some measures. Cheese
shipments are doing real work in that strength, with buyers abroad still hungry for product
even as everything gets more expensive to move. That is the kind of
headline that should make a small creamery's export plan easier to write. Instead, several
members tell us they are quoting freight costs that have roughly doubled since last year,
booking space weeks further out than usual, and in a few cases holding finished product in
the cave longer than planned simply because there is nowhere for it to go yet.

The numbers back up what members are describing anecdotally. Container rates on some of the
routes our exporters use most have nearly doubled over the past year, and industry trackers
estimate container disruptions are now costing farm and food exporters several hundred
million dollars a month in lost sales, mostly because carriers find it more profitable to
send empty containers back to Asia for another round of imports than to fill them with
outbound agricultural cargo. A wheel of cheese competing for space against that logic is not
a fight most of our members can win on price alone.

## It isn't just the container

Packaging has its own version of the same squeeze. Pallet costs climbed sharply this year as
lumber prices spiked, and paper pulp — the stuff cheese boxes and case liners are made of —
is running well above what it cost a year ago. None of this is particular to cheese, but a
wheel of aged cheddar sitting an extra six weeks for a container is a different kind of
problem than a pallet of dry goods sitting the same six weeks. Time is an ingredient here,
and the current bottleneck is spending it for you.

Members who sell mostly at home are not fully insulated either. The same packaging inputs
that are getting expensive for an exporter's crate are getting expensive for a domestic
wholesale case, and a supplier squeeze anywhere in the chain tends to show up everywhere
eventually. If you have not yet asked your packaging supplier for a fall forecast rather than
a fall quote, that conversation is worth having now rather than in November.

We don't have a fix for global container flows — nobody does, and we'd be skeptical of anyone
who claimed to. What we can do is pass along what's working for members who are getting
product out anyway: booking further ahead than feels natural, building freight variance into
quotes rather than absorbing it, and talking to buyers early about delivery windows instead
of promising a date and hoping. A few members with domestic buyers have also told us this is
the year they finally built out that channel properly, simply because it removed one more
thing that could go wrong at the port.

For everything above, our Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding a steady stream
of these questions and is a reasonable place to start if you want to compare notes with other
exporters rather than guess alone.
