The federal milk pricing formula that sets minimum class prices is under review again, and the federation has been fielding calls from members who want to know whether it's worth writing in. Short answer: yes, and the window is open now. Federal order pricing rules get revisited periodically, and every round tends to be shaped most by the commenters who show up, which historically skews toward larger processors with dedicated policy staff. A comment from a five-person creamery reads differently to a hearing officer than a form letter from a trade desk, and it carries real weight precisely because it's rare.

Dairy pricing reform has been on our advocacy agenda for several cycles, and the sticking point for our members is almost always the same: pricing formulas built around commodity cheese and butter-powder economics don't map cleanly onto small-batch, aged, or mixed-milk operations. A formula tuned for high-volume plants running one product line can leave a farmstead maker's actual costs invisible in the math entirely. Add in a margin-compressed winter, with feed and packaging costs both up, and the gap between what the formula assumes and what a small creamery actually spends only gets wider.

## What the federation is asking members to weigh in on

Our Standards Committee has been drafting language for the current comment period that asks regulators to account for smaller processing volumes and multi-product runs when they model class price impacts, rather than assuming every operation looks like the plants the formula was originally built around. We're not asking anyone to oppose the safety net that's been paying out through this margin-compressed stretch. We're asking that the rules underneath it not quietly assume a scale that most of our members don't operate at.

One member who submitted comments in a past round told us the hardest part wasn't the writing — it was believing a small operation's comment would actually be read alongside the big filings. It was, and it was cited in that round's summary of public input. That's not a guarantee every comment gets quoted, but it is a guarantee that a small operation's math doesn't reach anyone's desk if nobody sends it.

## How to add your voice

You don't need a lawyer or a trade consultant to file a comment. A page describing your own production volume, your own milk cost, and how a specific provision would land on your operation is more persuasive than a long legal argument, because it's the thing regulators say they don't get enough of. The Standards Committee has a short template members can adapt rather than starting from a blank page, and we're glad to look at a draft before you submit it.

If you've never filed public comments before, this is a low-stakes place to start: no hearing, no travel, just a written submission with a real deadline. Keep it concrete — your own herd size, your own make schedule, the specific line in the formula that doesn't match your costs. Regulators read a lot of general objections and very few operation-specific ones, and it's the specific ones that tend to get referenced when a rule is finalized.

We'll keep members posted as the comment period moves toward a decision, and we'll flag any provision that looks like it's heading in a direction worth a second round of letters. Contact the Standards Committee for the template and the current filing deadline, and let us know if you'd like us to reference your comment, without naming your operation, in our own filing.
