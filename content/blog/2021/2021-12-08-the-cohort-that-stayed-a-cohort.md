This isn't the story of one member. It's a pattern several of you described to us this fall, closely enough that it deserves its own post rather than a single name attached to it — call her the assistant cheesemaker at a mid-sized creamery who signed up for a course this spring mostly because there wasn't much else to do.

She's not alone. Enrollment in our online coursework has run well ahead of where it sat before 2020, and one of the quieter effects of two years of virtual programming is that a course cohort now tends to keep existing after the course ends. Craft cheese training broadly has followed the same arc this year — even boot-camp-style programs built around a room and a table spent a stretch running entirely over video before anyone found their way back to an in-person format. A shared chat thread that started as "does anyone else's affinage room smell like this" turns into a standing Thursday call. Nobody planned it. It just didn't stop.

## What the cohort actually does for you

The formal reason to take a course is the credential at the end of it — a line on your make sheet, a certificate you can point to when a distributor asks who trained you. That part hasn't changed. What's changed is that the informal reason has caught up to it. A member on one of these standing cohort calls this fall told us the group had talked her out of scrapping a batch that looked worse than it was, and talked another member into finally calling her state inspector before a problem instead of after one.

That's not something a syllabus can put on a slide. It's closer to what an in-person conference hallway used to do for members who could afford the travel and the days away from the vat — except now it's available to someone running a two-person creamery who's never once had the staffing to leave town for four days.

## If you want one of your own

You don't build a cohort like this on purpose, but you can put yourself in the room where one starts. Course descriptions on our site list who else is enrolled by region and creamery size before the first session, which is worth five minutes of your time before you sign up. If you're deciding between two course dates for the same credential, ask the Education Committee which cohort still has an active group thread from a prior offering — some of ours are two years old and still meeting.

It also matters what the course actually covers, not just who's in the room. A cohort built around a food-safety credential tends to stay useful longer than one built around a single technique demonstration, because the questions members bring back to the group keep changing as their operations grow — a recordkeeping question in month three, a supplier audit question in month nine. If you're choosing a course specifically to build a lasting group rather than just to earn the credential, weight that toward the food-safety and business-side offerings over the narrowly technical ones.

None of this replaces the in-person conference we all miss. But for a member who's never had the travel budget for one, a Thursday call with five people who make cheese for a living might be the closest thing we can offer them right now, and it's turning out to be worth more than we expected when we first put these courses online. If your own cohort is still meeting a year or two after the fact, the Education Committee would like to hear about it — that's exactly the kind of retention story a course catalog never captures on its own.
