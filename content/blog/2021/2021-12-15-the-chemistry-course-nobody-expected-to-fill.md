Cheese Chemistry started its Prairie cohort on December 6, which means members are two weeks into a four-week run that ends right around New Year's, an odd time to ask people to sit through titratable acidity and casein structure on a screen. Nobody on our education team expected this cohort to fill. It filled anyway, and faster than a couple of our more crowd-pleasing courses this fall.

We think we know why, and it isn't nostalgia for a college lab. It's that the people signing up have spent close to two years doing the actual chemistry blind — adjusting a make on instinct because a supply hiccup changed their milk, or troubleshooting a rind problem over video with nobody in the room to smell it with them. A course that explains why pH drop behaves the way it does under a given starter culture isn't abstract to that group. It's the thing they wish they'd understood three make days ago, back when the answer wasn't a video call away.

## What the course actually covers

Cheese Chemistry is not a substitute for time at the vat, and we say that up front every time we run it. What it does cover, over its four weeks, is the underlying behavior most makers pick up secondhand rather than by name: how starter culture activity drives acid development, what's actually happening to casein and calcium during coagulation, why a moisture problem at week one shows up as a texture problem at week six, and how humidity and temperature interact inside a small aging room in ways that don't scale down neatly from commercial references. The format leans discussion-heavy rather than lecture-heavy, which has turned out to matter more online than it did when the room was physical. Members bring specific, sometimes embarrassing problems from their own operations, and the cohort works through them together instead of watching a demonstration happen to someone else's cheese.

That discussion format is also, frankly, the part we were least sure would translate to a screen. Cheese education elsewhere has leaned on shipping physical samples to participants so a virtual class can still taste and handle something real, and we've borrowed that idea for two of our other courses this year. Cheese Chemistry doesn't do that — there's no sample box, no kit — and we worried that would make it feel abstract in a way members wouldn't tolerate for four weeks. So far the opposite has happened. Without a shared physical object to focus on, the conversation stays on each member's own cave and vat, which several participants have told us is exactly what they wanted.

## Who this is actually for

If you're on the fence about whether this is the right course for where you are, the honest answer is that it rewards people who've already made cheese and hit a wall they couldn't explain, more than it rewards someone starting from zero. If that's not you yet, Sensory Foundations or Milk Quality & Herd Health are gentler starting points, and Cheese Chemistry will still be there once you have a few more make days behind you.

The Prairie cohort wraps the first week of January. We'll open the next cohort's registration in the new year, and if this run is any indication, it won't stay open long. If you want a seat, let your regional contact know now rather than waiting for the announcement to go out to the full membership.
