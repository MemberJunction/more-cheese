Earlier this month, the United States and the European Union agreed to pause, for four months, a set of retaliatory tariffs that had been sitting on European cheese and other goods since late 2019. The tariffs were never really about cheese — they came out of a long-running aircraft-subsidy dispute — but cheese got caught in it anyway, and a lot of members felt that in their landed cost on imported wheels.

We've fielded enough questions this week that it's worth laying out plainly who this helps and who it pinches, and what a separate piece of trade data from the same month means for members on the other side of the counter.

## Who this helps, and who it pinches

If your case carries European imports, or you distribute them, the pause is straightforward good news. Landed cost drops, at least for the length of the suspension, and buyers who'd quietly swapped an import line for a domestic substitute over the last year may come back around. If you're a retailer or a monger, this is a reasonable moment to check in with a distributor rep about whether a price you dropped in 2019 is worth re-listing.

If you're a domestic producer competing directly against one of the categories that got cheaper, the calculus runs the other way. A four-month pause is short, and it's explicitly framed as a negotiating window rather than a resolution, so we wouldn't recommend repricing your own product against it. Watch the case, don't chase it. The suspension is scheduled to run through early July, and whether it gets extended, made permanent, or allowed to lapse back into the original tariff schedule is a live question, one the Advocacy Committee is tracking through the summer. We'll flag any signal on that as soon as it's public, since a snap-back would hit differently depending on how much of your buying you'd shifted in the meantime.

## The bigger number underneath this

The tariff pause landed in the middle of a month that also set records on the export side: federal trade data for March showed U.S. dairy exports approaching their highest first-quarter value in years, cheese included. That's a separate story from the tariff pause, and a more durable one — it reflects steady demand growth in several overseas markets that predates this particular dispute and should outlast it. Members who export, even in small volume through a co-packer or a specialty distributor, are operating in a genuinely stronger market than a year ago.

Put the two facts together and the picture for spring is mixed but not bad: cheaper competition on the import side, for now, against a backdrop of real export growth. If your business touches both sides of that — buying some cheese, selling some cheese — the net effect is probably close to a wash, but it's worth running your own numbers rather than assuming it nets out for you.

In the short term, our advice is unglamorous: if you buy imports, get a written quote now while the window is open rather than waiting. If you compete against them, hold your price and your story — a four-month tariff pause doesn't change what's actually in your wheel.
