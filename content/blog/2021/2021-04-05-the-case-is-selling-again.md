A month ago we wrote about the market turning; this week we can say something more specific. Cheese demand is firming not just because winter restocking ran its course, but because the places that buy in volume — school cafeterias, catering kitchens, sit-down restaurants — are actually reopening. That's a different kind of recovery than a spreadsheet number bouncing around, and it's worth understanding before you plan your spring production.

The public-health backdrop matters here more than usual. Vaccination has been accelerating through March into April, with national coverage climbing week over week, and several states have begun easing capacity limits on indoor dining as a result. None of that is guidance we're qualified to give you — we're a cheese trade group, not a public-health authority — but it is context for why your phone might be ringing differently than it was in January.

## Foodservice is buying again, unevenly

Foodservice historically accounts for roughly half of all cheese and butter sales, and trade reporting over the past couple of weeks has described that channel as still below pre-pandemic levels but picking up noticeably through late winter into spring as dining restrictions ease. Members who sell into wholesale and foodservice channels are telling us orders are coming back, but not evenly across the country. A distributor account that went quiet for a school district still on remote learning isn't going to reopen until that district does, and some won't be back to a five-day in-person week until fall. If a chunk of your volume runs through one or two institutional accounts, it's worth a direct call to ask where they actually stand rather than assuming a national headline applies to your contract.

Export demand has also been a quiet source of strength this spring. Trade reporting has described international buyer interest ticking back up even as block cheese prices dipped and recovered over the past couple of weeks — a sign the export floor under the market is holding. If you're not an exporter yourself, this mostly matters because it firms up the broader block-and-barrel market that a lot of formula pricing is pegged to — one more reason the number on your milk check has been drifting up since February.

## What we'd actually do with this

Don't over-commit production to a recovery that's still patchy. A member who ramps a make schedule assuming foodservice snaps back to pre-2020 levels by summer could easily end up with more aged wheels than the case can move if a region's reopening stalls or reverses. Build in slack, especially if your inventory sits in a category — semi-hard, aged cheddar — where stocks were already elevated coming into this year.

On the flip side, if you've been holding back on booking farmers' market or direct-to-consumer slots because you assumed nobody was buying, this is a reasonable week to reconsider. Foot traffic at outdoor markets has been recovering faster than indoor foodservice in a lot of the reports we're seeing from members, and it doesn't carry the same all-or-nothing exposure as a single institutional contract.

If your pricing runs off a lagging formula and you want a second opinion on whether now is a good time to renegotiate terms with a distributor, our Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded a lot of these calls over the past year and is a reasonable first stop.
