Every few weeks a member emails us some version of the same question: is the Sensory Evaluation Certificate worth the time if I've already been tasting cheese professionally for years? The honest answer is that the credential was never built to teach you how to taste. It's built to teach you how to describe what you're already tasting in language a buyer, a judge, or a co-worker can act on.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. A cheesemaker who can say "a little sharp" knows something. A cheesemaker who can say "acetic sharpness building on the finish, likely a slow-drop pH batch" can troubleshoot the next make. The certificate spends most of its hours on that second kind of language: a shared vocabulary for texture, aroma, and finish that doesn't depend on two people happening to grow up eating the same cheeses.

## How the coursework is structured

The certificate runs through the Sensory Foundations course, offered this spring as part of the Alpine cohort, plus a set of guided tasting panels members complete on their own schedule. The panels are the part people underestimate. Each one asks you to score and describe a flight of samples, then compares your notes against a reference panel's — not to tell you that you're wrong, but to show you where your vocabulary and someone else's diverge on the same wheel.

For members running their own cave or counter, that comparison work has a practical payoff beyond the credential itself. If you're training a new hire to do intake tasting, or writing tasting notes for a retail case, consistent language is what makes those notes useful to the next person who reads them. A few members have told us the biggest change after finishing wasn't their palate — it was that their staff started agreeing with each other faster.

## Where it fits with the rest of the ladder

The Sensory Evaluation Certificate doesn't require the Cheese Foundations Certificate first, but most members find it easier after it; foundations gives you the make-process vocabulary that sensory work builds on. It's also a common step before pursuing Competition Judge Accreditation, since judging panels lean hard on the same shared-description skill. If judging is where you're headed eventually, this is the credential to knock out first.

Registration for the current guided-panel cycle is open through the education page, and the panels run asynchronously, so a rotating cave schedule or an irregular retail week doesn't lock you out. If you've been putting this one off because you assumed it was a beginner course, it isn't — it's closer to a shared language exam, and most members who've been tasting for years are the ones who get the most out of it.

Members who've finished the credential tend to mention the same surprise: the hardest samples weren't the strongest or strangest cheeses in the flight, they were the mild ones. It's easy to write three sentences about a pungent washed rind. It's much harder to find precise, useful language for a young cheese with a short, clean finish, and that's exactly the skill a busy retail case or a competition entry sheet actually needs. A flat "mild, creamy, nothing unusual" tells a buyer nothing they couldn't have guessed from the label.

That's also where the guided panels earn their keep. Working through a flight where three of five samples sit in a narrow band forces you to reach for texture, mouthfeel, and finish instead of falling back on intensity as a stand-in for description. Members doing intake tasting for a wholesale account have told us this is the single most useful habit the credential built, because most of what crosses their table in a given month is closer to that middle band than to either extreme.

If your caves or counters run mostly in that same middle territory, the credential is arguably more useful to you than to someone specializing in a handful of aggressive, easy-to-describe styles. It's a reminder that "unremarkable" is rarely the right word for a cheese — it usually just means nobody has taken the time to describe it well yet.
