The federal aging requirement for raw-milk cheese — the rule requiring at least 60 days
before a raw-milk wheel can be sold — is back under active review, and our Food Safety
Committee wants members to understand what's actually being asked before they sign
anything. This isn't a new rule. It's a question about whether the old one still does
what it was written to do, and the research behind it has moved a long way since the
number was first set.

The short version: 60 days of aging was set decades ago as a rough proxy for pathogen
die-off, and the regulator reviewing it has said plainly that the research since then is
mixed. Some pathogens decline steadily over that window. Others, under certain moisture
and salt conditions, decline much more slowly, and low pH may matter more than the
calendar does. A recent study looking specifically at avian flu in raw-milk cheese found
that aging alone did not reliably inactivate the pathogen, while a lower pH measurably
helped — which is exactly the kind of chemistry-over-calendar finding that's driving this
review. None of that is new to anyone who has spent a season watching a cave, checking pH
before release rather than counting days on a calendar. It's new that federal reviewers
are saying it out loud in a formal proceeding.

## What the federation is asking for

We are not asking members to take a position on whether 60 days is the right number —
reasonable cheesemakers disagree, and this isn't a debate we're going to settle on a
blog post. What we're asking is narrower: if the standard changes, it should change based
on a wheel's actual chemistry — pH, moisture, salt — rather than a single date that
applies the same way to a washed-rind and a hard grating cheese. Those are different
foods with different risk profiles, and a one-size aging clock has never quite fit both.
A rule built around measurable endpoints would also give members a clearer path to
demonstrate compliance on their own terms, instead of waiting out a fixed number of days
regardless of what the wheel is actually doing.

If your operation makes anything raw-milk and aged, this is worth ten minutes of your
week. The comment period isn't open indefinitely, and general public comment volume tends
to favor whichever side organizes first. A single line from a working cheesemaker —
what you actually measure before you release a wheel, and why — carries more weight in
this kind of proceeding than most of us expect. We've had members tell us after the fact
that they wished they'd written something, usually after reading a rule change they had
no hand in shaping.

## Where to send it

The Food Safety Committee has a short template members can adapt on the advocacy page,
along with a summary of what's changed since the last time this rule was reviewed. If
you've never submitted a comment before, that's the easiest place to start — it walks
through where the language actually goes and what regulators tend to skim versus read in
full. Keep it specific: a description of your own aging and testing practice does more
work than a general statement of support or opposition. If you make raw-milk cheese and
have opinions about what 60 days does or doesn't tell you, we'd rather you write them down
for the record than tell us about them at the next committee call.
