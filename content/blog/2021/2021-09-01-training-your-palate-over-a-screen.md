A year and a half ago, we would have told you sensory training needed a room. A group of
tasters, a shared plate of reference samples, someone walking the table pointing out what
"barny" or "chalky" actually tastes like next to what it isn't. It's hard to teach a vocabulary
you can't point at. The Sensory Foundations course has spent this year figuring out how to do
that pointing over a shared screen instead, and enough cohorts have now come through it that we
can say plainly: it works, with some adjustments.

The core method hasn't changed. Panel training starts with a "gold standard" sample and a
deliberately flawed one, tasted side by side, so the difference registers before the vocabulary
does. What's different online is that everyone needs their own physical samples in front of
them at the same time, which means the course now ships a coordinated tasting kit to each
participant ahead of the session rather than relying on one plate passed around a table.
It's more logistics on our end. Members who've taken it tell us it hasn't cost them anything in
what they actually learn.

## Why the vocabulary still matters

None of this is about developing a fussy palate for its own sake. A shared, precise vocabulary
is what lets a wholesale buyer, a competition judge, and your own quality check on the cave floor
mean the same thing when they say a wheel is "ammoniated" instead of just "off." Members who've
gone through sensory training tell us the biggest change isn't in how they taste — most working
cheesemakers already have a good palate — it's in how fast they can describe a problem to someone
else, whether that's a co-worker, a distributor, or us, when something needs troubleshooting.

The reference-standard approach — training against a known defect rather than just tasting a lot
of cheese and hoping the vocabulary sticks — also turns out to travel well over video. A
mailed defect sample is still the same defect sample whether you're smelling it in a shared
classroom or your own kitchen, and a handful of members in this year's cohorts have told us the
smaller group size on a video call actually made it easier to ask a basic question they might
have hesitated to ask in a room of twenty.

Running a course this way means the syllabus now has a shipping deadline built into it. Kits go
out roughly ten days ahead of the first live session so members have time to receive them,
refrigerate the samples properly, and let anything that traveled cold come back to a tasting
temperature before class starts. We learned that lesson the hard way in an earlier cohort, when
a delayed shipment meant part of one group tasted a reference sample a full two days later than
the rest — close enough that the course still worked, but far enough that we rebuilt the
shipping window around it. Members in rural areas with slower delivery windows should register
a little earlier than the deadline suggests, simply to leave room for a kit that takes longer to
arrive than the calendar assumes.

## Where to start

If you've never taken formal sensory training, our entry-level course is the starting point, and
it feeds directly into the Sensory Evaluation Certificate for members who want the credential on
their record. If you're already comfortable identifying common defects on your own product but
want to be able to describe them the way a judging panel would, that's the gap this course is
built to close. The next cohort opens for registration shortly — check the course calendar on
the member site, and if you've got a specific defect you've been struggling to name, bring a
sample to the first session. It's the fastest way to get an answer.
