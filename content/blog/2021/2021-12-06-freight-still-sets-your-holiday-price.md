Two things are true about the case you're stocking for the holidays this year, and members keep asking us which one matters more. Milk and cheese prices have been climbing for most of the fourth quarter — federal reporting this week put Class III and Class IV both well above where they sat in the spring, continuing a run of global dairy auction results that have been trending upward since late summer. And separately, the truck that was supposed to bring your order on Tuesday is running on Thursday, if it's running at all.

Both are real. Neither is going away by New Year's.

## What the price line is actually telling you

A firmer commodity price is, on its own, not bad news. It usually means demand for cheese held up better than a lot of us expected going into a second pandemic winter, and that a stronger blended milk price is finally working its way into your input costs in a way you can plan around instead of getting surprised by. If you sell wholesale, this is the argument for revisiting a contract you locked in back when the board was quieter. If you sell at the counter, it's the argument for a price tag conversation with your regulars now, before the January invoice does it for you.

The freight side is a different animal. Driver shortages, delayed pickups, and staffing gaps at processing plants aren't a price signal you can hedge against — they're a scheduling problem that shows up as an empty spot on your loading dock. Trade reporting this quarter has been blunt about it: labor shortages have kept some plants running below capacity all year, and freight complexity has become as much of a headache for sellers as it is for buyers. Several members told us this fall that a supplier they'd used for years simply couldn't confirm a delivery window anymore, and that the workaround wasn't a new supplier, it was carrying more inventory than they're used to carrying. That ties up cash and cave space at the exact moment you need both for holiday volume.

## What to actually do with six weeks left

If you haven't built a freight delay into your holiday order math, do it this week, not after a shipment misses. Order earlier than feels necessary, and build slack into whatever date you tell a wholesale buyer to expect their order — a week of padding costs you nothing if the truck shows up on time, and saves the relationship if it doesn't.

On the pricing side, don't wait for a supplier invoice to force your hand. If your cost basis has moved, your shelf price should move with it in a planned way rather than a scramble. A clear, calm explanation to a regular customer about why the wedge costs forty cents more than it did in October lands a lot better than silence followed by a surprise.

It's also worth saying plainly that a firmer market doesn't mean firmer margins for everyone at the same time. A creamery selling direct to consumers can pass along a cost increase in a single sign at the counter. A creamery locked into a wholesale contract signed back in the spring is stuck absorbing it until that contract comes up for renewal — which is exactly why several members have told us they're pushing for shorter contract terms next year, even if it means a little more negotiating overhead twice a year instead of once.

We'll keep tracking both threads — commodity price and freight reliability — through the rest of the virtual conference season. If your creamery has a freight story worth sharing, the Membership & Outreach Committee wants to hear it; member accounts of what's actually happening on the loading dock are more useful to us right now than another data table.
