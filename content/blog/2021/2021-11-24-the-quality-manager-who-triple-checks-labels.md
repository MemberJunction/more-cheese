We don't always name names in this column, and this week is one of those weeks — the member asked us to keep the creamery off the record while she's still finishing up holiday production, and we're happy to oblige. She's been a federation member for several years, and for most of that time she's been the quality manager at a small farmstead operation in the upper Midwest that leans hard into the holiday season the way a lot of small producers do. We caught up with her this week, in the thick of it, to ask what actually keeps her up at night between now and the end of the year.

Her answer wasn't the cave, and it wasn't the make schedule. It was the label copy.

"Everyone worries about the wheel," she told us. "I worry about the sticker on the wheel." At a small operation, the same handful of people who are aging cheese in the morning are packing gift boxes in the afternoon, and her job is to be the person who slows down when everyone else is moving fast. She checks every lot number against every batch record before it leaves the building, and she reads every ingredient statement out loud — not silently — because she says she catches more that way.

## What changed this year

This is her third holiday season doing the job, and she says the volume finally feels manageable in a way it didn't the first two years. Part of that is experience. Part of it, she told us, is that her creamery moved more of its holiday sales to pre-orders this fall, which spreads the packing work out instead of stacking it all into the last week of November. "We used to guess how much gift box inventory to build," she said. "Now people tell us, and we build to that."

She's also become the person on staff who fields origin-labeling questions from newer hires, walking them through what has to be on a package versus what's just nice to include. It's not glamorous work, and she'd be the first to say so. But she's the reason a mislabeled lot doesn't leave the building, and at a farmstead creamery, that's not a small thing during the busiest six weeks of the year. Freight has also been tighter this fall than she's used to, she added, which means a caught labeling error now costs more time to fix than it would have two years ago — there's less slack in the schedule to redo a run and still make a shipping window.

## What she'd tell a newer quality manager

Asked what she'd pass on to someone doing this job for the first time, she didn't hesitate: build your checklist before the rush starts, not during it. "You will not have time to think clearly in the second week of December," she said. "Whatever you were going to remember to check, write it down now." She also pointed newer members toward the federation's Food Safety & HACCP coursework as a good place to build that habit early, rather than learning it under deadline pressure the way she mostly did.

If you're heading into your own holiday crunch this week, her approach is a fair model: slow down exactly where everyone else speeds up, and put your checklist in writing before you need it.
