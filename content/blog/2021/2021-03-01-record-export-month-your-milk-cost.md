Federal trade data released this month show U.S. dairy exports set an all-time record, with skim milk powder and dry whey both posting their strongest volumes ever and cheese turning in its second-best export month on record. If you sell finished cheese domestically, that headline probably didn't cross your desk. It should have, because a record export month pulls on the same milk supply that feeds your own vat, even if you never ship a wheel overseas.

We've had a run of calls from small-batch members this week asking a version of the same question: is milk about to get more expensive, or is this a one-month story? The honest answer is we don't fully know yet. What the data does show is that this wasn't a one-off spike — export growth has been building for several months running, and Class III futures have been firming as the year has gone on. Strong exports mean more of the milk supply is being pulled toward powder and whey production, which is a slower, quieter kind of pressure than a headline price jump, but it's a real one. Trade press coverage this quarter has noted processors adding milk-powder capacity to keep up with the order book, which tells you this isn't demand that's going to ease off on its own in the next month or two.

It's worth sitting with that connection for a second, because it isn't obvious from the trade numbers alone. When more milk solids leave the country as powder and whey, less of that same milk is available for a domestic cheese vat at the price it used to command. A member who buys spot milk for a small-batch run is competing, indirectly, with an export order that may be headed halfway around the world. That's not a reason to panic about your next invoice, but it is a reason to expect this kind of pressure to keep showing up unevenly, region by region, rather than easing all at once.

## What this actually changes on your make sheet

A record export month doesn't automatically mean your input cost moved by the same amount — your contract terms, your co-op's pooling formula, and your region's basis all sit between the national trade data and what shows up on your invoice. A few members on this week's calls described very different pictures: one Midwest creamery said their component pricing barely moved, while a Northeast affineur buying on a spot basis felt it already and had to adjust a wholesale quote mid-negotiation.

Before you touch your price list, it's worth separating a temporary input bump from a structural one. If your milk cost has moved and stayed moved for two consecutive statements, that's a signal worth acting on. If it's one month, it may be noise working through the system rather than a new baseline. Our *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* course walks through exactly this kind of decision — when to hold, when to reprice, and how to talk to a wholesale buyer about a cost-driven increase without it sounding like an excuse or, worse, an apology.

One practical habit several long-time members mentioned: keep a running log of your landed milk cost alongside your finished-cheese price, updated monthly rather than reconstructed from memory when a buyer pushes back. It turns a defensive conversation about a price increase into a documented one, and it's far easier to have that conversation calmly if you're not doing the math for the first time on the call.

## The other half of the story: softer retail traffic

It's also worth saying plainly that this kind of export strength is landing in a year where specialty and artisan cheese sales haven't fully recovered their pre-2020 rhythm. Foodservice accounts are still rebuilding, and some members tell us tasting-room and market-stall traffic remains well below what it was two years ago. A firmer input cost without matching retail recovery is a genuinely hard spot to be in, and we don't want to wave that away with a market update dressed up as good news.
