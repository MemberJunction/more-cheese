Not every member story this year is about a new product or a new market. Some of the most useful ones are quieter — a person who used a slow stretch to learn a part of the business she'd never had reason to look at before. That's the case with a clerk at a small specialty cheese shop in the upper Midwest, who spent a good part of last year's shutdowns doing something she describes as "finally reading the actual numbers instead of just ringing them up."

## From the register to the spreadsheet

She'd worked the counter for three years before curbside pickup replaced the case entirely for a few months. With foot traffic gone, the owner asked her to help reconcile vendor invoices against what was actually selling — a job that had always belonged to someone else. What she found surprised her: two of the shop's steadiest-looking cheeses, the ones customers asked for by name, were barely breaking even once spoilage and markdowns were counted.

"I'd been telling people those were our best sellers," she told us. "They were our most *popular*. Not the same thing." She started tracking sell-through by wheel instead of by category, which took a spreadsheet nobody had bothered to build before. It's not glamorous work, and it's not why most people get into cheese. But it's the kind of thing a lot of small counters skipped for years because there was always a line to serve instead.

Dairy sections generally have had a stronger January than usual this year, with more households making dedicated trips rather than grabbing cheese as an afterthought on a bigger errand. For a counter-service shop, that shift changes who's walking in and what they're looking for — fewer impulse buys, more people who already know they want a wedge of something specific. She noticed the same thing from her own register data before anyone told her the trend had a name.

## What changed on the floor

The practical result: the shop trimmed one slow-moving line, leaned harder into two others, and adjusted a supplier order that had been running heavier than it needed to since before she started. None of that required a single new product. It required someone willing to sit with numbers that don't feel like cheese work, during a stretch when the usual rhythm of the counter had already been upended anyway.

She's since started training the shop's newer part-time staff on the basics of the spreadsheet too, on the theory that anyone who can read a ledger line makes better recommendations at the case. Customers ask for a story about a cheese, she told us, but they decide based on price and what's actually moving — and a clerk who knows both numbers can have a better conversation than one who only knows the first.

She's not alone. A federal relief loan program reopened to small businesses again in January, and more than one member told us the paperwork forced the same kind of close look at payroll and overhead that she'd been doing at the register. We've heard versions of this story from members who spent an unplanned quiet season learning the parts of the business that usually get deferred — inventory counts, supplier terms, a proper cost sheet. If that's a gap in your own shop, our Education Committee can point you toward members who've built similar tracking from scratch and are usually glad to share the template. It's not the part of cheesemaking anyone dreams about. It's often the part that keeps the rest of it possible, and it tends to get learned fastest by whoever's closest to the register when things get quiet.
