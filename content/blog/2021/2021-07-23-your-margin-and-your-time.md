A survey published this spring in the Journal of Dairy Science put a number on something most members already feel in their gut: cheesemakers lose product to quality problems on a regular basis, and it's rarely a rare event. Among the sixty-one artisan producers surveyed, thirty-nine percent reported failing to meet their own quality standards at least once a year, and a third said it happens monthly. Sixty-two percent said the resulting loss runs somewhere between zero and five percent of annual product — manageable on its own, until you stack it against everything else eating into a thin margin.

That figure sits next to an older but still relevant one. A separate industry survey found that only about eighty percent of specialty cheesemakers were operating profitably even during years of strong sector growth, with ninety-two percent calling profitability an ongoing worry rather than a solved problem. And the pattern wasn't the one you'd expect: cheesemakers with higher gross revenue tended to run thinner margins on average, not fatter ones. Growth on its own was never the fix — and neither, on its own, is better cheese.

## Where the squeeze actually lives

Spoilage and rework are the parts of your cost sheet that are easiest to ignore because they don't show up as a single bad decision. A rind that colors wrong, a wheel that develops surface mold before it's ready to sell, a batch that just doesn't hit the mark — none of those feel like pricing problems in the moment. But every wheel you can't sell at full price, or can't sell at all, has to be paid for by the wheels that do sell. If you don't know what your loss rate actually costs you, you're pricing blind.

That's precisely the gap our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course exists to close. Its opening session doesn't start with what the market will bear — it starts with your own numbers: feed, packaging, labor, aging loss, and the wheels that don't make it to sale at all. Members who've taken it consistently tell us the aging-loss line is the one they'd been guessing at for years, and the one that moves their price floor the most once they actually measure it.

## What changes once you know the number

Once a loss rate is a real percentage instead of a shrug, a few decisions get easier. You can tell whether a new wholesale account is worth the shelf space once you've built its likely loss into the math instead of treating it as a rounding error. You can also make a better case to a buyer for a price increase, because "my costs went up" lands differently than "my costs went up, and here's the twelve percent of last quarter's batches that didn't make it to a shelf at all."

Demand this summer is giving some members an opening to test that math. One farmers market reported this month that it's running with roughly a fifth fewer vendors than it had before the pandemic, yet stands were selling down to scraps by late morning because customers kept showing up faster than tables could restock. That's exactly the condition where a member who knows her real cost per wheel can hold a firmer price, and a member who's still guessing ends up leaving margin on the table out of habit.
