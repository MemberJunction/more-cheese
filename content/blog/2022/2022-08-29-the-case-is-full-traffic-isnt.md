For most of this year, the story members told us was about supply: not enough milk, block prices climbing past two dollars a pound, a case that couldn't stay stocked. That story has quietly flipped. Blocks have come off their spring peaks, the tightest points in the milk supply have eased, and the raw-material side of the ledger finally looks less alarming than it did in May. What's replaced it, according to several members who called the Membership & Outreach Committee this month, isn't relief. It's a quieter, harder problem: fewer people are actually stopping at the case.

Foodservice traffic has been soft all summer as menu prices climbed faster than most operators wanted, and a slower restaurant trade tends to ripple straight back to wholesale wedge and block orders. Retail hasn't collapsed — grocery cheese sections are still doing fine on paper — but several members selling into both channels describe the same pattern: baskets are smaller, substitutions toward lower-priced items are more common, and a customer who used to add a second wedge on impulse is more likely to stick to the one they came in for.

## What this means for your order sheet

If your wholesale volume has softened even as your input costs have started to ease, you're not imagining a disconnect — you're seeing two different markets move on two different schedules. Milk supply responds to weather, herd size, and feed availability. Foodservice and retail traffic respond to how stretched a household or a restaurant's regulars feel that month, and that lags behind commodity prices by weeks or months. A block price that eases in August doesn't automatically bring back a diner who cut back on cheese plates in June.

"We finally stopped fighting the milk truck for supply, and now I'm fighting for shelf space in a case where the buyer's ordering smaller," one member selling through a regional distributor told the committee. "Nobody warned me the two problems wouldn't happen at the same time." That's a fair complaint, and it's a useful reminder that easing input costs and easing demand pressure are two separate recoveries, not one.

## Where to focus instead of the invoice

For members feeling this squeeze, the more useful lever right now is probably the order conversation itself rather than the price sheet. A smaller, more frequent order with a distributor beats a large one that risks aging on a shelf while a buyer works through softer sell-through. If you haven't talked to your accounts directly about what's actually moving off their shelves this quarter, that conversation is worth having before you assume a slow month is about your product rather than the traffic walking past it.

The Membership & Outreach Committee is compiling notes from these calls into a short, informal read on what members in different regions and channels are seeing this fall — not a forecast, just a comparison point so you're not guessing in isolation. If you want to add your own experience to that picture, or just want to talk through what a softer quarter means for your own ordering, reach out to the committee directly.
