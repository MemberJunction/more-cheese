If you've looked at your milk check or your cheese invoice in the last few weeks and done a double take, you're not imagining it. The Class III price for May came in as the highest it's ever been for the month, and Class I isn't far behind it — up more than a dollar and a half per hundredweight from April alone, and well ahead of where things sat this time last year. Cheddar blocks and barrels have followed the same line upward, and butter's not far behind.

None of this is happening in isolation. Feed costs are up sharply — corn alone is running well above what it cost producers a year ago — and fuel and freight are up with it. The same inflationary pressure that's showing up at the gas pump is showing up in a cheesemaker's ledger. Higher milk prices sound like straightforwardly good news if you're the one selling milk, but for a small creamery buying it, or a shop marking up a wheel to cover a delivery that costs more than it did in January, the math gets tighter, not looser.

## Where the pressure is actually landing

We've heard from members on both sides of this. Producers are glad to see stronger returns after a couple of lean years, but several told us their input costs are climbing just as fast, which means the "record price" headline doesn't always translate into a record margin once feed, fuel, and labor are accounted for. Margin coverage programs that track milk price against feed cost are showing real improvement this spring, but that's a national average — it doesn't tell you what your specific herd, your specific supplier contract, or your specific delivery route is actually costing you this month.

On the retail and food-service side, members are having harder conversations with buyers about why a wheel that cost a certain amount last quarter costs noticeably more now — and why that's not a markup decision so much as a pass-through of what's happening upstream. If you sell wholesale, this is a good season to be specific with your accounts about why prices are moving, rather than leaving them to guess. A short note that ties your price change to what's happening in the broader milk market tends to land better than a bare number on an invoice. If you're the one buying, ask your suppliers the same question before you assume the increase is padding rather than pass-through.

## What to actually do with this

We're not going to tell you where prices go from here — nobody credibly can, and a trade newsletter that pretends otherwise isn't doing you any favors. Traceability rules are also tightening federally this year, which means the paperwork trail behind a load of milk or a batch of cheese is only going to get more scrutinized, not less; that's a separate conversation, but it's worth keeping in the back of your mind as you plan any changes to how you source or ship.

What we can say with more confidence is that a spring like this one rewards members who track their own cost of production closely enough to know, wheel by wheel, whether a price increase on the supply side is something you need to match, absorb, or push back against. If pricing your own product with any real confidence has been more guesswork than math lately, the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course walks through exactly that process step by step — and this is as good a season as any to take it, while the numbers are moving enough to make the exercise concrete instead of theoretical.

If your own margin math doesn't add up after working through it, that's worth a conversation with your accountant before it becomes a bigger problem than a single tight quarter. We can point you toward the course; we can't tell you what to charge.
