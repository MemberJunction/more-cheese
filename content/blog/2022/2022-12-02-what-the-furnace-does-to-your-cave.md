Every year around this time, we get a version of the same call: a member's cave, which had been holding a steady 85 to 90 percent humidity all fall, suddenly starts reading in the 60s. Nothing about the cave changed. What changed is that the building around it got a furnace running, and forced-air heat is one of the most efficient humidity thieves in a small production facility. Cold air simply can't hold as much moisture as warm air, so the moment you heat it, its relative humidity drops — and if your cave shares any airflow at all with the heated part of the building, that dry air finds its way in.

This matters more in early December than at almost any other point in the year, because it's exactly when a lot of members are also asking their caves to hold more inventory than usual for the holiday case. A rind that's drying out faster than planned, on wheels you're counting on for gift boxes and year-end orders, is a bad combination. The good news is that this is one of the most fixable problems in cheesemaking, once you know to look for it.

## What to check first

Start with where your make room and your aging space share a wall, a door, or a duct. If your furnace vents anywhere near the cave, even indirectly, you'll likely see the humidity drop track the thermostat almost exactly — check your logs against your heating schedule and the pattern usually jumps right out. A door that gets propped open more often in winter, because staff are moving product in and out for holiday orders, is another common and often-overlooked culprit.

Once you've found the source, the fix is rarely a bigger investment than a humidifier and some weatherstripping. A standalone humidifier sized to your cave's volume, run on a hygrometer-controlled cycle rather than continuously, will usually recover a healthy range within a day or two. Sealing gaps around doors and ducts keeps that recovery from being undone every time someone opens the cave to pull holiday orders. For members running a walk-in cooler as their aging space, check the door seal specifically — those wear out faster than people expect, and a compressor working harder in winter can dry the air inside as a side effect.
