If you've looked at your milk check this month and felt a flicker of relief, you're not imagining it. Federal pricing data has the all-milk price running roughly 30 percent above where it sat a year ago, a record for this time of year. For a member paying by the hundredweight, that's real money. The catch is that almost everything sitting between the barn and your cheese case got more expensive at the same time, and this month it's heat doing a lot of the damage.

A stretch of extreme temperatures moved across a wide swath of the country in the past few weeks, and it hit livestock operations hard in exactly the states you'd expect — the southern Plains, parts of the Southwest, stretches of the Midwest already dealing with drought. Cows under heat stress eat less, produce less, and need more from the ration to make up the difference, which is a bad combination when hay and grain are already expensive. Year over year, corn is running roughly 15 percent higher, soybeans about 10 percent higher, and alfalfa hay as much as 25 percent higher in some markets. Add in higher costs for fuel, labor, and just about every input a farmstead operation touches, and the overall index of what farmers pay for goods and services is up more than 12 percent from last summer.

## What that means for your case

For members who buy milk rather than produce it, the price you're paying reflects that squeeze whether or not you can see the thermometer at the farm. For members who milk their own herds, the math is starker: a record price per hundredweight can still net out to a thinner margin than last year's more modest price, once feed and cooling costs are subtracted. A few things worth doing this week if you haven't already:

- Pull your last two feed invoices side by side. If your ration cost per cow has jumped more than your milk price has, that's worth flagging to whoever sets your pricing, not absorbing quietly.
- If your herd is showing signs of heat stress — reduced intake, panting, milk yield sliding — our Milk Quality & Herd Health course covers the practical side of managing a herd through a heat event, including ration adjustments that don't wreck your components.
- If you're setting wholesale or retail prices off last month's cost sheet, redo the math. A lot has moved in four weeks.

None of this is new to anyone who lived through the past two summers, but the scale of it this year is bigger than the last two combined for a lot of our members. We'd rather members raise prices deliberately, with the numbers in front of them, than get caught flat by a fall reconciliation that doesn't add up.

## What we're watching

We'll keep an eye on the next round of federal dairy production data, due in early August, and on whether the current heat pattern breaks or holds through the rest of the month.
