If you sell wholesale milk into a cheese plant, this spring has been a good one to be paying attention. Class III has been running several dollars above where it sat a year ago, and the reason isn't a mystery: cheese demand, both at retail and through food service, has been strong enough to pull the whole milk check with it. Butter and powder are up too, but cheese is doing the heavy lifting.

That matters for members on both sides of the counter. If you're a producer selling fluid milk or bulk curd into a larger operation, a stronger Class III number is real money, and it's worth checking whether your contract passes that number through cleanly or smooths it over several months. If you're running your own vat and buying cultures, rennet, and packaging against a tighter margin than your milk price alone would suggest, this is the spring to ask your supplier for the same pricing transparency you'd want from a buyer.

## Where the demand is actually coming from

The clearest signal in the data isn't a single blockbuster order — it's that both grocery cheese sales and restaurant cheese use have stayed above last year's levels at the same time, months into what should have been a return to something more ordinary. Food service in particular has been recovering steadily as more kitchens run closer to full staffing and full hours, and every one of those kitchens is a pizza, a sandwich, or a cheese board that wasn't being made from the same volume a year ago.

For a small or mid-size creamery, that shows up less as a single big number and more as steadier reorder rhythms from the accounts you already have. A distributor who was trimming order sizes eighteen months ago calling to ask if you can hold a standing weekly volume is a different conversation than a one-off spot order, and it's worth treating it as one — that's the kind of relationship a longer supply agreement is built on, not just a good month.

## What to actually do with a strong milk check

A record price doesn't automatically become a record margin. Feed, packaging, and freight have all moved too, and members who've told us they came out ahead this spring are the ones who ran the math on their own cost side before assuming the milk number told the whole story. If you haven't recalculated your cost per finished pound since your last price increase, this is a reasonable week to do it — not because the numbers are bad, but because they've moved enough that last quarter's assumptions may not hold.

If your pricing conversations keep circling back to "what should this actually cost," the federation's *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* course walks through building a real cost-per-wheel model rather than working backward from what the market seems to be paying. It's a practical companion to a season like this one, when the top-line numbers look good enough that it's tempting to stop asking harder questions underneath them.
