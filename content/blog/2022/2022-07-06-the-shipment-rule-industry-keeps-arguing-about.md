Interstate shipment of aged and raw-milk cheese has been an on-again, off-again fight for as long as most of our members have been in business, and this summer it's on again. A comment period opened last month on how shippers document aging and handling when a wheel crosses a state line, and our Membership & Outreach Committee has heard from enough members already that we wanted to flag it here rather than wait for the newsletter roundup.

The short version: nothing about the underlying rule is changing yet. What's under discussion is the paperwork a small creamery has to generate to prove a wheel met its aging requirement before it left the state where it was made — and whether that documentation burden falls the same way on a shop shipping fifty pounds a month as it does on an operation shipping fifty thousand.

## Why this keeps coming back

This isn't unique to cheese, and it isn't new. Cross-border dairy trade has been the subject of formal disputes for years, including an early-2022 ruling that found a neighboring country's dairy quota administration was functioning as a de facto barrier to market access — a reminder that quota and documentation rules can restrict trade just as effectively as a tariff line, even when no one calls it that. The dispute in that case turned on paperwork: import notices that quietly set aside quota volume for a narrow class of processors, which had the practical effect of shutting smaller exporters out of a market that was supposed to be open to them.

Domestically, the same dynamic shows up at a smaller scale. A recordkeeping requirement written with a large processor's compliance staff in mind doesn't shrink for a two-person creamery, and the paperwork cost lands hardest on exactly the members who can least absorb it. The mechanism is different — an aging log instead of an import notice — but the effect is the same: a rule that looks neutral on paper can function as a barrier in practice, depending entirely on who has the staff to comply with it easily and who doesn't.

We've said this before and we'll keep saying it: a documentation standard that only a large operation can comply with efficiently isn't a neutral rule, whatever its intent. Several members who ship into multiple states told us this spring that the current draft language would require a duplicate aging log for each destination state, on top of the log they already keep for their home state's health department.

## What we're asking members to do

The comment period on this rule is open for a few more weeks. If interstate shipment is any part of your business — even occasional wholesale orders that cross a state line — this is worth ten minutes of your time. Our Membership & Outreach Committee has a short guide on what to include in a comment (specifics about your own shipping volume and documentation costs carry more weight than general objections) and can point you to the docket if you don't already have it bookmarked.

If you've already commented, or if your state association has taken a position, let us know — we're tracking which states have organized responses so we can coordinate rather than duplicate. And if you're headed to the annual conference in Asheville later this month, the Membership & Outreach Committee will have an update on where this stands by the time we're all in the same room.

None of this is a fast process. Rules like this one tend to move on a timeline measured in quarters, not weeks, and the comment period closing this summer is just one step before whatever agency is writing the rule reviews the record and issues something closer to final language. That's exactly why the committee keeps pushing members to weigh in now rather than waiting for a "final" version to react to — by the time a rule is final, the window for reshaping it has mostly closed, and what's left is compliance, not comment.

We know a comment letter can feel like shouting into a docket that nobody reads. In our experience it's read more carefully than members assume, especially when it comes from someone who ships product across state lines every month and can describe, in specific numbers, what a duplicate log actually costs in labor and time. General frustration doesn't move a rule. A concrete account of your own shipping volume, your current recordkeeping, and what a second log would add to it does.
