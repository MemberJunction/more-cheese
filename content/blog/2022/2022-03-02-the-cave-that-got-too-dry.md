A member in the upper Midwest called our office last month with a problem that took her a while
to diagnose: her aging room was losing wheels to premature rind cracking, and nothing about her
recipe or her make had changed. She's been running a small farmstead operation for about six
years, aging mostly cheddar and a washed-rind wheel she sells locally, and she agreed to walk us
through what turned out to be a seasonal, easily missed cause.

"I checked everything I knew to check first," she said. "Milk source, culture, salt rate, wrap
timing. All of it matched what I'd been doing for years. It wasn't until I actually logged the
humidity reading twice a day for a week that I saw the pattern — the room was running dry, and
it had started right when the furnace kicked on for the season."

## The furnace was the culprit

Her aging room shares a wall with the part of the building heated through the winter, and once
the furnace started running steadily in late fall, it had been quietly pulling ambient moisture
out of the aging space through the shared wall and a door that doesn't seal as tightly as she'd
assumed. "The room's still cold enough — it's not a temperature problem. It's a moisture
problem, and those two things get treated like they're the same issue when they're not."

The fix, once she'd found the cause, was cheap: a simple humidifier on a timer, a better door
seal, and a log sheet she now checks daily instead of relying on memory. "I lost more wheels in
December than I want to admit before I figured this out. Now it's a five-minute check every
morning, and I catch drift before it becomes a problem instead of after."

## Why this is worth flagging to other members

We're passing this along because it's a seasonal pattern, not a one-off. Any farmstead operation
that ages cheese near a heated part of the building is exposed to the same failure mode every
winter, and it's the kind of problem that's easy to miss because nothing about the recipe or the
milk changed — only the room did. If your rind behavior has been drifting since the weather
turned cold and you haven't logged humidity directly, that's the first thing worth checking
before you start second-guessing your make sheet.

She also had a word of caution about over-correcting. "Once I started paying attention, I
almost swung too far the other way and ran the room too humid trying to compensate. It's worth
tracking both temperature and humidity together, not chasing one number in isolation." A room
that runs too wet invites its own problems — unwanted mold and off odors on wheels that were
never meant to develop a rind that aggressive — so the goal isn't just "more humid," it's
"steady, and at the right number for the specific cheese in that spot on the shelf."

One large aging room can lose several gallons of water into the air every week during a cold
snap, which is a lot of moisture to replace consistently by hand. A timer-driven humidifier
solves the consistency problem in a way that remembering to mist the room twice a day never
quite does, especially once the person doing the misting is also covering everything else a
small farmstead operation asks of one person in the winter.

If you want to compare notes with other members who've dealt with cave climate drift, the
Standards Committee has fielded similar questions before and can point you toward members
willing to talk through their own setup. It's a small, unglamorous fix, but it's the kind of
thing that quietly saves a season's worth of wheels once you catch it.
