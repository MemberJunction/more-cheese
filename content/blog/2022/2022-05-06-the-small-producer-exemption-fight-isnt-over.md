We get some version of this question every few months: where does the push for small-producer exemptions under federal food-safety rules actually stand? The honest answer is that it's further along than it was a year ago and nowhere near settled, which isn't as satisfying as a yes or no but is the truer picture.

A meaningful share of our smaller members — creameries making a few hundred wheels a week, not a few hundred thousand — have spent the last couple of years telling us the same thing: a recordkeeping and traceability standard built with large-scale processors in mind doesn't scale down cleanly, and the cost of compliance can land disproportionately on the operation least able to absorb it. A big plant can add a line item and a part-time data-entry position and barely notice. A two-person creamery absorbing the same requirement is looking at real hours pulled away from making cheese. That's the case our members have been making directly, through petition signatures and letters to their own state and federal representatives, for a couple of years running now.

## What's actually moved

Federal regulators extended the public comment period on the proposed traceability rule once already, specifically to let small and mid-sized producers weigh in with more detail than the original window allowed — a sign, we think, that the volume and specificity of that input was landing. The rule under discussion widens the list of foods subject to additional recordkeeping requirements, and certain cheeses are on that list. Where the exemption line ultimately gets drawn for specialty and artisan producers is still being worked out, and we'd rather tell you that plainly than promise a carve-out that hasn't been finalized.

What we can say is that the volume of specific, well-documented member input — actual make volumes, actual staffing numbers, actual cost estimates for added recordkeeping — has been part of the record regulators are working from, and that kind of detail tends to carry more weight than a general objection. A letter that says "this will be expensive" gets read and set aside. A letter that says "this adds roughly six hours a week for an operation our size, and here's the math" gets cited.

If you haven't weighed in yet, the Food Safety Committee is still collecting exactly this kind of documentation from small producers, and it's more useful coming from you directly than filtered through us. A one-page account of what a new recordkeeping requirement would actually cost your specific operation, in hours and dollars, is worth more than a strongly worded paragraph — and it's the kind of thing a committee volunteer can help you put together if writing it up from scratch feels like one more task on a long list.

## Staying ready either way

Whatever the final rule looks like, waiting to find out before building basic traceability habits is the riskier bet. A simple lot-tracking system you can produce on short notice — where a batch's milk came from, when it was made, where it shipped, who received it — is good practice regardless of which way this particular exemption question breaks, and it's the same discipline that helps in a recall you hope never comes. Members who already keep this kind of record consistently tend to describe the eventual compliance work, whatever it turns out to require, as an extension of something they were already doing rather than a system built from nothing under deadline pressure.

We'll keep members posted as this moves, and we won't pretend a resolution is closer than it is just to give you a tidier update. If you want to add your voice or just understand where things stand for your own operation, reach out to the Food Safety Committee — that's where this work is actually happening, and where the next round of comment collection will start.
