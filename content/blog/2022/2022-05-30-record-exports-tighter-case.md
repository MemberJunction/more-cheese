Dairy trade figures released this spring showed the strongest single export month the sector had recorded in years, with export value clearing $900 million for the first time and cheese volume climbing sharply alongside whey and lactose. For most of our members, that number lives three steps removed from their own ledger. But it still shows up, and this month it's worth walking through why.

## Why an export record touches a domestic case

When a large share of a country's dairy output moves overseas, less of it competes for shelf space at home. That's part of why cheese cases stayed tight through the spring even as cold-storage inventories were running above their five-year average — a lot of new supply was heading to a container rather than a wholesale account. Members who sell mostly domestic have told us this is the part of the trade conversation that's easiest to miss: exports don't just move product, they move pricing pressure around, and they do it quietly enough that it rarely gets blamed for anything.

There's a second layer worth knowing about if you sell anywhere near a border. Quota administration changes for cross-border dairy shipments took effect this spring, the kind of technical, unglamorous rule change that rarely makes a member newsletter but absolutely changes what a broker will quote you. If you've ever wondered why a shipment that cleared easily last year suddenly needs another form, this is usually why. It's not arbitrary; it's a rule that changed underneath you, and it's worth reading rather than just routing around.

None of this is advice on where to sell. It's context for a question we've been getting a lot this month: "why does my case feel tighter when the news says supply is up?" Increasingly, the answer is that the supply is up somewhere else, headed somewhere else, and the domestic market is left working with what's left.

## The margin side hasn't gotten easier

Exports being strong doesn't mean input costs went anywhere. The federal margin measure most dairy producers watch closest — the gap between milk price and feed cost — actually improved into late spring, landing at its best reading in years. But that number moved because milk prices climbed as fast as feed costs did, not because feed got cheaper. Corn and alfalfa were both sitting at some of the highest recorded prices for the program's history, and fuel costs for anyone hauling milk or finished product were up sharply too. "Margin looks fine on paper" and "margin feels fine on the farm" are two different sentences, and members running smaller operations have told us the gap between them is wider than it looks from a spreadsheet.

If you're a small or mid-size producer trying to figure out whether an export-adjacent buyer is worth chasing, the honest answer is that the paperwork burden is real and worth understanding before you say yes to a first order. Whatever side of this you're standing on — selling into it, competing against it, or just trying to make sense of why your case looks the way it does this month — the federation's market updates will keep tracking both halves of this story as the season goes on.
