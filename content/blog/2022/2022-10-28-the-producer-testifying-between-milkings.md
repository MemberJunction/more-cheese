Most of our member spotlights feature someone whose name you'd recognize from a conference hallway or a committee roster. This one is about a member most of you have never met: a farmstead cheesemaker in the upper Midwest who milks forty goats twice a day, runs a two-person cave operation, and has spent the last six weeks writing public comment on a state aging-rule hearing in whatever hour is left after the second milking.

She joined the federation four years ago mostly for the newsletter and the discount on lab testing, she told us, and never expected to end up reading proposed rule language line by line. That changed the first time a hearing notice landed in her inbox with language she couldn't parse without help, and a committee volunteer walked her through what it actually meant for an operation her size. "I realized nobody was going to translate this for me every time," she said. "So I started reading the actual bills instead of waiting for someone else's summary."

## What the testimony actually looks like

She's not a lawyer and doesn't pretend to be one. Her written comments this fall have focused on one narrow, concrete point: how a proposed change to minimum aging measurement would affect a wheel her size differently than it affects a wheel four times as large, because the density and salt penetration timelines aren't the same. That's the kind of detail that gets lost in a general industry comment letter but lands hard with a legislative committee when it comes from someone actually making the cheese.

"I'm not trying to win an argument," she told us. "I'm trying to make sure whoever writes the final rule has actually seen a small operation's numbers, because most of what crosses a statehouse desk is written with a much bigger plant in mind." She keeps a running document of every hearing date and comment deadline she's tracking, shares it informally with two other small producers in her region, and says the biggest barrier for most farmstead members isn't the writing — it's simply knowing a hearing is happening before the window closes.

## What it costs her, and what it's worth

She's candid that this isn't free. Comment periods have a habit of landing during calving season, market prep, or exactly the week she's short-staffed, and there's no direct payoff she can point to on a balance sheet. What she gets, she says, is a rule that at least reflects her operation's reality more often than it would have otherwise, and a handful of relationships with other small producers who now call her before they file anything themselves.

If you've been meaning to weigh in on a state or federal proposal but weren't sure where to start, the federation's advocacy updates flag open comment periods as they appear, and the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with members like her who've done this before and are generally happy to compare notes. You don't need a law degree. You need the actual bill text, twenty minutes, and — as she put it — "the willingness to say what's true about your own operation instead of guessing at what sounds official."
