This is the month a lot of local farmers' markets reopen for the season, and members running a stand alongside a cave are stretched in two directions at once: getting a market table ready while the aging room quietly shifts under them with the weather. Every spring we hear from members whose aging rooms suddenly stop behaving. A cave that held steady all winter starts swinging a few points of humidity a day, a rind that was doing exactly what it should is drying faster than expected, and nobody changed a single setting. Nothing did change, at least not inside the room. What changed is outside it.

Spring is the season when the gap between daytime and nighttime outdoor temperatures widens, and that swing works its way into any aging space that is not fully isolated from the building around it. A cave built into a basement, a converted barn, or a walk-in cooler retrofitted for aging all respond to it differently, but the underlying problem is the same: your climate control system was tuned for winter conditions, and winter just ended.

## Airflow, not just the thermostat

The instinct when humidity starts drifting is to reach for the humidifier or the dehumidifier and adjust the setpoint. That is not wrong, but it treats the symptom. A cave that is fighting outdoor temperature swings usually has an airflow problem before it has a humidity problem — air exchange rates, door-opening habits, and where cold air pools near the floor all shift as the outside air changes. A member who runs a small aging room in an older building told us that repositioning a single fan, rather than touching the humidifier at all, fixed a swing that had been bothering her for two weeks.

If your rinds are drying unevenly, check whether the airflow near the door or vent differs from the airflow at the back of the room — that mismatch grows more pronounced as outdoor air gets warmer and more variable. Wheels near a heat source, even an indirect one like an exterior wall that catches afternoon sun, will age differently than wheels on the shaded side of the same rack, and that difference gets more visible in spring than in the steadier temperatures of deep winter.

## A course built for exactly this

This is the seasonal problem the Humidity & Airflow Control course was built to address, and if you have been meaning to take it, spring is the right time — you can apply what you learn against a room that is actively giving you feedback, rather than waiting for a hypothetical problem next winter. The course covers reading your own cave's airflow patterns, not just the general theory, which matters because no two rooms behave identically.

For members without the time for a full course right now, the practical version is simpler than it sounds: walk your cave at two different times of day this week, note where the air feels still versus moving, and compare that to where your problem wheels are sitting. More often than you would expect, the fix is a fan, a shelf moved six inches, or a door left cracked for twenty minutes rather than a full hour. Your cave will tell you what it needs if you give it a few days of paying closer attention before you touch the controls.
