The register closeout at a member retailer like Village Curds & Ferments happens every night regardless of the calendar. The one this week is different. A shop manager we spoke with runs a second tally alongside it — not for the till, but for herself, a page of notes on what sold, what sat, and what she is not reordering come January.

"The register tells you what happened," she told us. "It doesn't tell you why." This week the two don't always agree, and figuring out the gap is the actual job.

## What sold does not always explain itself

Holiday sales at a specialty counter run on gift sets, wedge boards, and a handful of showpiece wheels that customers buy once a year and forget about the other eleven months. Her notes this week flag three cheeses that moved faster than the register alone would suggest — paired with a jam or a cracker at the counter, not sitting alone in the case — and two that sold through her holiday allocation but that she is quietly moving to a smaller order in January, because the sell-through masked a lot of samples given away to move them.

That distinction between "sold" and "sold on its own merits" is one a lot of members lose track of this time of year, when volume covers for a lot of sins. A wedge that only moves paired with something else is telling you about its price point, not its popularity. If your holiday numbers looked strong across the board, it is worth a second pass at what actually earned its shelf space versus what got carried by a gift set. Industry reporting on specialty retail this fall has been pointing the same direction: shops that are adding boards, sandwiches, and other prepared formats around their cheese case are partly doing it because those formats move product that would otherwise sit and turn.

## The list she is bringing to January

Her second tally isn't just about product. It has three lines for staffing — who handled the rush well, who needs more time on the scale before spring, and one apprentice she wants to put in front of customers more, because holiday week is where you learn who can talk someone into trying a new cheese without sounding like they're selling them one.

She is also flagging two suppliers for a conversation in the new year: one whose cold-chain handling on a December shipment left a wheel softer than she wanted at the counter, and one who came through on a last-minute reorder that saved her holiday case. Neither conversation needs to happen before January 2, but neither one should wait past it either — a closeout that only balances the register and skips the people and supplier notes is only half a closeout.

Her last line, this year, is one for herself: which new formats earned a permanent spot on the case instead of a seasonal one. A board program she started in the fall turned out to move slow-selling wedges she'd been carrying at a loss all summer. That's a January decision, not a December one, but she likes to write it down while the reasoning is still fresh instead of trying to reconstruct it from a spreadsheet in three weeks.

If you run a retail counter and haven't built a habit of a second, non-financial tally alongside your year-end numbers, this is a good week to start one. It doesn't need to be elaborate — a few lines while the year is still fresh in your head will tell you more about your case than the sales report alone.
