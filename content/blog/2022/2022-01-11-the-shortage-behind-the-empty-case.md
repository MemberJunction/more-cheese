Several of our retail members opened January short-handed, and it isn't a coincidence. A winter wave of illness is keeping grocery and specialty retail workers home across the country at a rate that's thinning shelves even at large chains, and small specialty counters — where two or three people often run the entire cheese program — feel a single absence far more sharply than a supermarket with a deep bench does.

The coverage gap we're hearing about isn't the delivery-truck story we wrote about a few weeks ago, though severe winter weather is compounding that too. This is specifically about who's behind the counter on a given afternoon. A cheesemonger calling out sick doesn't just mean fewer hands cutting wheels — it means whoever's left is also fielding customer questions, restocking, and running the register, all without the product knowledge that took the absent staffer years to build. A few members told us they've started closing an hour early on short-staffed days rather than run the counter thin and risk a bad customer experience or an unsafe rush at the slicer.

## What members are trying

There's no single fix here, but a few approaches have come up more than once in conversations this week:

- Cross-training a second person on basic cheese handling and labeling, even if they're not your primary monger, so a single absence doesn't shut down the whole case.
- Trimming the number of active cheeses on the counter temporarily rather than trying to run a full program with a skeleton crew — a tighter, well-tended selection beats a sprawling one nobody has time to check.
- Being upfront with regular customers about reduced hours or a smaller case. Members report that regulars are largely understanding right now, more so than they expected going into the new year.

None of this is a substitute for actual staffing, and we're not going to pretend a rotation trick solves a labor shortage. But if you're triaging this week, prioritize whatever keeps your food-safety basics intact — proper glove changes, correct case temperatures, clean slicers — over keeping every variety on display. A short case is a minor inconvenience. A food-safety lapse from a rushed, undertrained fill-in is not.

## The part that's easy to miss

It's worth saying plainly: this kind of short-term staffing strain is exactly when small mistakes compound. If you're pulling in a stockroom employee to help behind the counter for the first time, don't assume they already know your allergen labeling or your cross-contact rules just because they've worked in the store for years. A five-minute refresher before their first shift on the counter is cheap insurance, and it's a habit worth keeping even once staffing normalizes.

If your team could use a fast, practical refresher, the Education Committee can point new or temporary counter staff toward shorter modules rather than a full course — ask on the member portal. And if you're managing through this right now, we'd genuinely like to hear what's working; member stories like these are often the most useful thing we publish, especially when the fix turns out to be simpler than expected.
