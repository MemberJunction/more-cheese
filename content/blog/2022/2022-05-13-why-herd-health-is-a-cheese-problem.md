Every spring we hear the same complaint from a certain kind of member: the vat is doing everything right and the cheese still isn't behaving. Same culture, same temperature, same set time — and somehow a batch that won't firm up the way it did in February. Nine times out of ten, the answer isn't in the cheese room. It's upstream, in the milk, and more specifically in the herd it came from.

## What changes underneath a spring flush

As pasture greens up and cows shift off winter feed, butterfat and protein ratios move, sometimes by more than a maker expects. A milk that tests fine on paper can still behave differently in the vat because the fat-to-protein balance has shifted, which changes how the curd sets and how much moisture it holds. Layer on somatic cell count, which tends to drift with herd stress, transition periods, and anything that disrupts a normal milking routine, and you have two variables moving at once — right when demand for fresh spring cheese is picking up and nobody wants to slow production to chase the problem down.

None of this is exotic; it's a well-documented seasonal pattern, not a mystery specific to any one herd. But documented and accounted-for are two different things. A make sheet written in February, when milk was colder and more consistent, doesn't automatically know that May's milk needs a different set time or a slightly adjusted culture rate. The producers who handle spring flush well aren't the ones with unusually stable herds — they're the ones who checked the numbers and adjusted the sheet instead of assuming last month's settings would still work.

This is exactly the gap our Milk Quality & Herd Health course is built to close. It's aimed at cheesemakers, not veterinarians, and it treats the herd as the first stage of your process rather than someone else's problem. The course walks through reading a milk quality report the way a maker needs to — what a somatic cell count trend actually predicts about yield and shelf life, how butterfat swings should change your make sheet before you even open the vat, and what questions to bring back to whoever supplies your milk if the numbers are moving in a direction you don't like.

## A practical habit, not a certificate to frame

You don't need to become a herd nutritionist to benefit from this. The most useful thing several past participants told us they took away was simpler: a habit of pulling the milk quality numbers before troubleshooting the cheese, instead of after. A quality manager at a mid-sized creamery in the Northeast put it well on a course call last month — she said she used to blame her culture first and her milk last, and this spring she flipped the order, and it saved her two batches already.

If your make has felt slightly off this spring and you've been assuming it's a culture or timing issue, it's worth ruling out the milk first. The course is self-paced, runs through the Education Committee's course catalog, and pairs well with anything you're already doing around seasonal recipe adjustments. It won't turn you into a herd nutritionist, and it isn't meant to — the goal is a maker who can read a quality report well enough to know when to call one in, not a maker who's expected to diagnose a herd issue solo.
