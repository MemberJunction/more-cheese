Most weeks, this space goes to a producer. This week it goes to a member on the retail side, because what she's been doing with her case since June is worth passing along to anyone who sells cheese by the pound, not just the ones who make it.

She runs the cheese counter at a mid-sized specialty grocer, and for years her pricing followed the wheel: whatever the wholesale invoice said a wheel cost, divided across the case, marked up by a standard percentage, done. It worked fine when wholesale prices moved slowly. It stopped working this year, when several of the references she buys moved sharply up in the spring and have been drifting back down since. "I was either underpriced on stuff I bought at the peak or overpriced on stuff I bought after it came off," she told us. "My customers noticed both, and they notice a lot faster than my spreadsheet did."

## Pricing by the wedge, not the wheel

So she rebuilt her spreadsheet around each individual cut instead of the wheel it came from — tracking landed cost per wedge at time of cut, not at time of invoice. It sounds like more work, and for the first few weeks it was. Now it's a fifteen-minute Friday task, and it's given her something she didn't have before: a real-time view of which cheeses in her case are actually making money this month versus which ones are riding on last quarter's cost basis.

The bigger change was upstream of the spreadsheet. She started calling her distributor rep every other week instead of once a quarter, specifically to ask what's moving in the wholesale market before it shows up on an invoice. "I used to find out prices had moved when I opened the box," she said. "Now I usually know a week or two before, and that's the difference between adjusting calmly and scrambling." That's held up well through a summer where wholesale prices have been sliding after a strong spring — she's repriced down twice already without a single case of stale cost data catching her off guard, which is more than she can say for last year.

Not everything changed. She kept her house rule of never discounting a cheese just because it's aging past its ideal window — if it's still good, it goes on the counter with a story and a taste, not a markdown sticker. And she didn't touch her staff's tasting-note habit, the one where every new cut gets a line in the shop's shared notebook before it goes in the case. "That notebook is worth more to my regulars than any price I could put on a sign," she said, and given how many of them ask for it by name, she's probably right.

## What this means for your case

Trade coverage of this year's wholesale swings keeps pointing to the same thing: buyers who track cost closely are weathering the volatility better than buyers who wait for the invoice to tell them what changed. That's as true at her counter as it is at a distributor's desk, and it means the customers walking up to a case like hers are already primed to trust a shop that can explain what they're paying for. A per-wedge cost view makes that explanation honest instead of aspirational — you can point at a number and mean it, rather than shrugging at a shelf-talker written six weeks ago.

If your pricing has been chasing a wholesale market that's moved more this year than it has in a while, you don't need new software to try what she did — a shared spreadsheet and a standing call with your distributor gets you most of the way there.
