A member out of the upper Midwest told us this week that she'd underestimated exactly one part of her holiday plan: not the milk supply, not the aging schedule, but the packing table. Her farmstead operation has spent three years building a following at markets and through a small wholesale list, and this is the first season she's committing real volume to holiday gift boxes — curated wedges, a jar of something local, a card with the farm's story on it. The cheese, she said, was the easy part.

## The part nobody warns you about

Cutting a big wheel into consistent, presentable wedges for a gift box is a different skill from cutting for the weekly market stand. A market customer forgives a ragged edge. A gift recipient, especially one who paid a premium for the box, notices. She's been practicing her cuts on wheels she'd otherwise sell as seconds, timing herself, and comparing waste percentages between a straight wire cut and a knife finish. Early on she was losing close to a tenth of a wheel to trim on her first attempts; by her fourth practice batch that was down to something she could live with.

Wrapping is its own problem. Paper that looks elegant on the counter can trap moisture against a washed rind in a way that ruins it by the time a gift box reaches someone three states away. She switched suppliers twice this fall before settling on a breathable wrap that still photographed well for the shop's holiday listings — a small decision that took longer than she expected and mattered more than she expected. She's since started keeping a one-page log of which wrap held up on which rind type, so she's not re-learning the same lesson next November.

The other adjustment was one of timing, not technique. She used to plan aging around when a wheel tasted right. This year she's planning backward from a ship date: a wheel meant for a mid-December box has to be cut, wrapped, boxed, and in a courier's hands by a date that has nothing to do with whether the paste is at its peak. That's meant pulling a few wheels a little earlier than she'd like and being honest with buyers about what "peak" means for a gift item versus a counter sale.

## Building the schedule backward

Export demand for domestic cheese has stayed strong all year, which is part of why some of her usual supplemental wholesale supply has been harder to lean on this fall — more of the broader market's output is going overseas instead of sitting in reserve for smaller retail programs. That's pushed her to plan her own inventory further ahead than she used to, rather than counting on a late top-up order if her holiday boxes sell faster than expected.

She's not alone in learning the packing side the hard way in a single season.
