Every winter we get a wave of questions from retail members about which credential is worth putting a counter staffer through, and Certified Cheesemonger is consistently the one people ask about first. That's reasonable — it's the credential most directly tied to what happens at the case, in front of a customer, in real time. It's also the one people most often misunderstand, so this week we're walking through what it actually tests.

## What's on it

Certified Cheesemonger is built around the skills a strong counter person uses every shift: identifying and describing cheese by style and origin story, matching a customer's stated preference to something on the case they haven't tried, handling storage and cut-and-wrap correctly enough that a $40 wedge doesn't dry out in three days, and knowing when a question is above your pay grade and needs to go to whoever manages the program. It leans practical rather than academic. You won't be asked to diagram a cheesemaking process from memory; you will be asked to talk a customer through a substitution when the thing they wanted sold out an hour ago.

That's a deliberate design choice, and it's also the biggest source of confusion. Members sometimes assume the credential certifies production knowledge — how a wheel was actually made — when it's really certifying retail competence: how well you can sell, store, and talk about cheese that someone else made. Those are related skills, but they're not the same skills, and conflating them sets the wrong expectations for staff going in.

## Who it's for, and who it isn't

If you run a counter with any staff turnover at all, Certified Cheesemonger gives you a baseline you can hire and train against instead of reinventing an onboarding checklist every time someone new starts. It's also a reasonable next step for a strong seasonal hire you want to keep — a credential on a resume is a cheap way to signal you're serious about the trade, even in a part-time retail role.

It is not a substitute for Food Safety & HACCP if you're running a plant, and it's not a substitute for Sensory Evaluation if what you actually need is someone who can grade a batch for quality control. "I put my first assistant maker through cheesemonger because it was the name I recognized," one retail member told us this week. "She came out great at talking to customers and no better at reading a vat, because that was never what the course covered." We hear a version of that story every year — members who put a production hire through the cheesemonger track only to discover it didn't touch the skills that hire actually needed on the floor. If you're not sure which credential matches which role, that's a two-minute conversation with the Education Committee, and it's worth having before you enroll anyone.

The credential doesn't require a prior certificate, which makes it a reasonable entry point if you're new to a formal credentialing path at all. Course enrollment runs through the member portal, and several members have told us the practical case-work sections land better once you've had at least a few months on an actual counter — it's easier to reason through a customer scenario when you've lived a version of it. If you're weighing it for a staff member this spring, the Education Committee keeps a short list of members willing to talk through what the exam day actually looks like.
