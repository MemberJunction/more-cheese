Most creameries hit their highest production volume of the year between now and the end of December, and that means more people, more product, and more equipment moving through your cave than at any other point on the calendar. It also means more chances to carry Listeria monocytogenes in on a boot, a cart wheel, or a wet apron than you'll see the rest of the year. Environmental monitoring programs are usually built and tested during calmer months, and a lot of members tell us theirs quietly falls apart the moment fall traffic picks up.

## Why this specific season is the risk

Listeria thrives in cool, damp conditions and survives refrigeration far better than most of the organisms your food-safety plan is built around. A cave that's humid enough to age wheels properly is also a cave where the organism, once introduced, doesn't die back the way it might on a dry surface elsewhere in your facility. Add holiday-season staffing — often a mix of your regular crew plus seasonal help who haven't internalized your zone boundaries yet — and you have more foot traffic crossing between raw and finished-product zones than your written plan probably accounts for.

The failure pattern we hear about most isn't a dramatic one. It's a seasonal hire who doesn't know that the boots for the receiving dock don't belong on the aging room floor, or a returning employee who reasonably assumes last year's zone map still matches this year's floor plan after a small equipment move. Neither is negligence. Both are exactly the kind of gap a fall refresher catches before a swab does.

## What a fall refresher should actually cover

A few members have shared what they walk seasonal and returning staff through before volume ramps up, and it's worth borrowing:

- Re-walk your zone map physically, not just on paper — stand at each doorway and confirm the boundary matches what's on the diagram, especially if you've moved a rack or added a second aging room this year.
- Reissue color-coded footwear or covers for anyone crossing zones, and actually check that the color coding is still intact; faded or swapped boot covers are a recurring finding in the environmental monitoring stories members send us.
- Increase drain and floor swab frequency for the highest-traffic weeks rather than holding to a flat monthly schedule — several members told us they shift to biweekly swabbing from November through the holiday rush and back off again in January.
- Walk new seasonal staff through what a positive environmental swab actually triggers at your facility, so nobody's improvising a response for the first time during your busiest week.

None of this replaces a real HACCP plan or your existing environmental monitoring program — it's a seasonal check on whether the plan you already have is actually being followed once the calendar gets crowded.

If your written plan hasn't been updated since your facility last changed shape, or if you're building an environmental monitoring program from scratch, the federation's *Listeria Control in Small Creameries* course walks through zone mapping, swab scheduling, and corrective-action documentation sized for operations without a dedicated quality department. It runs several times a year specifically because fall is when most members realize they need it. Reach out to the Food Safety Committee if you want a copy of a zone-map template before your own seasonal rush starts — a fifteen-minute walk-through now is a lot cheaper than a recall conversation in December.
