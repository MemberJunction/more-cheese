Federal trade data put U.S. cheese exports on a fourth straight month of growth this summer, and international trackers reported this week that global cheese prices have now risen for ten consecutive months, largely on a shrinking global milk supply. Neither number is dramatic on its own, but together they describe the same thing from two directions: demand for cheese is strong nearly everywhere at once, and supply isn't expanding fast enough to meet it. For members who spent the summer watching their own wholesale allocations get tighter, that combination probably feels less like good news and more like confirmation of something you already suspected: a meaningful share of this year's cheese is leaving the country, and the case at home is feeling it.

We've heard from a handful of mid-size producers this week asking a version of the same question. If export demand keeps climbing into the holiday season, should you be locking in your domestic wholesale contracts now, or is it worth holding back a slice of production to sell at a premium once inventory gets scarcer? There isn't a single right answer, and we're not going to pretend there is. But there is a pattern worth naming.

## What's actually driving the number

Export growth this year has come from a mix of things: sustained overseas demand for aged and semi-hard styles, a currency environment that's made U.S. product competitive, and buyers overseas rebuilding inventory after a couple of thin years. None of that traces back to any one company or trade body — it's a broad lift across the category, and it's compounding on top of feed and input costs that were already elevated heading into fall.

The effect for members is less about any single number and more about timing. Export contracts tend to get negotiated months ahead. If a distributor commits volume to an overseas buyer in September for a November shipment, that's cheese that isn't sitting in a domestic case in November — and your local retail account is going to notice the gap before you do. It's the same dynamic that's been showing up all year in the price data: strong demand meeting a supply chain that can't reroute itself overnight.

## What we'd suggest checking

A few members on our Membership & Outreach Committee's informal producer calls have started doing something simple: pulling their last three years of fall wholesale orders and comparing committed volume against what they actually had on hand to sell by October. If the gap has been widening each year, that's worth a conversation with your distributor before you're locked into a contract you can't fill.

If you're negotiating a new wholesale agreement this fall, ask your distributor directly whether they're also selling into export channels, and if so, how they prioritize allocation when both markets want the same aged wheels. It's a fair question, and a distributor worth keeping should have a straight answer for you.

We don't track pricing data ourselves, so we can't tell you what to charge. What we can say is that a wholesale case getting tighter for structural reasons, rather than a bad production year, is worth planning around rather than reacting to. If you want to talk through what a tightening domestic case might mean for your own fall production planning, the Membership & Outreach Committee is a good first call, and it's worth having that conversation before your contracts are signed rather than after.
