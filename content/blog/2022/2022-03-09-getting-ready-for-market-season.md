Most regional farmers-market seasons open within the next month or two, and if you're planning to sell direct this year — whether that's your first season at a stand or your tenth — now is the week to start the paperwork, not the week before opening day. Every member who's told us about a scramble in April traces it back to the same thing: assuming last year's permit would just carry over.

It usually doesn't. Permits for selling dairy products at a market are typically tied to a specific season, a specific location, or both, and many jurisdictions require a plan review or inspection before they'll reissue one — sometimes with a 30-day lead time built into the process. If your market runs in a county or city you haven't sold in before, budget even more time; a first-time mobile retail license review can take longer than a renewal.

## What to check this month

**Confirm your permit covers what you're actually selling.** A license for fluid milk and yogurt doesn't automatically cover cheese, and a cheese permit that covers pasteurized product doesn't automatically cover raw-milk cheese aged past the 60-day floor. Read the actual scope of your permit, not just its name.

**Check your market's own vendor requirements, separate from your state or county permit.** Many markets require their own vendor application, proof of insurance, and sometimes a market-specific food-safety review, on top of whatever your local health department requires. These often have earlier deadlines than the permit itself.

**Line up your cold-chain equipment before opening day, not the morning of.** A stand-side refrigeration failure on your first Saturday costs you more than a slow first weekend — it costs you a food-safety incident report and, potentially, standing with the market manager. Test it now, while you still have time to fix or replace it.

**If you're expanding to a new market this year, ask about local demand support.** Federal grant support for local and regional food marketing has expanded over the past year, and some of that funding flows through to individual markets or vendor cooperatives in the form of promotional support or reduced stall fees. It's worth asking your market manager directly whether any of that applies to you.

## Where to go deeper

The federation's **Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets** course walks through the full setup — permitting, stand layout, sampling rules, and the pricing conversations that come up differently at a market stand than they do with a wholesale buyer. It's a good refresher even if you've run a stand for years, since permit and sampling rules shift more often than most members expect.

And if this is your first season selling direct, talk to another member who's done it before you commit to a full market calendar. Almost every experienced stand operator we've talked to started with one market, not five, and added markets only once the first one was running smoothly. There's no prize for overcommitting in year one.

One more thing worth doing this month: call your market manager directly rather than relying only on the vendor packet. Packets get updated late, managers usually know about a schedule change or a new inspection requirement before it's written down anywhere, and a five-minute call now can save you a wasted trip in April.
