If you've been watching your milk check this year and thinking the number looks better than the rest of your books feel, you're not imagining it. Butterfat has been the story of 2022's dairy markets — premiums that ran well above normal for most of the year, with the components tied to fat content carrying a bigger share of the check than they have in a long time. For members who make higher-fat styles, that's real money. For members buying milk as an input, it's a cost line that's been climbing right alongside it.

The gap between "the milk check looks great" and "the year has been good" is exactly what we've heard from members most in the last few weeks. A strong headline price doesn't automatically turn into a strong margin, and this year has been an unusually clean demonstration of why. Retail prices for conventional milk this fall are running close to record levels too, which tells you the increase has moved through the whole chain — not stopped at the farm gate and not stopped at the creamery door either.

## Where the squeeze actually sits

For members running their own herds, the arithmetic is straightforward and not particularly comforting: two consecutive years of tighter feed markets, driven by weather that hasn't cooperated in several growing regions, have pushed input costs up faster than most producers can pass along. A milk check that's up on paper can still leave you flat once you've paid for hay, grain, and the diesel to move both.

For members buying milk to make cheese, the calculus is different but the pressure is similar. When the fat-heavy component of the price index runs hot, styles that lean on butterfat — triple crèmes, richer washed rinds, a lot of what shows well at the fall competition table — cost more to make than a straight yield calculation from a year ago would suggest. If your pricing model still uses last year's component ratios, it's worth pulling it out and checking the math against this year's numbers before you set fall wholesale prices.

## What we'd ask you to check

Two things, concretely. First, if you haven't repriced since spring, do it now — component costs have moved enough that a stale price sheet is probably underpricing your higher-fat lines specifically, not your whole catalog evenly. Second, if you're a producer-member, this is a good year to have an honest conversation with your feed supplier about contract terms for the year ahead, before winter buying season locks prices in again.

None of this is dramatic news — no single number here should surprise anyone who's been keeping half an eye on the market. But margins get eroded exactly this way: a little at a time, through a component nobody's line item is quite labeled for.
