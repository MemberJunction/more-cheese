Every member spotlight we've run on milk prices this spring has looked at the number from the outside — what it means for the case, what it means for a buyer's margin. This one is about the decision that comes before any of that: whether to sign.

A farmstead member who makes a washed-curd style for regional wholesale accounts told us she's spent most of May going back and forth on a supply contract that would lock in this year's milk price for twelve months. It sounds like an easy call when the number on offer is the best one she's seen in years. It isn't.

"Everybody's telling me to grab it while it's good," she said, "and on the milk side, sure. But I'm not just buying milk. My hay bill went up right along with everyone else's, my propane for the vat room is up, and if I lock a price on one side and my costs keep climbing on the other, I haven't actually protected anything — I've just fixed the number I'm least worried about."

Feed has been the real pressure point for producers like her this spring. Corn, soybean meal, and alfalfa have all moved up sharply since last year, and members who haven't already locked in their own feed supply are watching that side of the ledger just as closely as the milk price they're being offered. A milk contract signed in isolation, without a matching look at where feed and fuel costs are headed, tells only half the story.

## The part that doesn't show up in a market report

She keeps two ledgers now instead of one: the milk-price contract terms in one, and a running estimate of her own input costs in the other, updated monthly instead of at renewal. It's more paperwork than she used to do, and she's the first to say it's not a system she'd have built without a nudge from another member at a spring workshop who mentioned doing something similar.

That's the part of a record-price year that a market report can't capture — the actual decision happening at a kitchen table, weighed against costs that a headline number doesn't mention. A strong milk price is real relief for a producer who was underwater eighteen months ago. It is not, by itself, proof that this is a good year to take on new fixed costs, expand a herd, or sign a longer contract than usual. Members closer to the ground than the aggregate numbers keep making that distinction, and it's worth repeating: the milk check and the balance sheet are two different documents.

Her advice to other members weighing the same contract: don't evaluate the milk price alone. Pull your actual feed, fuel, and packaging costs from the same months and put the two numbers side by side before signing anything. If the spread between them is narrower than it was a year ago, that's the number that should be driving the decision, not the headline price.

If you're working through a similar contract decision this summer, the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with other producer members who've been comparing notes on exactly this question — not for financial advice, but for the kind of plain comparison that's hard to get anywhere else.
