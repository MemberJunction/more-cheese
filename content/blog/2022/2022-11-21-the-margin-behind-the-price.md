Retail whole milk has been sitting near a record high for months, and members who buy fluid milk as an input rather than sell it retail have been telling us all fall that the sticker price on the shelf is only half the story. The other half is what it costs to keep a herd fed and a truck moving, and that number has climbed longer and faster than most producers have been able to pass along.

## Why this fall feels different

The federal Dairy Margin Coverage program, which pays producers when the gap between milk price and feed cost narrows past a chosen threshold, has now triggered payments for ten months running this year — a streak long enough that it stopped being news to most members and started being background noise, the kind of number you check the way you'd check the weather. Ten straight months of a program built for margin emergencies quietly paying out isn't a one-quarter blip. It's the shape of the year.

Trade coverage this fall has carried the same story from a different angle: retail cheddar and whole milk prices have kept climbing even as other categories cool, which tells you the pressure on dairy specifically hasn't let up the way some members hoped it would by now. For a farmstead creamery buying milk rather than milking it, or a small operation supplementing its own herd's output during a lean stretch, that's not an abstraction — it's the line item that decides whether a holiday order actually pencils out.

## What we're hearing from members

A few farmstead members have told us they're locking in feed contracts earlier than usual this year rather than buying month to month, even at a premium, because the volatility itself has gotten more expensive than the average price. Others are holding off, betting that spring brings relief. Neither strategy is obviously right, and we're not going to pretend we can call it — but if you haven't already, this is a reasonable week to pull your own numbers and see which side of that bet you're actually on, rather than defaulting to whatever you did last year.

If you sell wholesale, this is also the stretch where a buyer's holiday order lands, and a few members flagged that retail accounts have been slower than usual to commit to volume this month — understandable, given they're watching their own margins compress from the other direction. If a buyer is hedging on your holiday order, that's worth a direct conversation now rather than a surprised phone call in December, when there's a lot less room to adjust either the volume or the price.

There's no membership-wide fix for a feed market that's tighter than anyone would like, and we're not going to pretend a newsletter changes what your next invoice looks like. What we can do is make sure you're not sizing the problem alone. The Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding more of these margin conversations than usual this fall, and if you want to compare notes with another producer who's sized the same problem, that's exactly the kind of introduction they make.

If your own numbers have you rethinking a contract before the year turns over, reach out before you sign anything — a second read from someone who bought feed under this year's conditions is worth more than it costs to ask.
