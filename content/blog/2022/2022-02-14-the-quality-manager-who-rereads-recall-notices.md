One member keeps a folder of recall notices going back years, sorted by cause rather than by date. Listeria in one column, mislabeled allergens in another, a smaller pile for temperature excursions in transit. As the quality manager at a mid-sized farmstead creamery, she is the person who reads the folder before anyone else on staff, and the person who decides which recall becomes a line item on next week's cleaning schedule.

"Most recalls aren't really about the thing that failed," she told us. "They're about the record that wasn't there when someone went looking for it. You can have a genuinely clean plant and still lose weeks explaining yourself because a log has a gap in it."

## Reading someone else's mistake for lessons

She's been a federation member for several years, long enough to remember when her creamery's cheese program was closer to a side project than a full operation. What changed her approach to quality management, she says, wasn't a single incident on her own line. It was watching other creameries work through theirs in real time, in the trade press and in ICF course-forum threads.

"You get better at this by studying other people's bad weeks," she said. "Somebody posts a question about a swab result they didn't expect, and half the room has an opinion. That's more useful to me than any single training module, because it's live and it's specific."

That habit shows up in how she runs her own operation now. Every recall notice that crosses her desk gets read twice: once for what happened, once for whether her own paperwork would hold up under the same question. If the answer is uncertain, it becomes a task before it becomes a liability.

## What she'd tell a newer quality manager

Milk supply has been tight again this winter — national dairy market reports this month show cheese production running slightly below the prior period even as demand holds — and a tighter supply chain tends to mean less room in a quality manager's week for anything that isn't strictly necessary. Her advice for members feeling that squeeze is blunt: don't let documentation be the thing that slides.

"When you're busy, the paperwork is the first thing that feels optional," she said. "It's also the first thing an inspector or a buyer is going to ask about. Keep the boring parts boring and consistent, and save your energy for the actual cheese."

She's not precious about where the discipline comes from. Some of it is a spreadsheet template she borrowed from a fellow member years ago and has never stopped using. Some of it is simply reading the recall notices, twice, and asking whether her own creamery could answer the same questions.
