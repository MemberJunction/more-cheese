Most of the country slows down this week. A member's aging room does not. Wheels still need turning, rinds still need washing, and a cave that drifts a few degrees warm because nobody checked it on a holiday doesn't forgive the lapse just because everyone was busy with a turkey. We talked to a member who runs affinage for a mid-sized creamery about what the week actually looks like from the inside of the cave rather than the dining room.

"People assume the cave runs itself," she told us. "It doesn't. It's the most consistent thing in the building, but consistent isn't the same as automatic — it's consistent because somebody's in there every single day making sure it stays that way, including the days nobody else is working."

## What doesn't pause

Her aging room holds wheels on rotations that range from a few weeks to over a year, and every one of them is on a schedule that doesn't care what the calendar says. A washed-rind wheel that's due for its rind treatment on Thanksgiving gets its rind treatment on Thanksgiving, holiday or not, because skipping a day on a schedule like that isn't a minor inconvenience — it's the kind of gap that shows up as an uneven rind or an off flavor months later, long after anyone remembers which day got skipped and why.

That means her holiday week actually involves more solo hours in the cave than a normal week, not fewer, since the rest of the crew is off and someone still has to do the walk-through. "I've made peace with it," she said. "It's twenty minutes, twice a day, checking humidity and turning what needs turning. The trade-off is I get the cave completely to myself, which some years is the calmest twenty minutes of my whole week."

## The part that's actually hard

The harder problem isn't the daily walk-through — it's the ambient shift that happens across the building this time of year as heating systems switch on for the season, which can quietly dry out a room that was perfectly calibrated in October. Most cave-aged wheels want to hold somewhere in a fairly narrow humidity band depending on style — a hard natural rind sits drier than a washed rind or a bloomy wheel wants — and the margin for error shrinks fast once a building's ambient air turns dry. As a rough rule of thumb, a larger aging room can need several gallons of added moisture a week through the coldest stretch of the year just to hold the line, and that number only goes up once the furnace is running around the clock instead of cycling on and off.

She said the first cold snap each November is when she pays closest attention, because a cave that's been humidity-stable all fall can drift dry fast once the building's heat kicks on in earnest, and a dry cave shows up on a rind before it shows up on any gauge you're checking — a wheel that's losing moisture too quickly will crack or crust before your hygrometer confirms anything is wrong. Her fix is unglamorous: more frequent misting, a humidifier reservoir she now checks twice a day instead of once, and a log she actually reads back over week to week instead of just recording numbers and moving on.

Her advice for members managing their own aging rooms through the holiday stretch is not complicated: don't assume this week's readings will match last week's just because nothing else changed in the recipe or the room. The building changed. Check the room like it's a new season, because for the cave, it is — and a rind that looks fine on Thanksgiving morning can look noticeably different by the following weekend if nobody adjusted for the season along the way.

If your own aging room needs a second pair of eyes before the holidays get busier, the Standards Committee maintains an informal list of members willing to do a remote consult on cave readings — a fresh perspective on your humidity log is often faster than troubleshooting alone.
