Late last month, the federal food-safety regulator published a final rule requiring certain high-risk foods to carry a formal set of tracking records from the farm to the retail counter. Fresh, unripened soft cheeses made the list — cottage-style, chèvre, cream cheese, ricotta, queso fresco, and their cousins. Aged and cave-ripened wheels are not on it. Frozen product is not on it either.

We have already heard from a dozen members this week asking whether this changes what they do on Monday morning. It does not, not yet, but it changes what your paperwork needs to be able to say in a few years, and the earlier you build the habit the less painful the deadline will feel.

## What the rule actually asks for

The core idea is a set of "key data elements" tied to specific "critical tracking events" — receiving milk, cheesemaking, packing, shipping. For a soft-cheese producer, that means being able to answer, for any batch, where the milk came from, when it was made, what lot it packed into, and who it shipped to. Most members already keep versions of this in a make sheet and a shipping log. The difference is the rule wants it in a consistent, retrievable format, not scattered across three notebooks and a spreadsheet someone else built.

The compliance runway is long, and the timeline has already shifted once since the rule was finalized, so we are not going to print a hard date here that could be wrong by the time you read this — check the Food Safety Committee's advisory page for the current deadline before you plan around it. What we can tell you is the direction: additional traceability records for foods on this list, soft cheese included, is where the regulator is headed, and members with aged-only product lines should not assume they are exempt forever if their line ever expands into fresh format.

Members should also know the list itself covers more than cheese — some fresh-cut produce, shell eggs, nut butters, and certain seafood are on it too. If your creamery also runs a small farm stand with fresh produce or eggs, this rule may touch you from two directions at once, not just the cheese side.

## Where the argument still has teeth

The comment record on this rule ran for more than two years before it was finalized, and small producers were a big part of it — testimony about lot-coding costs, about small-batch runs where "lot" and "day's make" are the same thing, about traceability software built for large processors rather than a two-person creamery. That advocacy did not stop the rule, but it shaped some of the flexibility built into how a lot can be defined for a small operation, and it is worth understanding if you make soft cheese and want to know why the recordkeeping expectations look the way they do.

The federation submitted comment during that process on behalf of members with fresh-cheese lines, arguing for a lot definition that matched how a small creamery actually batches product rather than how a plant does. Some of that language made it into the final rule's flexibility provisions; some of it did not. We will keep flagging developments here as the compliance date approaches or moves again.

If your operation touches anything on the fresh soft-cheese side — even a side project alongside your aged line — a few practical steps now will save you a scramble later. Start recording batch and lot numbers the same way every time, even before you are required to. Ask your distributor what tracking information they expect from you and compare it to what the rule actually requires; the two lists are not always the same. And if you have questions about whether your specific product falls inside or outside the traceability list, that is exactly the kind of question the Food Safety Committee exists to help members work through — reach out through the member portal.
