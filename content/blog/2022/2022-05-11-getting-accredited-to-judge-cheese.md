With in-person judging back on the calendar this year after two seasons of scaled-back competition, we've fielded more questions about the Competition Judge Accreditation than about any other credential this spring. Most of them boil down to one thing: members want to know whether they're actually ready, or whether wanting to judge and being qualified to judge are two different questions. They are, and that's the point of the credential.

## What the accreditation actually tests

The Competition Judge Accreditation isn't a rubber stamp for enthusiasm. It covers structured sensory scoring against defined category standards, so two judges looking at the same wheel of aged cheddar or the same wedge of soft-ripened triple-crème arrive at comparable scores rather than just comparable opinions. That means learning to separate a technical defect — an off paste, a cracked rind, an unbalanced acid-to-fat ratio — from a stylistic choice a maker deliberately went for. It also covers category boundaries, because misjudging where an entry belongs is one of the most common errors flagged in past competitions, and calibration exercises, where candidates score reference samples and compare their marks against a panel benchmark before they ever judge a live entry.

The credential builds on our Sensory Evaluation Certificate rather than replacing it. If you haven't taken that certificate yet, start there — the judge accreditation assumes you can already describe what you're tasting in specific, repeatable language before it asks you to score it against a rubric. It also assumes some comfort with a scoresheet under time pressure, since a real panel might work through dozens of entries in a category over the course of an afternoon, and a judge who lingers too long on any one wedge slows the whole table down.

Entries are up again this year as more small and mid-sized producers put product back into competition after a couple of quiet seasons, and categories are getting more crowded, particularly in soft-ripened and aged natural rind. More entries per category means judging panels need more accredited people, not fewer, and it means the standards those judges apply need to hold steady across a bigger, more varied field. A panel that's inconsistent this year doesn't just produce a confusing scoresheet — it produces results that makers stop trusting, and trust in the results is the entire point of entering.

If you're a maker rather than a prospective judge, the accreditation is still worth understanding, because it's the same rubric your entry gets scored against. Reading through it before you submit can save you from an entry that's technically excellent but filed in the wrong category, which happens more often than you'd think, especially as producers push into hybrid styles that don't map neatly onto a single traditional category.

## Getting started

The accreditation runs as a self-paced module followed by a scored calibration session, and the Awards & Competition Committee schedules calibration sessions in the weeks before each major competition rather than on a fixed calendar, so timing depends on when you want to be ready to judge. If you're aiming to be on a panel this season, reach out to the committee now rather than waiting until entries close — calibration sessions fill, and accreditation isn't something you can rush the week before. Members who went through it last year told us the calibration session itself was the part that changed how they tasted, not the self-paced module, so budget real attention for it rather than treating it as a formality.
