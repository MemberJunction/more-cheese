We heard about this one before we'd finished writing Wednesday's piece on price sheets, which tells you something about how many members are already down this road. A farmstead member who runs a small mixed herd operation with her husband spent most of August doing exactly the exercise we described — separating milk cost from everything else on her price sheet — and what she found surprised even her.

She'd assumed, like a lot of small producers, that the spring's record milk prices were the thing squeezing her hardest, and that this summer's pullback would bring some relief. Instead, when she actually broke her cost sheet into categories going back two years, milk turned out to be the smallest mover. Her packaging costs were up sharply, her propane for the aging room had climbed even before this winter's heating season starts, and a freight surcharge from her distributor had quietly become a fixed cost she'd never separately itemized.

## What changed once she could see it

"I'd been pricing off one number for years because it was the number everyone talked about," she told us. "Once I actually split it out, milk wasn't even the problem anymore. It was everything I'd been lumping in as 'overhead' and never looking at again." That's a common pattern — a flat overhead assumption works fine until enough of its components move at once, and this year several of them have.

The practical result was a repriced wholesale sheet that added a modest per-wheel increase tied specifically to packaging and energy, itemized separately from the milk-cost line so she can adjust each independently going forward. She's also started asking her distributor for a breakdown of what portion of her freight bill is fuel surcharge versus base rate, which is a conversation she says she wouldn't have known to have a year ago.

## The part worth borrowing

You don't need a farmstead operation or a two-person creamery to get something out of this. The habit that mattered here wasn't a specific number — it was refusing to let one visible, widely-discussed cost (milk) stand in for everything else that's actually moving. If your last price adjustment was keyed entirely to a milk cost index, this is a good week to pull your own invoices and see what else has changed since you last looked.

If you want a structured version of this exercise rather than a spreadsheet built from scratch, Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese runs from members' own cost data and is the same course our Wednesday post pointed to — worth a look whether you're a two-person farmstead or a retail counter with a very different cost structure.

What makes her situation useful as an example isn't that it's dramatic — it's that it's ordinary. A two-person farmstead operation, a decade of pricing off the same formula, no crisis forcing the change. She just happened to sit down with her invoices at the right moment, partly because a neighboring producer had mentioned doing the same thing at a summer market. That's often how these habits actually spread among members: not through a webinar or a certification, but through one producer telling another what she found when she finally looked.

She's planning to repeat the exercise every quarter going forward rather than waiting for another price swing to prompt it, and she's mentioned it to a few other members in her region who are now doing the same on their own price sheets. If you're one of them, or you want to be, the committee and course above are both there — but honestly, so is just opening last year's invoices and starting to sort them into three piles.
