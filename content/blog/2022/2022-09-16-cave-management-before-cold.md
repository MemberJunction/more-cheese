Late September is when a lot of caves start acting up. The outside air is cooler and drier than it was in August, but not consistently so — you might get a warm, humid week right after a cold snap — and that inconsistency is harder on a maturation room than a steady summer heat wave ever was. Most cheese styles hold their target range somewhere between 50 and 55 degrees Fahrenheit and 80 to 95 percent relative humidity, with harder natural-rind styles wanting the drier end of that band and washed-rind or bloomy styles wanting the wetter end. If your compressor and humidifier have been running on the same settings since June, this is a good week to revisit them.

That's the exact problem our Cave Management course was built to walk through, and it's worth a look whether you're new to affinage or you've been running a cave for years and just want to sanity-check your fall routine.

## Why fall is harder than it looks

In summer, most caves are fighting one direction of drift: too warm, too humid, all the time. The fix is usually just "run the equipment harder." Fall is trickier because the drift reverses itself over a matter of days. A room that's fine on Tuesday can be uncomfortably dry by Friday if an early cold front pulls moisture out of the air your intake is drawing from. Hold the air too dry for too long and rinds crack while the cheese loses weight and flavor it can't get back. Hold it too wet, on the other hand, and you get slick rinds, slip skin, and mold you didn't ask for.

The course spends real time on this, not just target humidity and temperature ranges, but how to read a trend line on your data logger and catch a drift before it shows up on a wheel. It also covers the mechanical side that's easy to ignore until something fails: when to service a humidifier before it's needed daily again, how to check door seals that loosened over a summer of frequent opening, and how to set up a monitoring routine that flags a problem overnight instead of the next time someone happens to walk through the room.

The most common mistake we hear about isn't a bad piece of equipment, it's a good piece of equipment left on a setting that made sense for a different season. A humidifier dialed in for August's baseline humidity will often run too little in a drier October, and by the time someone notices the rinds are cracking, the affected wheels are already a couple of weeks into the damage. The course teaches a simple habit that solves most of this: reset your equipment's target range at each seasonal changeover rather than only adjusting when something already looks wrong.

It also covers which styles are more forgiving of a fall transition and which aren't. Semi-hard styles tend to tolerate a wider humidity swing without much visible effect, which makes them a reasonable one to practice on if you're new to reading trend data. Soft, bloomy, and washed-rind styles are far less forgiving, and the course spends extra time on monitoring routines for exactly those cheeses.

We built this course for the member who's outgrown "check the cave twice a day and adjust by feel" but isn't ready to hand the whole system over to an automated controller, or who has one and doesn't fully trust it yet.
