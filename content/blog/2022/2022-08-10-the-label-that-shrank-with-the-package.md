A different kind of question has been showing up in the Membership & Outreach Committee's inbox this month, alongside the usual renewal and course questions: what happens to your net-weight labeling when the wedge itself gets smaller.

With packaging and raw-material costs up sharply this year, some members are trimming portion sizes rather than raising a shelf price outright — a wheel that used to finish at nine pounds now finishing closer to eight and a half, a retail wedge trimmed by an ounce or two. That's a legitimate business decision, and it's not one unique to cheese: reporting this summer found the same quiet resizing happening across the grocery aisle, from tissue boxes to snack bags, as manufacturers absorbed rising costs by adjusting what's inside the package rather than the price on the shelf tag. It is also, several members have reminded us, one that only works if the label on the package is honest about what changed.

## What the rules actually require

Net-weight labeling standards exist for exactly this situation, and they're not optional or a matter of house style: the declared weight on the package has to reflect the actual weight in it, full stop, regardless of whether the recipe, the mold, or the price changed since the last print run. The requirement covers where the statement sits on the package, how prominent it has to be, and that it can't be padded out with nonfunctional empty space in the container to make a smaller product look larger on a shelf. A label that was accurate in March and hasn't been updated since a portion change in July is a labeling problem, not a pricing one, and it's the kind of thing an inspector will flag before a customer ever notices.

The federation isn't asking anyone to hold a price at a loss. We're asking members who are resizing product this year to treat the label reprint as part of that decision, not an afterthought that gets caught up with next quarter's other packaging changes. If your printer run is on a quarterly cycle and your portion size changed mid-cycle, that's a gap worth closing sooner rather than later. "We didn't think of the label as part of the pricing decision until a wholesale buyer asked us about it directly," one member selling into regional grocery told the Membership & Outreach Committee this month. "By then we'd already shipped two runs with the old weight printed on them."

## What we're doing about it

The Standards Committee has been compiling member questions on this into a single reference on current net-weight labeling requirements, since the same question — how precise the declared weight actually needs to be for a hand-cut wedge that varies wheel to wheel — has come up from enough different members this month that it's clearly not an isolated case. We expect to have that out to members before the next quarterly newsletter, along with a short checklist for anyone resizing a product mid-cycle.

In the meantime, if you're resizing a product this year, loop in whoever handles your label proofs before the change goes to print, not after. The Membership & Outreach Committee can also connect you with members who've been through the same resizing decision recently and can tell you what actually tripped them up — most of it, so far, has been timing rather than the math itself.
