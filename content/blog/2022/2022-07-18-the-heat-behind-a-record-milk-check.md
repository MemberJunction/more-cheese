If your milk check this month looks better than it has in years, you're not imagining it. Class III prices have been climbing steadily through the summer, and members tell us they haven't seen numbers like this since before most of us can remember. The catch, as always, is what's sitting underneath the number.

Two things are happening at once. First, feed and input costs have kept climbing right alongside the milk price, so a bigger check doesn't automatically mean a bigger margin — several members on our advocacy calls this month have said their actual take-home is flatter than the headline price suggests. Second, and this is the part we want to flag now rather than after your next order comes up short, a stretch of unusually hot weather across several major dairy regions has been knocking down herd yields just as cheese demand hits its summer peak.

Heat stress is not a new problem, but it compounds in ways that are easy to underestimate. Cows eat less, drink more, and produce less milk per head when the thermometer stays high for days at a time — and per-cow output is exactly the number a small or mid-sized creamery can't make up for by simply buying more cows. If you source milk locally, ask your supplier now, not in August, whether they're seeing component drops as well as volume drops. Butterfat and protein content tend to dip under sustained heat before volume does, and that shows up in your yield per vat before it shows up anywhere else.

## What this means for your case

For members running a retail case or a wholesale account list, tighter milk supply on top of already-elevated prices usually shows up as slower restocking and firmer wholesale quotes, not empty shelves. It's worth having a conversation with your buyers now about what a delayed shipment looks like so nobody is caught off guard in the next few weeks. A few members in the Membership & Outreach Committee's regional calls have already started building a short buffer into their order timing, and it's a reasonable hedge if your cash flow allows it.

We'd also gently push back on the instinct to treat a record price as pure good news. Several of you have told us that this year's margins, once you net out feed, transport, and labor, are thinner than the topline price suggests — a pattern that shows up across regions, not just in one climate zone. If you haven't run your numbers since spring, this is a good week to do it again. A milk price chart tells you what buyers are paying; it doesn't tell you what you're keeping.

There's also a wrinkle for anyone bringing in cheese or cultures from overseas this summer. A separate heat wave affecting parts of Europe has been straining regional power grids, and members who import aged or affinage-dependent product have mentioned longer lead times as suppliers manage energy costs at their aging facilities. If that's part of your supply chain, build in a little slack before you commit to a fall order.

None of this is a crisis — it's a squeeze, and squeezes are manageable if you see them coming. If you want to talk through what it means for your specific supply chain, the Membership & Outreach Committee holds open office hours most months, and we're always glad to compare notes with what other members are seeing in their own regions. Given how differently this is landing region to region, that comparison might be the most useful thing we can offer you this week.
