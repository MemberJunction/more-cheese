If your milk check looked smaller this month than it did in May, you weren't imagining it. Class III has retreated from its spring peak, and Class IV has slipped a little too, off slightly higher milk production and an economy that's giving domestic cheese and butter demand a harder time than it did earlier in the year. Cheddar block prices have leveled out rather than kept climbing, and the dry whey market has eased from where it sat in the spring. Butter has bounced around a similar band all summer rather than setting new highs.

For members who spent the first half of the year pricing off a record-setting market, that's worth a second look at your sheet — not because the market has collapsed, it hasn't, but because a plateau after a run-up changes what "normal" means for the rest of the year. A price sheet built in April, when the milk market was still climbing, is not the same price sheet you want walking into September.

## The part that isn't cooling

Here's the number that matters more than the milk check right now: input costs kept climbing well after the milk market itself turned over, and several members tell us packaging and raw-material costs specifically are up somewhere between 15 and 57 percent over last year, depending on what they're buying and from whom. Energy for pasteurizing, cooling, and running a cave hasn't gotten any cheaper either. So a milk price that's finally leveling off doesn't translate into breathing room if everything downstream of the vat kept climbing while the milk price was still up there. That's the squeeze worth watching this fall: a top-line number that looks like relief on a market report, and a bottom line that doesn't feel like it.

Specialty and artisan cheese sales have kept growing through this stretch even as the broader dairy case has softened, which tracks with what a lot of our members in direct and specialty retail have told us this summer: shoppers trading up rather than down when they do buy cheese, even while cutting back elsewhere in the cart. That's not a guarantee it holds through a fall with a shakier economy behind it, but it's the trend line worth banking on if your case leans artisan rather than commodity. If you sell primarily wholesale into a softening commodity market, the calculus is different, and the Standards Committee has heard from more than one member this month asking how to talk to buyers about holding a price when the headline number on the market report is falling. "The market report doesn't know what I paid for boxes in June," one longtime wholesale-focused member put it to us bluntly. There's no template answer for that conversation, but knowing your actual input costs — not the spring ones — is the first thing you'll need walking in.

## What to actually check this week

Three things are worth pulling before you talk to a single buyer: your current cost per finished wheel, including packaging and energy, not just milk; the last date your price sheet was updated relative to the last date your packaging invoice changed; and whether the softening commodity market is actually reaching your specific buyers yet, or whether they're still working off contracts written closer to the spring peak. That last one determines how much room you actually have to negotiate.

Pull your numbers this week if you haven't. A market that's stopped climbing is still a market you have to reprice for, and the members who come out of this fall in the best shape will be the ones who separated the milk-price story from the cost story instead of assuming they moved together.
