This week's Birch Alpine Styles workshop drew the room we'd hoped for when we scheduled it: a mix of members who make washed-rind and tomme-style wheels for a living, and members whose job is getting those wheels onto trucks and across a border. That second group showed up in bigger numbers than past Alpine sessions, and it wasn't a coincidence.

U.S. cheese has had a strong export year, and a meaningful share of what's leaving the country is headed south rather than across the Atlantic — Mexico alone has taken up more than a quarter of total U.S. cheese exports over the last several months. That's changed the conversation at a workshop like this one. It's no longer only "how do I get a European affinage style right," it's also "how do I make one that travels well and clears paperwork cleanly on the other end." Both questions got real time on the agenda.

## The cave floor conversation

The hands-on portion covered rind development and humidity management for Alpine-style wheels, the same technical core this workshop always teaches. What was different this year was how much of the Q&A drifted toward logistics: cave-to-truck temperature handoffs, how long a wheel can sit at a border facility before rind quality suffers, and how to document aging conditions in a way that satisfies a receiving country's paperwork without slowing your own process down.

One member working the affinage side put it plainly during the afternoon session: a beautiful wheel that arrives soft at the edges because of a six-hour delay in an unrefrigerated bay is not a beautiful wheel anymore, it's a returned shipment. "You can do everything right in the cave," she said, "and still lose the wheel in a warehouse bay you never see." That's not a knock on the craft instruction — it's a reminder that for members shipping internationally, the cave is only half the job.

A distributor-side member added a smaller, practical note that got several people writing: keep a simple temperature log template on hand for every load, even short regional runs, because the first thing a receiving inspector asks for is rarely the thing a shipper thought to bring. That kind of paperwork friction isn't imagined — federal licensing rules for how dairy import quotas get administered were updated again just last month, a reminder that the compliance side of cross-border cheese keeps moving even when the recipe doesn't.

## Where this connects to the rest of your year

If you make Alpine styles and haven't thought hard about your export packaging and cold-chain documentation, this is a good week to start, while the workshop conversations are still fresh. The Affinage Fundamentals course covers the aging side in depth; for the logistics side, your regional distributor contact through the federation's directory is the fastest way to compare notes with someone who's already solved the border-timing problem you're about to run into.

For members who came for the craft and skipped the export conversation, that's a fine reason to attend too — not every wheel needs to cross a border, and the humidity and rind-care fundamentals taught this week apply just as much to a wheel headed to your own retail case. We'll have workshop materials posted to the member portal within the week for anyone who wants to review the session outside the room.

Our thanks to everyone who traveled for this one, especially the members who came further than usual to be in the room for the export-side conversation. That combination is exactly what we hoped this workshop would become, and we'll be building more logistics content into next year's Alpine session based on how much of the room leaned into it this time.
