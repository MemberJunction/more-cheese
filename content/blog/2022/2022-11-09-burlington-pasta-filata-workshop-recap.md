Tuesday's Workshop: Hollow Pasta Filata in Burlington, Vermont filled every hands-on station well before the registration deadline, and the room stayed full for the entire day — a good sign for a technique that's notoriously hard to teach from a slide deck. Pasta filata cheeses live or die on feel: the stretch, the temperature window, the moment curd goes from rubbery to workable to blown out. You can describe that in a handout. You can't really teach it without hot water, curd, and someone standing next to you telling you to slow down.

## What the day actually covered

The workshop split roughly in half between stretch mechanics and troubleshooting. The morning session worked through curd pH and moisture targets before stretch, since members who've struggled with pasta filata almost always trace the problem back to starting the stretch too early or too late rather than anything about the stretching itself. Several attendees told us afterward that watching a curd sample get stretched at the wrong pH — and seeing it tear instead of ribbon — did more for their understanding than any amount of written explanation had.

The afternoon moved into the failure modes members had actually brought with them: cheese that skins over before it firms, wheels with a hollow or spongy center, batches that stretch fine in the workshop's water bath but behave differently back in a home cave with different water hardness and mineral content. The instructor worked through several of these case by case, which is exactly the kind of troubleshooting that's hard to get from a written course and one of the clearer arguments for showing up to an in-person workshop over a recorded one.

## What members said they're taking home

A few attendees told us the single most useful part of the day wasn't the technique demonstration at all — it was standing next to three other small producers who'd hit the same hollow-center problem and hearing how differently they'd each tried to fix it before landing on the same answer the instructor gave. That kind of peer comparison is part of why the federation keeps running these as in-person, hands-on sessions rather than shifting everything to video: a stretch you can feel with your own hands, and a room full of people who've fought the same batch, teach things a recorded demo can't.

One recurring theme in the feedback: several members asked whether a follow-up session focused specifically on scaling stretched-curd batches from a hobby-sized pot to a wholesale-sized vat would be useful. We don't have that on the calendar yet, but it's the kind of request the Education Committee weighs when planning next year's workshop slate, and member interest is exactly the signal that moves a topic from "maybe" to "scheduled."

If you couldn't make it to Burlington, the federation runs regional workshops across the calendar year specifically so a hands-on session isn't a once-a-year opportunity tied to the annual conference. If pasta filata, or another stretched-curd style, is part of your production and you weren't able to make Tuesday's session, keep an eye on the events calendar for the next regional slot, and reach out to the Education Committee if a particular technique or troubleshooting topic keeps coming up in your own cave. Workshop topics get built from exactly that kind of member feedback, and a session that fills its hands-on stations early — the way Tuesday's did — is usually a good argument for running it again somewhere else.
