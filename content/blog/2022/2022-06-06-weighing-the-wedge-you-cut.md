Most of the advocacy work the federation does on labeling standards never becomes a headline, and that's mostly by design — the goal is usually to keep a workable rule workable, not to fight a losing battle against a rule that's already decided. A comment period open right now is a good example of the quieter kind of advocacy that matters just as much.

At issue is how net-weight tolerances get calculated for cheese sold as a random-weight item — the wedge cut to order at a counter, or the wheel a small creamery ships direct to a handful of retail accounts, where the exact weight isn't known until the piece is cut and can't be marked on a label ahead of time. Extension guidance for direct farm marketers has spent real time this spring walking through what state weights-and-measures inspectors actually check on these items, and it's more granular than most small producers expect: scale certification, tare weight, and how a price-per-pound sticker has to be calculated once a piece is weighed at the counter.

The proposal under review would apply the same statistical sampling method currently used for high-volume prepackaged product to these smaller, hand-cut lots. On paper, that sounds like consistency. In practice, a sampling method built around thousands of identical packages a day behaves very differently when it's applied to a batch of forty wedges cut by one person on a Tuesday.

## Why the batch size matters

A large commercial packager can absorb the occasional out-of-tolerance package inside a run of ten thousand without much risk of a compliance flag, because the sampling math evens out. A small creamery cutting to order doesn't get that averaging effect — a handful of wedges running a little heavy or light against a stated weight, out of a much smaller total, can look statistically worse on paper even if the actual cutting practice hasn't changed at all. The rule, if adopted as written, wouldn't ban hand-cutting. It would just make small lots look riskier under the same math built for a different kind of operation, and it would land hardest on exactly the direct-to-consumer sellers the extension guidance is trying to help.

The federation's comment on this proposal, filed through the standard channel available to any member with a stake in the outcome, has asked for a batch-size adjustment to the sampling method rather than a blanket exemption — a position several members helped shape after comparing notes on how their own cutting and labeling practices actually work day to day. That's the pattern worth naming: the strongest comments in a docket like this one aren't abstract objections. They're specific descriptions of an actual counter, an actual scale, and an actual Tuesday.

If you cut and label cheese to order and this proposal would touch your practice, the comment period is still open, and testimony from members doing exactly this kind of hand-cutting carries real weight precisely because there isn't much of it in the docket yet. The Standards Committee can point you to the filing and a short template for describing your own process — not to write the comment for you, but to make sure it lands on the part of the rule that actually affects you.
