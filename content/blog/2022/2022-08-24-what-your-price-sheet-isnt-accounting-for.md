We've written twice this month about milk and block prices coming down off their spring records. What we haven't spent enough time on is why that pullback isn't showing up in most members' margins the way it should — and it's not because anyone did the math wrong.

Cheddar blocks eased through July after peaking earlier in the year, and Class III milk has followed the same downward curve. On a spreadsheet built around milk cost alone, that reads as good news. But packaging, freight, and energy inputs haven't moved in the same direction. Several members in our Pricing & Margin course this cohort reported cardboard and film costs up in the range of 15 to 30 percent year over year, and a couple of small-plant members told us their electric bills for cave climate control were the single largest line-item increase they'd seen in a decade of running the same equipment.

## Why the milk number isn't the whole picture

A price sheet built two or three years ago probably has a formula that ties your wholesale or retail price to a milk cost index, with everything else folded into a flat overhead assumption. That flat assumption is exactly what's breaking right now. If your packaging and energy costs have both risen by double digits while your formula only reacts to milk, you're absorbing that gap somewhere — usually in a margin number nobody's looked at closely since the spring.

The fix isn't complicated, but it does take an afternoon: pull your last four quarters of packaging invoices and utility bills, line them up against your milk cost for the same periods, and see whether your price formula has actually kept pace with total input cost or just milk cost. Members who've done this exercise in Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese this cohort have mostly found the same thing — a gap of a few percentage points that's been quietly eating margin since spring while the milk number told a more comforting story.

## Where to take this next

If you've never separated "milk cost" from "everything else" on your price sheet, that's the single most useful change you can make this quarter, and it's the first module in Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese. The course runs in small cohorts and works from your own numbers rather than a hypothetical creamery, so what you leave with is a formula you can actually use, not a worksheet you'll forget by October.

And if you're a retail counter rather than a producer, the same logic applies to your wholesale cost, your case losses, and your labor — the milk number is only one input among several that have all been moving this year, and it's rarely the one doing the most damage to your margin.

None of this requires new software or an accountant on retainer. A simple three-column worksheet — milk cost, packaging and energy, everything else — run against the last eight quarters is usually enough to see whether your price formula has kept pace with your actual costs or just with the headline you've been reading. Members who've done this tell us the exercise takes less time than they expected and surfaces at least one cost they'd stopped noticing entirely, because it had crept up in small increments rather than one visible jump.

The other thing worth flagging: this isn't a one-time fix. Energy costs in particular have been volatile enough this year that a formula built in August may already need revisiting by winter, when heating and cave climate control costs typically rise again. Building the habit of a quarterly review, rather than treating this as a single correction, is what actually protects your margin over the full artisan season rather than just through the current price dip.
