Every few weeks, someone asks our membership desk the same question in a slightly different form: can I ship this cheese to a customer in another state? The honest answer depends almost entirely on one number — how many days the wheel has aged — and it trips up more members than you'd expect, including some who have been shipping for years.

Federal rule treats raw milk itself differently from cheese made with it. Raw fluid milk cannot move across state lines for sale, full stop. Cheese made from unpasteurized milk is a narrower exception: it can cross state lines only if it has been aged at least sixty days at a temperature not less than 35°F and is clearly labeled as unpasteurized. Miss either condition — the sixty days, the labeling — and a wheel that's perfectly legal to sell at your own farm stand becomes a federal violation the moment a delivery truck crosses a state line.

## Why this keeps surfacing as an advocacy question

The rule itself hasn't changed recently, but the conditions around it keep shifting in ways that make members nervous about their own compliance. Direct-to-consumer sales have grown steadily in the last couple of years, and a wheel that was always going to be sold locally is now, for some members, a wheel that might end up in a cooler bound for another state. That's exactly the moment a sixty-day clock stops being an abstraction and starts being a business decision.

State rules layer on top of the federal floor and vary more than most members assume. Some states restrict raw-milk cheese sales further regardless of age; a handful treat the federal sixty-day standard as the ceiling of what they'll allow even for cheese made and sold entirely in-state. We hear from members who assumed their home state's rule matched the federal one and found out otherwise only after a shipment got flagged.

## What we're asking for, and what you can do

The federation's position, consistent with what members have told us through comment letters and committee calls, is not to relitigate the sixty-day standard itself but to push for clearer, more consistently published guidance on how it interacts with state law — so a small producer can find an answer without hiring a food-law attorney first. We've submitted comments along those lines before and expect to again this year as relevant dockets open.

If interstate shipment is part of your business or something you're considering, two things are worth doing now. First, confirm the actual age of any lot you're shipping against a dated make record, not a guess — "about two months" isn't a defense if a regulator asks. Second, check your destination state's own rules before you check the federal ones, since state law is often the tighter constraint. Cheese production and milk supply have both been running tighter than usual this winter, which only raises the stakes of a shipment getting held up or rejected over a paperwork gap rather than an actual quality problem.

The Standards Committee tracks state-by-state raw-milk shipping rules as members report them, and we'll keep that resource updated as the picture changes. If you've run into a state rule that surprised you, tell us — it helps us know where the next comment letter needs to focus.
