If your input costs have moved twice since January, you're not imagining it. Federal market
reporting has had milk running well above where it sat a year ago, production is down slightly
on fewer cows and tighter feed supplies, and energy markets have gotten noticeably jumpier in
the last week alone. None of that is news to anyone who's looked at a hay invoice lately. What's
less obvious is how to move your wholesale or counter price in response without training your
best customers to wait for a sale.

That's the exact problem *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* was built to walk through, and
with a new cohort opening in the spring, this feels like the right week to talk about what's
actually in it.

## Cost-plus is a floor, not a strategy

The course starts where most producers already are: cost-plus math, where you tally milk,
labor, packaging, and overhead, add your target margin, and land on a number. That's necessary
and most members are already doing some version of it. But cost-plus alone tends to produce
prices that lag reality — you're always pricing last month's costs into this month's wheel,
and in a quarter like this one, "last month" and "this month" aren't close.

The course's second half is where the more useful work happens: building a small buffer into
your cost model so a feed-price swing doesn't force an emergency reprice every six weeks, and
learning to separate a genuine cost increase from a temporary spike you can absorb for a cycle.
Not every uptick belongs on the price tag immediately. Some do. Telling the difference is a
skill, not a formula, and it's the part members say is hardest to learn alone.

### Raise the number without losing the story

The other half of repricing is the conversation, not the math. A retail customer who's paid the
same price for two years and sees a jump needs a reason, and "our costs went up" is true but
thin. The course spends real time on this: a short shelf card or counter line that names what
changed — feed costs, a longer aging schedule, a smaller batch this season — does more for
customer retention than silence followed by a surprise. Members who've priced this way well
report fewer complaints than the ones who just changed the number and hoped nobody noticed.

For wholesale accounts, the advice is blunter: give your buyer notice, not an invoice surprise.
A four-to-six-week heads-up on a price change, with the reason attached, keeps most accounts
from shopping around — the course includes a short template letter members have adapted for
exactly that call.

What it covers, plainly:

The course does not touch anything the federation can't speak to. It doesn't tell you what your
dues should fund a margin calculator with, and it won't give you a target number — every
creamery's cost base is different, and a course that handed out a universal price would be
handing out bad advice. What it gives you is the framework: how to build a cost model that
updates instead of one you rebuild from scratch every time hay gets expensive, and language for
the price conversation that doesn't sound defensive.

If your last reprice happened by instinct rather than by model, the spring cohort is a good
place to start building the model instead. Check the course calendar for the next enrollment
window, and if you've already taken it and want the refresher materials, the Education
Committee keeps the current version on file.
