Online cheese sales have been climbing for two years now, and a lot of members who started shipping direct to consumers out of necessity in 2020 have kept it up because it works. Direct-to-consumer food and beverage sales have kept growing right through this year, and cheese has ridden that wave along with everything else in the specialty grocery aisle. What we hear less about, until it becomes a problem, is that the rule set doesn't travel with the cheese. A wheel that's fully compliant to sell out of your own case can hit a different labeling requirement, a different weights-and-measures rule, or a different registration threshold the moment it crosses into a neighboring state's mailbox.

This isn't a new problem, but it's a growing one, because the volume of interstate direct-to-consumer shipping has grown faster than most small creameries' compliance habits have. A member who ships a dozen orders a month can absorb the occasional rule they didn't know about. A member who ships two hundred is running real exposure if their labels, disclosures, or registration status don't match what the receiving state expects.

## Where the patchwork actually bites

The clearest example is labeling. Several states require disclosures on refrigerated shipped foods — hold-temperature guidance, "keep refrigerated" language, sometimes a specific minimum font size — that go beyond what a package needs to carry to sell over your own counter. A label built to satisfy your home state's inspector doesn't automatically satisfy the state your customer lives in. There's also a registration layer: some states treat an out-of-state creamery shipping directly to a resident as its own kind of food facility, requiring a filing separate from anything you've already done at home.

None of this is about raw milk or the sixty-day aging rule we've written about before — that's a federal question with a fixed, well-established answer. This is a state-by-state patchwork sitting on top of it, and it applies just as much to a pasteurized cheddar shipped in a cold pack as it does to anything aged.

Packaging adds a second layer on top of labeling. Foam liners, gel packs, and insulated cartons that hold a safe temperature through a one-day regional shipment often aren't enough for a three-day cross-country one, and a member who scales up their shipping radius without revisiting their packaging spec is trading a labeling risk for a spoilage risk instead.

## What we're asking for, and what to do now

Our Membership & Outreach Committee has been collecting examples of states where the same shipment would need two different labels depending on destination, and we're using that to make the case — through coalition conversations with other food-and-agriculture trade groups — for a more uniform disclosure standard for direct-shipped perishable foods. That's a slow process, and we're not going to pretend otherwise. Rulemaking on labeling standards moves in years, not months, and a coalition ask carries more weight with more members' shipping records behind it.

In the meantime, if you ship direct to consumers in more than two or three states, it's worth a call to our office before you assume last year's label template still covers you. We can point you to members who've already mapped their own multi-state shipping compliance, and to the specific states where we've had questions come in recently. A mismatched label is a fixable problem if you catch it before a shipment goes out — much less fixable after a customer in another state files a complaint with their own state's regulator.

If you've already been through this exercise for your own creamery, we'd like to hear about it too. The clearest examples in our advocacy file so far have come from members who ran into a specific state's rule the hard way and were willing to share what they learned. That's how we build a case that a regulator or a legislative committee will actually take seriously, rather than a general complaint about paperwork.
