January has been a strange month on the wholesale side. Cheese and butter prices climbed steadily through the first three weeks, enough that a few members told us they were bracing to raise their own numbers, and then the last week of the month gave a good chunk of that gain back — butter dropped sharply in a matter of days, and block and barrel cheese both eased off their highs. If your price sheet only gets revisited once a quarter, this is exactly the kind of month it's built to miss.

We're not going to tell you whether the correction sticks or whether wholesale climbs back before spring — nobody has that answer with any confidence right now, and the honest thing to say is that dairy commodity markets have been unusually jumpy for the better part of a year. What we can talk about is how to build a pricing process that doesn't leave you either underpriced after a spike or stuck defending a number the market has already walked away from.

## Separate your cost floor from your market ceiling

The members who weather months like this best tend to track two numbers, not one: the actual cost of a wheel — milk, cultures, labor, aging space, packaging — and a separate read on what the wholesale market is doing that week. The cost number moves slowly and should set your floor. The market number moves fast and should inform where you sit above that floor, not replace the floor entirely. If you're pricing purely off wholesale comparables, a swing like January's can pull your own number down below what the wheel actually cost you to make, especially on anything that's been aging for months and carrying that cost the whole time.

A rolling four-week average of your relevant wholesale benchmark, rather than the latest print, smooths out exactly the kind of whipsaw this month produced without making you slow to react to a real, sustained move. It's a small change to how often you look at the number, and it keeps a single volatile week from rewriting your whole spring price list.

## Where this gets taught properly

This is the core of what the Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course covers, and it's aimed squarely at makers and small counters who've been pricing off instinct and a competitor's shelf tag rather than their own cost structure. The class builds a real cost floor from your own inputs, walks through how to layer a market read on top of it without losing that floor, and covers when it actually makes sense to hold a price through a dip versus adjust with it.

If January's swing left you second-guessing a number you set in early January, that's a reasonable moment to take the course rather than wait for the next volatile month to catch you the same way. Cohorts run several times a year — check the course catalog on the member portal for the next one, and bring your own cost numbers if you can; the class is more useful with real data in front of you than with a hypothetical wheel.
