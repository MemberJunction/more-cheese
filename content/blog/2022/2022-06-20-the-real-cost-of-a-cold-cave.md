Most of the dairy news this year has been about the milk check, and there's plenty of reason for that. Feed costs have climbed sharply since 2020, and this summer they're sitting at levels several members have described as unlike anything they've budgeted for before. But when we asked our Membership & Outreach Committee what's actually coming up in member calls this month, feed wasn't the first thing mentioned. The aging room was.

Cave climate control is not a small system on a small operation. Holding a room at 52 to 55 degrees with 85 to 95 percent humidity, month after month, for wheels that won't be ready to sell for a year or more, means a compressor and a humidifier that never really get to rest. A member running a single walk-in cave for hard, natural-rind cheddar and a wet cave for washed-rind wheels is effectively running two climate systems on top of everything else it takes to keep a creamery open. When electricity rates move, that cost doesn't show up as a line on the milk price. It shows up on a utility bill three weeks later, for wheels that were already committed to the cave months before the rate changed.

## Why this is a harder number to plan around

A milk price is published. A feed contract has a term. An electric rate change, especially for a small commercial account, can be harder to see coming and harder to hedge against — most creameries don't have the volume to negotiate a fixed industrial rate, and a lot of aging rooms were built into older buildings where insulation and compressor efficiency were never really optimized for the load they're now carrying. For a wholesale account holding six-figures' worth of wheels in inventory at any given time, an unplanned jump in the cost of holding that inventory is a margin problem that doesn't resolve until the cheese sells.

We don't have good aggregate numbers yet on how much this has moved for the membership as a whole this year, and we're not going to guess at a figure we can't support. What we can say, from committee conversations, is that it's worth treating your aging room's utility cost as its own line in your cost accounting rather than folding it into general overhead — several members who started doing that this spring told us it changed how they thought about pricing wheels that sit in the cave the longest.

## What to do with this if you're feeling it

If your rate has moved and you haven't repriced long-aged product to reflect it, our Membership & Outreach Committee has been fielding exactly this question and can point you to members who've been through a repricing conversation with wholesale buyers recently — that's a conversation worth having before the invoice for a nine-month wheel goes out, not after. If you're evaluating whether an equipment upgrade would pay for itself, that's a longer conversation, and one where "ask a member who's already done it" beats guessing.

None of this is a reason to panic about the cave. It's a reason to know your number. Members who track cost per wheel by aging length going into a rate-volatile year like this one tend to have an easier time explaining a price increase to a buyer than members who discover the number for the first time when a buyer asks.
