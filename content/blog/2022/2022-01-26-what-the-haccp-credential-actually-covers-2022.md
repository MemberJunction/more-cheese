Every time a compliance threshold shifts, as one did again this month, our phones start ringing with a version of the same question: is the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate going to satisfy what a regulator is going to ask me for? It's a fair question and one we'd rather answer plainly than let a member assume either too much or too little.

The short version: the credential prepares you to build and defend a written food-safety plan. It does not replace a facility inspection, it does not certify your specific product, and it is not a legal opinion on whether your operation is compliant. What it does is teach you the structure a plan needs — hazard identification specific to your process, the critical control points in your make and aging schedule, the monitoring and recordkeeping that proves you're actually following the plan you wrote, and what a corrective action looks like when a control point fails a check.

## Who this is actually for

We built this credential for the member who's never had to write a formal plan before, not for someone brushing up on an existing food-safety department's procedures. If your creamery has grown into a compliance threshold this year, or you've simply never had a written plan because your scale didn't require one until now, this is squarely your starting point. Members already running a mature food-safety program tend to use it differently — as a refresher before a recertification cycle, or as onboarding for a new hire who needs to understand the plan they're inheriting.

The coursework walks through a real make process from raw material intake to finished wheel, which means the hazards and control points you build in class map onto decisions you're already making on your floor — a cave humidity reading, a pasteurization temperature log, a label that has to match what's actually in the wheel. It's built around your process, not a generic textbook one.

## What it doesn't do, and what to pair it with

The credential won't tell you whether your specific creamery falls inside or outside this month's exemption threshold — that's a numbers question about your own sales history, not a food-safety question, and we'd rather you check it against the actual rule than guess. It also isn't a substitute for a facility-specific consultation if your process is unusual enough that the standard hazard categories don't map cleanly onto it.

It's also worth saying that compliance dates themselves aren't as fixed as they look on paper. A federal agricultural-water compliance date that was supposed to take effect for larger operations today, January 26, was instead pushed back at the last minute, with the regulator opting to use enforcement discretion while it reworks the underlying requirement. That's not a dairy rule specifically, and we're not suggesting anything in your own compliance calendar is about to move the same way. But it's a useful reminder that a written plan built to the spirit of the requirement — real hazard analysis, real monitoring, real records — tends to hold up regardless of exactly when an enforcement date lands, in a way that a plan built purely to hit a deadline doesn't.

If you're building a plan for the first time, start with the credential and give yourself real weeks, not days, to draft the plan behind it — a rushed plan reads like a rushed plan to anyone who has to review it. If you've already got a plan you inherited or wrote years ago, the recertification track is a faster path and a good excuse to check that the document still matches what you're actually doing in the creamery today. Either way, the Food Safety Committee can point you to the next cohort and answer questions the syllabus doesn't cover.
