Every case has a wheel that moves fast and nobody quite knows why. At Orchardmere Cheese & Provisions, the counter staff got tired of guessing and started writing it down.

It began three winters ago as a habit, not a system: a clerk kept a small notebook behind the register and jotted down what customers actually said when they bought — or didn't buy — a given cheese. "Too sharp for my husband." "Reminds her of a trip to the Alps." "Wanted something for a cheese board, ended up buying the whole wedge instead." None of it was scientific. All of it turned out to be useful.

## From notebook to case plan

By last year the habit had outgrown the notebook. The shop now keeps a running tasting-note log for every cheese that comes through the case, tied to the season it sold in and, loosely, to what else was on the counter that week. It's not inventory software — it's closer to a diary that happens to double as a buying guide. When a new wheel from a small creamery comes in on approval, someone pulls up the notes from the last thing that tasted similar and has a rough sense, before a single sample is cut, of who in the regular customer base is going to want it.

The payoff shows up at reorder time. Instead of restocking on gut feel or on what a rep is pushing that month, the shop can point to specific language — "the nutty one that sells out by Friday," "the one people buy for a gift, not to eat that week" — and make a case for or against a SKU that has nothing to do with the wholesale price and everything to do with whether it will actually move. It has also made training new counter staff faster: instead of memorizing flavor profiles from a sheet, new hires read a season's worth of real customer reactions and start recommending cheeses with more confidence in their first month than staff used to have after three.

There's a food-safety upside too, even if it wasn't the point. A log that tracks how fast a given cheese actually sells is also a log of how long anything sits in the case before it moves — useful information for anyone thinking harder about rotation, and a habit our Food Safety Committee has quietly recommended to other retail members who ask us how to tighten up their case turnover without buying new equipment.

## Worth borrowing, whatever your counter looks like

You don't need three years of notebooks to start. A single index card taped under the register, or a shared note on a phone, will do the same job at a smaller scale: write down what a customer said when they bought something, or when they walked past it. Read it back in a month. Patterns show up faster than you'd expect, and they're a lot more useful than a rep's sell sheet when it's time to decide what goes back on order.
