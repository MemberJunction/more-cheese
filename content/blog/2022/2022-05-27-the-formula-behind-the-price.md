Dairy pricing reform is not a topic that makes for a punchy headline, which is probably why it's stayed a quiet corner of our advocacy work even in a spring when milk prices are the loudest thing in the industry. Members have been signing petitions and writing letters on it for a couple of cycles now. This week we want to explain why the formula question still matters, even — especially — when the number it produces looks good.

The federal system that prices milk sorts it into classes depending on what it's used for, and each class gets priced through its own formula. This spring those formulas are producing historically high numbers across the board, with every class running well above where it sat a year ago. For a producer selling into the right class at the right moment, that's a strong season. For a small creamery whose margin depends on which class its milk gets counted under, the formula itself — not just the headline price — is what actually decides whether the season is good.

## Why the formula, not just the number

A high headline price doesn't automatically reach every producer the same way. Federal legislation introduced earlier this year would direct the agency responsible for milk pricing to hold formal hearings re-examining how the fluid-milk price is calculated, on the theory that the mechanism hasn't kept pace with how the industry actually sells and prices milk today. National farm-policy groups have raised a related, more technical point: the standard used to calculate one part of the pricing formula — the allowance processors get credited for the cost of turning milk into cheese, butter, and other products — is based on survey data that may not reflect what processing actually costs anymore, on either side of the equation.

Members have told us for years that classification rules can lag behind how milk actually moves through a modern specialty supply chain, particularly for smaller operations selling a mix of fluid, cultured, and aged product rather than sitting neatly in one class. Our advocacy position has never been that prices should be higher or lower — it's that the mechanism sorting milk into classes should reflect how our members' operations actually work, so that a strong market for milk in general translates into a strong season for the specific creamery producing it.

This spring's numbers make the case louder than usual. When every price class is up substantially year over year and members are still telling us their own margins haven't moved the way the headlines suggest, that gap is exactly the reform conversation, playing out in real invoices rather than in a hearing room.

## What we're asking members to do

If your operation has felt that gap this season — strong market prices that didn't show up proportionally in what you were actually paid or charged — the Membership & Outreach Committee wants to hear the specifics, and the Standards Committee has been compiling member examples to bring to an upcoming coalition meeting on dairy pricing reform. A concrete number from your own ledger is worth more to this effort than a general complaint, however justified the complaint might be.

We'll keep members posted as the coalition work continues, and expect this to be a multi-year process rather than something a single hearing resolves. In the meantime, if you're new to advocacy participation, a letter-writing action is a lighter first step than testimony, and the committee can point you to the current one.
