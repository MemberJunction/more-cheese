Ask most cheesemakers or counter staff how they know a wheel is ready, or how they'd describe a flavor to a customer, and you'll usually get an answer built entirely on instinct — years of tasting that never got written down as a method. That instinct is real and it's valuable, but it's also hard to teach, hard to hand off to a new hire, and surprisingly easy to lose track of when a shop gets busy and nobody's stopping to actually taste anything on purpose. Our Sensory Foundations course exists for exactly that gap, and this is a good month to think about it, before the holiday case turns tasting into something that only happens when a customer asks.

## What a trained palate actually means

Sensory evaluation, done properly, isn't about having a sensitive tongue. It's a discipline with a structure: a shared vocabulary for describing what you're tasting, a habit of tasting in a consistent order and way, and — critically — practice comparing a sample against a known standard rather than against your memory of what it tasted like last time. Industry guidance on sensory training makes the same point again and again: the fastest way to teach someone to recognize a flaw is to let them taste the flaw next to a clean, correct version of the same cheese, side by side, so the difference registers as a comparison rather than an abstract description.

That's a very different exercise than casually tasting your own product while you work. It requires slowing down, tasting deliberately, and writing down what you noticed in words specific enough that someone else could read your notes and know what you meant. A note that says "a little off" teaches nothing. A note that says "faint bitterness on the finish, more pronounced than the reference wheel" is something you, or a colleague, can actually act on.

## Building the habit before it's needed

The course walks through the basics: the vocabulary for describing appearance, aroma, texture, and flavor in terms that mean the same thing to two different tasters; how to set up a simple reference tasting with a known-good and a known-flawed sample; and how to keep tasting notes that are useful three months later, not just in the moment. None of it requires a lab. A kitchen table, good light, and a handful of samples you already have on hand is enough to start.

The timing matters more than it might seem. Once the holiday case fills up and staff are fielding a dozen questions an hour about what pairs with what, there's rarely room to develop a new skill from scratch. Members who've built a tasting habit earlier in the year tell us it pays off exactly now — a counter staffer who can describe a wheel specifically, rather than generically, handles the busy season with more confidence and fewer "let me just say it's good" moments.

If you've been meaning to formalize what your team already does by instinct, Sensory Foundations is built to take a few weeks, not a season, and it's a reasonable thing to start in November even with the holidays bearing down.
