A member who coordinates logistics for a regional dairy distributor told us this month that the hardest call she's made all year wasn't to a creamery about a late shipment — it was to her longtime case supplier, who couldn't give her a delivery date at all. "Usually you get a number, even a bad one," she said. "This time I just got 'we'll call you.'"

She runs the packaging and cold-chain side for a mid-sized distributor that moves product for a dozen or so member creameries, and her job in a normal year is mostly about routes and refrigeration, not sourcing. This spring it became something closer to procurement. With her usual cardboard supplier unable to commit to a timeline, she spent the better part of a month calling around — smaller regional box makers, a packaging broker one of our other members mentioned in passing, even a printer who occasionally does short runs of case stock as a side line.

The timing made it worse. Weekly market reporting has shown cheese prices holding firm even as producer margins stay thin, which means the creameries on her route are shipping steadily and can't afford a packaging gap to slow them down. "Nobody's cutting orders," she told us. "If anything people want more trucks, not fewer. So a stalled case order isn't a quiet problem, it's a loud one." That pressure is part of what pushed her past a single phone call and into a real search.

## Building a second column, not a replacement

What she settled on wasn't a full switch. She kept the original supplier for the volume she could get from them and added a second, smaller vendor to cover the gap — a setup she now describes as "a second column in the spreadsheet, not a backup plan I hope I never use." The new supplier is more expensive per unit and has a higher minimum order, so she's not moving everything over. But it means a single missed call doesn't stall a week's worth of outbound shipments for the creameries she serves.

She's also started sharing what she learned with the creameries on her route, several of whom were facing the identical problem on their own retail packaging. One farmstead producer she works with used her research to find a comparable regional supplier for its own wrap and label stock, cutting out weeks of trial and error. It's the kind of quiet, unglamorous problem-solving that doesn't show up in a quarterly report but keeps a lot of small operations moving — and it's the kind of thing a distributor is well placed to do, since she's already talking to a dozen suppliers a week for other reasons.

It also changed how she thinks about her own job. She used to see packaging sourcing as something the creameries handled on their own, upstream of her work. Now she treats it as part of the service she offers, the same way she'd flag a weather delay on a refrigerated route. "If I know before they do, why would I sit on it," she said.

## What she'd tell other members

Her advice for anyone facing the same squeeze is not complicated: start the second-supplier conversation before you're desperate, because desperate buyers get worse terms and slower callbacks. She also suggested that creameries talk to their distributors directly about packaging, not just product — many distributors are already doing the legwork on materials sourcing and are happy to share what they've found if a member simply asks.

If you're a member navigating your own packaging bottleneck, our Membership & Outreach Committee is a good first call — they've been keeping an informal list of vendors other members have had success with this quarter.
