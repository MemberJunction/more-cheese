The National Weather Service is calling this week's heat wave across the Midwest and Southwest one of the earliest and most intense on record, with several stations posting their first triple-digit readings in years and dewpoints high enough to push heat index values close to 120. For members running an aging room out of a converted barn, a basement, or a repurposed walk-in, this is the week that separates a cave that's been running fine from one that's about to reveal a weak point.

Every affineur has a stretch each spring when the outside temperature crosses some invisible line and the cave stops behaving the way it did in cooler months. It's rarely dramatic on day one — a degree or two of drift, a humidity reading that used to hold steady now creeping down by mid-afternoon. By the time it's obvious, you're usually a week behind on correcting it, and this year's heat arrived earlier and harder than most.

Ambient heat doesn't just push your cave's temperature up; it changes the whole relationship between temperature and relative humidity that your equipment was calibrated for. A cave running comfortably at 90% humidity in March can slide into the 70s in June with the same equipment on the same schedule, because warmer air holds more moisture and your humidifier has to work harder just to keep pace. Rind cheeses feel this first — a bloomy rind that was developing evenly can start drying at the edges while the center lags, and a washed rind can go from tacky to crusted in a few days if nobody's watching the hygrometer.

## What to check this week

Start with your recovery time, not your set point. If your cave usually returns to target humidity within twenty minutes of a door opening and it's now taking an hour, that's your early warning — not the daily average, which can look fine even as your equipment loses ground. Members who've been through a few summers in the same space tell us the fix is rarely a bigger humidifier; it's usually sealing the small air leaks around doors and vents that didn't matter when the outside air was closer to your target range.

Second, separate your rind categories if your space allows it. A single cave holding both bloomy-rind and washed-rind wheels through a heat spike is fighting two different humidity needs at once, and something will lose. If you can't physically separate them, prioritize the wheels earlier in their aging cycle — a young wheel that dries out in week two rarely recovers the way a wheel in its final weeks can tolerate a rough patch.

Third, check your washing schedule against the calendar, not the clock. A washed-rind wheel that needs washing every three days in a cooler month may need it every two once the cave warms, because surface moisture evaporates faster and the bacterial culture you're relying on needs a wetter surface to keep working.
