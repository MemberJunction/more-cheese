If you closed out your holiday numbers this week and didn't love what you found, you're not alone. A few members have told us their revenue for November and December looked fine on the surface, but once labor, freight, and packaging costs were subtracted out, the margin on their best-selling holiday items was thinner than they expected. That gap between top-line sales and actual margin is exactly what our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course spends its four sessions on, and it's why we keep pointing members toward it in January rather than in the fall when it might actually help plan the season ahead.

Input costs across agriculture and food production have been climbing since last fall, and forecasters going into this year have been warning growers and producers alike to plan for higher costs rather than assume a return to normal. Specialty cheese isn't exempt from that pressure just because the product commands a premium price, and winter supply-chain strain this month has only added to it. A wheel that costs more to make, ship, and staff for than it did a year ago still needs to clear a margin, and "it sold well" isn't the same question as "did it make money."

## What the course actually walks through

Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese isn't a course about raising prices — it's a course about knowing your real cost structure well enough to make a pricing decision on purpose instead of by habit. Sessions cover building a true landed cost per wheel or per pound, including the packaging and labor costs that are easy to undercount, reading your own sales data to tell which items are actually profitable versus just popular, and setting a wholesale price floor you can defend to a buyer who pushes back.

Members who took the course last cohort told us the most useful exercise was recalculating the margin on their single best-selling item using their actual costs from the past few months, not the assumptions they'd been pricing off for a year. More than one found the item they were proudest of was barely breaking even once labor was properly counted, which was an uncomfortable but useful thing to learn in January rather than next fall.

## Worth doing now, not next fall

January is an unglamorous time to think about pricing — the holiday rush is over, and it's tempting to just be relieved it's behind you. But your cost data from the past few months is as fresh as it will ever be, which makes this the best window to actually run the numbers rather than guess at them next October when you're setting prices for the next holiday season under pressure. A margin review now costs you an evening. A pricing mistake discovered next December costs you a season, and a strained one at that if costs keep climbing the way they have been.
