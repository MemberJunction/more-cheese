A member who manages the cheese counter at a small specialty grocer in the Mid-Atlantic told us something this month that a lot of our retail members will recognize: her wholesale invoices have gone up twice since January, and both times she absorbed the increase rather than touch the case price. By the second invoice, her margin on several of her best-selling wedges had thinned to almost nothing.

The third time it happened, in mid-March, she did something different. She raised prices on the affected cheeses by exactly the amount her cost had gone up, and she put a small card next to each one explaining, in a sentence or two, why. Not an apology. Just a plain statement that the increase was coming from the supplier side, tied to feed and freight costs, and that the shop wasn't padding it.

## What changed at the counter

She told us she expected complaints. She got a handful, but far fewer than she'd braced for, and several regulars thanked her for saying something instead of just letting the number creep. "People don't mind paying more nearly as much as they mind feeling like something's being pulled on them," she said. "The card took maybe ten minutes to write for six cheeses. It probably saved me twenty conversations at the register."

If you run a counter or a stand yourself, you've likely lived some version of this already this spring. National reporting on grocery inflation has made the same point at a much bigger scale: shoppers tend to believe prices are rising faster than they actually are, which means silence from the person selling to them fills in the worst assumption by default. Our member's card wasn't a marketing move. It was closer to just narrowing that gap between what a customer assumes and what actually happened to her invoice.

She's since made it a habit. Any time a wholesale increase gets passed through, it comes with a line of context, either on a shelf card or in the shop's weekly email. She keeps the language plain and short on purpose — this isn't the place for a lecture on grain markets, just enough of a reason that a customer doesn't have to wonder.

## A small template worth borrowing

For members running a retail counter or a farmers' market stand, her approach has three parts that don't take much time to copy:

- State the fact plainly: this cheese's wholesale cost rose this month.
- Give one concrete reason, without overexplaining: feed costs, a supplier's fuel surcharge, a tight milk supply.
- Skip the apology. A price increase tied to a real input cost doesn't need one, and over-apologizing can make customers more suspicious, not less.

She's quick to say this isn't a fix for every situation. A shop with thinner customer relationships, or a base that's more price-sensitive to begin with, may see a different reaction, and your mileage on any of this will depend on how much trust you've already built at your own counter. But for a business built on regulars, she thinks the honesty has paid for itself several times over already this spring, and she'd rather field a few pointed questions in March than lose a customer's trust over a number that quietly crept up on them.
