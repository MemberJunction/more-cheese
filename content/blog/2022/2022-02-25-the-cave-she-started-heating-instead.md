A member who runs a six-cow farmstead operation in the upper Midwest called our office this
week with a question we don't get often: was there any guidance on aging-room heating costs.
She's in her fourth year of production, small enough that the cave is a converted root
cellar, and she'd just gotten a propane bill that made her recheck the meter. Her answer to the
bill wasn't to turn the heat down. It was to start tracking it against her price sheet for the
first time.

We asked if we could tell her story without her name attached, and she said yes, as long as we
kept it useful rather than sympathetic. So here's what she told us.

## The cave doesn't care about the news

Her cave holds at 52 degrees most of the year without much help, but a cold snap in late
January pushed the root cellar low enough to slow her affinage schedule, and running a small
heater to bring it back up costs real money when propane and diesel prices are both climbing —
energy markets have been especially jumpy this week on top of an already-tight winter market.
"The wheels don't know the price went up," she told us. "They still need the same 52 degrees.
That's the part that took me a year to really believe — the cave's needs don't move with the
market, so my price has to be the thing that moves."

That's the practical lesson underneath her story: aging-room climate control is a fixed cost
that scales with energy prices whether or not your sales do, and for a small producer, it's
easy to treat it as a background expense instead of a line item that needs its own line on the
price sheet. She started keeping a monthly log this winter — propane cost, hours run, pounds
aged — specifically so that next year's price wouldn't be a guess.

### What changed in her pricing

She raised her wholesale price twice in the last six months, both times in smaller increments
than she felt she needed, both times with a short note to her two wholesale accounts explaining
why. Neither account pushed back. "I think people expect it now," she said. "Everybody's seen
their own heating bill." She's also started separating her aged wheels from her fresh product
on the price sheet in a way she didn't before, because the aged line is the one actually
carrying the energy cost — the fresh cheese moves fast enough that the cave barely factors in.

She's not alone in this. Small farmstead producers running converted spaces — cellars, barns,
unheated outbuildings retrofitted for aging — are more exposed to a cold winter and an energy
price swing than members running purpose-built, better-insulated rooms. If that's your setup
too, her log-and-adjust approach is a low-cost way to stop guessing at what your cave actually
costs you.

Where to take it from here:

If you're tracking your own aging-room costs for the first time this winter, our *Pricing &
Margin for Specialty Cheese* course has a short module on fixed-cost tracking that walks through
exactly this kind of log. And if propane or diesel costs are pushing your operation somewhere
uncomfortable, the Membership & Outreach Committee keeps a list of members willing to talk
through what's worked for them — a conversation this member said she wished she'd had a year
earlier.
