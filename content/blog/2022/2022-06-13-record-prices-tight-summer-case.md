Federal order milk prices set another record this spring, and butter has climbed with them, trading this month at levels not seen since 2015. If your feed and hauling bills have felt heavier since March, the market data backs that up, and it isn't a regional blip — it's showing up in every federal order region members have reported from this season.

None of this is a surprise if you've been watching your own milk check, but it's worth naming the mechanism plainly, because it's not just "prices are up." Export demand has been softening even as domestic prices climb, which is an unusual pairing — normally strong exports are what drag prices higher. This time it looks more like tight supply pushing prices up on its own, with demand along for the ride rather than driving it. That distinction matters for how long the run lasts: a supply-driven spike tends to correct faster than a demand-driven one, once more milk comes online later in the year.

## What it means behind the counter

For members making cheese, a higher and less predictable milk price makes the case for locking in supply contracts rather than buying on the spot market, even at a premium. Several of you have told us that's exactly what you did this spring, and it's paid off as the market has kept climbing rather than easing back. For members buying and reselling, the pressure shows up as a wholesale cost that keeps moving between orders, which is its own kind of headache when a printed price sheet goes stale in three weeks instead of three months.

There's a reason to stay optimistic about volume even with prices this high, though. Recent point-of-sale data on the specialty and snacking side of the cheese case shows those categories continuing to pull shoppers in even as everyday commodity cheese sales hold roughly flat — charcuterie-style formats in particular have kept growing through this stretch. That's worth remembering when a customer balks at a price tag: the category as a whole isn't shrinking under pressure, it's shifting toward exactly the kind of product most of our members already make.

We've also heard from a few retail members that this is the first cycle in a while where customers are asking about price before they ask about flavor. That's a real shift in how a cheese case gets shopped, and it's worth training staff to have an answer ready — something plainer than "supply and demand," even if that's the honest answer underneath it. "Milk itself costs more to buy this year, and that's most of what you're paying for" tends to land better with a customer than a market lecture, and it's also just true.

## What to watch through summer

The Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course has been running full cohorts all spring for a reason — members are recalculating margins more often than usual, and the course walks through how to rebuild a price sheet without guessing at what's actually driving the number. If your last margin review predates this spring's run-up, it's probably already out of date, and that's not a knock on anyone's bookkeeping; the market has simply been moving faster than most review cycles are built for.

We'll keep tracking the federal order numbers as they come in through the summer and flag anything that looks like a real turn rather than noise. If the current pattern holds — tight supply, softening exports, strong specialty demand absorbing the higher price — expect prices to stay elevated into late summer before any meaningful pullback. Plan your supply contracts and your case pricing with that timeline in mind rather than betting on an early correction that the data isn't showing yet.
