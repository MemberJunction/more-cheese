With next year's competition entry window still months off, this is a quiet stretch for members thinking about Competition Judge Accreditation — which makes it the right time to actually do the reading, rather than cramming it in the week before entries open. We hear from members every winter who assumed the credential would formalize a skill they already had. Most come away from it having learned something less obvious: that judging well is less about a sensitive palate and more about a specific kind of discipline most tasters never practice on their own.

## Two very different jobs on the same panel

Judging teams typically split into two roles that pull in different directions. One judge works from a perfect score and deducts points for specific, identifiable flaws and defects. The other starts from nothing and builds a score up based on outstanding, positive characteristics. Both roles require the same underlying skill — precise, structured tasting — but they require holding two different mental models at the same time, and members tell us that's the part accreditation training actually drills, more than flavor vocabulary itself.

It's a useful distinction even outside competition. A counter staffer describing a wheel to a customer is closer to the second role: building a case for what's good about it. A quality manager checking a batch against spec is closer to the first: hunting for what's wrong. Understanding both mindsets, and being able to switch between them deliberately, is arguably the most transferable part of the credential.

## What the accreditation actually covers

The coursework is built around structured comparison tasting — working through reference samples with known characteristics, and practicing consistent scoring against a shared standard rather than a personal preference. That consistency is the entire point of a judging panel: two judges tasting the same wheel independently should land on similar scores, for similar reasons, or the panel isn't doing its job. Members who've gone through it describe the early sessions as humbling, since most people's first instinct is to score based on what they personally like rather than what the standard actually calls for.

Accreditation also covers the less glamorous mechanics of judging: how feedback gets written so a maker can actually use it, what a fair comportment standard looks like across judges with very different backgrounds, and how to keep a panel's scoring consistent across a long day when palate fatigue is a real and documented problem, not just an excuse. A recent review of several years of official competition scoring found exactly the failure mode accreditation is meant to prevent: panelists who hadn't agreed in advance on the specific descriptors for a given cheese variety produced noticeably inconsistent scores, while panels that trained together and revisited their own agreement over multiple seasons got measurably more consistent over time. That's not a discouraging finding — it's a case for taking the training seriously rather than treating it as a formality, and for treating your own scoring consistency as something you build over repeated seasons rather than something you either have or don't.

If you're weighing whether to start the accreditation pathway this winter, it's worth talking to a member who's already judged before you commit — several volunteers on the Awards & Competition Committee have sat on panels for years and are glad to walk through what a real competition day actually feels like from the judge's side of the table. The credential itself is available through our certification pathway, and starting the coursework now means you're not learning the scoring discipline under the pressure of an actual competition deadline.
