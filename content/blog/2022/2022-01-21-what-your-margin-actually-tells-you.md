We wrote earlier this week about the price swing on butter and cheddar, and heard back from a few members with a fair question: how do you actually build a price sheet that doesn't need to be rewritten every time the commodity markets move? That's the exact gap our Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course is built to close, and this felt like the right week to walk through what's actually in it.

The course isn't a lecture on where prices come from. It starts from your own cost sheet — milk, cultures, labor, aging loss, packaging, the rent on your cave if you're paying rent on a cave — and builds a model that tells you what a wheel needs to sell for before you ever look at what a competitor charges or what a wholesale index says. "I'd been pricing off gut feel and what the shop next door charged for eleven years," one recent participant told us. "I didn't realize I'd been underpricing my aged wheels by almost a third until I actually ran the numbers on paper."

## What the model actually accounts for

The part members say surprises them most is aging loss — the weight a wheel sheds in the cave between the day it's made and the day it's sold. A cheese that loses fifteen percent of its weight over six months of aging needs to be priced as if you're only ever going to sell eighty-five percent of what you made, not the full block weight on day one. Skip that step and every long-aged wheel on your shelf is quietly subsidizing the cost of making it.

The course also spends real time on the difference between a price you can hold and a price you're hoping holds. A price sheet built off last month's spot commodity number will need rewriting every time that number moves — which, as this month showed, can be often. A price sheet built off your own trailing cost average moves slowly and predictably, and it's a much easier conversation to have with a distributor who wants to know why your invoice didn't change when the market did.

## Who this is for

This one is aimed squarely at owner-operators and quality managers who set their own prices and haven't had formal training in doing it — which, by our own numbers, is most of you. It runs as a self-paced online course with a live session for questions, so it fits around a production schedule rather than fighting it.

It's also worth saying who this course is not for. If you already run a full cost-accounting model and revisit it quarterly, you'll find the early material familiar. Where members tell us it still pays off even for experienced operators is the section on wholesale-versus-direct pricing, which walks through why the same wheel can carry two defensible prices depending on the channel it sells through, and how to explain that difference to a distributor without sounding like you're charging them more for the same product.

Feed and packaging costs have stayed elevated into this year, and margin compression from rising input costs doesn't show up cleanly on a spreadsheet unless you're tracking cost per pound made, not just cost per gallon of milk purchased. A model that separates milk cost from feed-driven production cost lets you see which part of your margin squeeze is temporary and which part is structural — and that distinction matters when you're deciding whether to raise a price or absorb a bad quarter.
