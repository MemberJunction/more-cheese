If your milk check has looked unusually good this quarter, you're not imagining it. Class III
pricing has been running near record territory heading into this spring, and it isn't a fluke —
it's the tail end of a squeeze that's been building since last summer. Milk production has
stayed below year-ago levels for months now, and the national dairy herd is smaller than it was
a year ago after a dry summer tightened feed supplies. When supply tightens like that while
demand for cheese, butter, and dry milk holds steady, prices move. Right now, they're moving in
members' favor.

We're not writing about this to celebrate a good quarter, though. We're writing about it because
the same input that's been squeezing supply — feed cost — just got a fresh shock, and the two
sides of that equation don't move on the same clock.

## Why your feed bill is part of the same story

Grain markets got a lot less quiet this week. Corn is one of the biggest single inputs on a
dairy ration sheet, and a meaningful share of the world's traded corn and wheat has historically
moved through the Black Sea region. This week's escalation there has already pushed grain
futures higher, and if that continues, expect your feed supplier's next quote to reflect it
before your milk buyer's next contract renewal does.

That's an uncomfortable lag for a lot of members. Feed costs tend to move first and fastest;
milk and cheese prices catch up more slowly, and not always by the same margin, and not always
for the same length of time. Several members told us during last summer's drought-driven feed
spike that the higher milk price took months to show up in their check, while the feed bill hit
the very next invoice. That's the pattern worth watching for again here.

## What we're telling members to check

A few practical, concrete things, not predictions:

- **Feed contracts renewing in March or April.** If you haven't already, get ahead of the
  renewal conversation instead of waiting for the invoice to tell you what happened. Ask your
  supplier directly how much of their own cost base is tied to imported grain versus
  domestically sourced grain — the answer changes how much cushion you should be building into
  your numbers for the next two quarters.
- **Export demand.** Record dairy exports have been part of what's supporting current milk
  prices. Anything that disrupts global grain and feed trade tends to ripple into broader export
  logistics too, and that cuts both ways for a specialty producer with international accounts.
- **Your own margin math.** A strong milk price is only good news if feed, labor, and packaging
  costs aren't quietly eating the difference. This is exactly the kind of quarter where sitting
  down with a real cost sheet — rather than a gut feel for whether things are "up" — tends to
  turn up a surprise, in one direction or the other.

None of this is cause for alarm on its own. Dairy markets have weathered feed-cost spikes before,
and a tighter milk supply cuts in members' favor as often as it cuts against them over a full
year. But it's a good week to run the numbers rather than assume last quarter's contract terms
still hold up. If you want a second set of eyes on your cost sheet, the Membership & Outreach
Committee can point you to a member in your region who has already worked through this exact
math for their own operation this season.
