A member who milks and makes on a small farmstead herd in the upper Midwest told us this week that she'd redone her cost-per-wheel worksheet twice since January. The first pass was in February, after she got her spring fertilizer quote and the number made her sit down. The second was last week, after diesel crept up again and she realized her February math no longer covered the hauling cost to get finished wheels to her regional distributor.

She's been on the federation's rolls a little over four years, long enough to have a make sheet she trusts and short enough to remember what it felt like to price a wheel for the first time with no history to lean on. What's changed this spring, she says, isn't the making — the aged cheddar and the washed-curd wheel she's known for haven't moved — it's the arithmetic underneath them. "I used to update the worksheet once a year, in January, and call it done," she told us. "This year January's number was already wrong by March."

## Raising a price without losing a customer

The hard part wasn't doing the math. It was deciding what to do with the answer. Her wholesale accounts are used to a stable price list, and a mid-season increase risks looking like she's chasing the headlines about record milk prices rather than covering her own real costs. She settled on a short note to her three regular accounts explaining the fertilizer and fuel numbers directly, with the new price attached rather than buried — the same instinct we've heard from other members raising prices this spring, that a plain explanation lands better than a surprise.

So far, none of her three accounts have pushed back. One asked her to hold the new price through the summer once it's set, which she agreed to on the condition that she can revisit it again in the fall if fuel moves further. She's treating that as a fair trade: certainty for her buyer, a review date for herself.

## What the worksheet actually tracks

She walked us through the worksheet itself, and it's simpler than it sounds: cost of milk per hundredweight, a fixed labor number per make day, a per-wheel packaging and cave-time allocation, and then the two lines that moved this spring — a hauling line tied to diesel, and a feed line tied to what she's paying for the hay and grain her small herd goes through over a winter. She doesn't try to predict where either number goes next. Instead she rebuilds the worksheet whenever a bill comes in meaningfully different from the last one, which this spring has meant more often than she'd like.

What surprised her, she said, was how much the hauling line had grown relative to everything else. A few years ago it was a minor entry, easy to round off. This spring it was large enough on its own to justify a price conversation with her distributor, separate from the fertilizer-driven increase she'd already planned for her wholesale accounts. She's treating the two as different problems that happened to land in the same season.

Her advice to newer farmstead members going into a season like this one is blunt: don't wait for your usual annual review to catch a cost spike. "If your feed bill or your fuel bill moves in a month, your worksheet should move with it, not six months later when you've already sold a season's worth of wheels at the old number." She's also candid that this only works if you're tracking the actual bills rather than a rough sense of them — she keeps her fuel and feed receipts in a single folder now, sorted by month, specifically so the worksheet update takes an evening instead of a weekend.

If you're working through your own cost-per-wheel numbers this spring, the Membership & Outreach Committee can connect you with members who've been through the same recalculation this year, including a couple who've offered to share their worksheet templates directly.
