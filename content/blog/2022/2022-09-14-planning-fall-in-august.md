Every August, a member of ours at Fernholt Creamery sits down with a spreadsheet she calls, without much affection, "the fall math." It tracks every wheel in her cave by make date, target age, and expected sale window, and it's the thing that tells her which cheeses get pulled for the holiday season and which ones keep going another four months.

"People assume aging is the romantic part of the job," she told us on a call this week. "It's actually the part with the most spreadsheets."

## Reading the cave before the calendar tells you to

Her creamery makes a washed-curd style that's ready in as little as eight weeks but genuinely improves out to six months, and every fall she has to decide how much of the current batch to hold. Hold too much and she's tying up cash and cave space heading into her busiest selling season. Sell too early and she's leaving flavor, and margin, on the table.

This year the decision is complicated by something outside her control. International trackers reported this week that global cheese prices have climbed for ten straight months against a shrinking milk supply, and her regional distributor has been asking for more long-aged product than usual as a result. It's a good problem in the sense that the wheels are worth more the longer they sit. It's a harder problem if it means less turnover on her cave shelves right when she needs the space for September's make, on top of a year where her milk costs have already run well above where they sat last September.

Retail analysts have been telling holiday-season sellers something similar since midsummer: shoppers who plan to buy specialty items for entertaining tend to commit to those purchases earlier than the general holiday rush, which means the inventory decisions that matter happen well before Thanksgiving, not during it. For a creamery, that translates into the same lesson at the production level: the fall math has to be settled before the calls start coming in, not during them.

Her solution, for what it's worth, was low-tech. She built in a hard cutoff: any wheel that hasn't hit its minimum age target by the third week of September gets moved to a "committed" shelf and is no longer available to reallocate, no matter what a buyer offers for it. "Once I started treating the shelf plan like a contract with myself, I stopped renegotiating it every time someone called with a good offer," she said.

## The part she didn't expect to matter

What surprised her most, she said, wasn't the math, it was how much calmer her fall got once the decisions were made in advance. In past years she'd found herself fielding calls from buyers in October and making shelf decisions on the spot, usually in her favor, and usually while she was also trying to manage a full make schedule. This year, by the time those calls started coming in, the wheels were already sorted and the answer was already written down.

Her advice for anyone building their first fall aging plan is to write down your assumptions about sale timing before you're under pressure to change them. A plan made in August, when nobody's asking you for anything yet, tends to be more honest than one made in October with a distributor already on the phone. It also means that when a genuinely good offer does come in, she can tell the difference between an offer worth breaking her own plan for and one that's just loud.
