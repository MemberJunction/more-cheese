A member who runs a small regional distribution route out of the upper Midwest told us she used to treat every winter storm as a surprise, even though the same three passes on her delivery map close for weather every single year. "I'd spend December reacting," she said. "This year I sat down in October and mapped out which accounts I could actually reach if a route closed, instead of finding out live on the phone with an unhappy retailer." It's a small shift in timing, but it's changed how her whole cold months operate.

Regional distributors carrying specialty cheese have a narrower margin for error than a large national carrier. A single truck often covers accounts a bigger company would split across three routes, and a missed delivery window doesn't just mean a late shipment — for a soft-ripened or fresh cheese, it can mean product that's no longer sellable by the time an alternate route gets it there. Ice, more than snow, is the real threat: a route that's merely slow in snow can shut down entirely once ice coats the road, and unlike a scheduled snowstorm, ice can close a pass with almost no warning.

## What she changed for this winter

Three specific changes, by her account, made the biggest difference:

- **She built a backup route for every account before the first hard freeze**, not during the first storm. Every retailer on her list now has a documented second path, even if it adds an hour to the drive, so a closure doesn't mean starting from scratch mid-delivery.
- **She started calling accounts ahead of a forecasted storm instead of after a missed delivery.** A retailer who knows a shipment might slip a day can adjust their own ordering; a retailer who finds out after the fact just loses trust in the schedule.
- **She stopped treating fresh, short-shelf-life cheese the same as her aged inventory on a weather-risk day.** When a storm is forecast, fresh orders now go out first, on the most reliable route available, even if that means an aged wheel waits an extra day. A day's delay barely touches an aged cheese. It can ruin a fresh one.

None of this eliminates weather risk — a bad enough ice storm still closes roads no matter how well you've planned. What's changed, she says, is that a closure is now a contingency she's already prepared for, not a crisis she's solving live while a retailer is on the phone asking where their order is.

## A pattern worth borrowing

Her route isn't unusual among members who distribute through the colder months. The specific mapping is hers, but the underlying habit — deciding your weather response before the weather arrives, and treating your most fragile inventory differently from your most durable inventory on a risk day — is something any member managing winter logistics can borrow, whether you're running one truck or coordinating shipments through a larger distributor.

If your account has questions about winter shipping terms in a distribution contract, or wants to compare notes with another member who's navigated a rough weather season, the federation's member advisory line is a good starting point, and several members active in the Membership & Outreach Committee have been through a difficult winter route before and are glad to talk through what worked.
