Diesel set a new national record earlier this month — a federal grain-transportation report clocked it at $4.849 a gallon during the week ending March 7, the highest on record. Trucking costs followed: California, the country's top milk-producing state, saw diesel climb past $6 a gallon by mid-March, more than a dollar higher than just a month before. If your wholesale account hasn't asked about a fuel surcharge yet, it likely will soon.

For most members, this shows up somewhere unglamorous: the line item for outbound freight or the invoice from whoever runs your cold chain. It's not the number on the milk check or the price on the block — and this month, that number has looked good, with dry whey pricing pushed to levels not seen since 2007. But freight compounds the same way milk price does, and it's been easy to miss while everyone was watching the more visible price swings from January and February.

## Where it actually bites

Refrigerated freight costs more to begin with, and cheese travels refrigerated. A route that made sense at last year's fuel prices can go from comfortable margin to break-even without anyone changing the wholesale price they're quoting. Members running their own delivery routes are feeling this directly in fuel spend; members who contract it out are starting to see the surcharge show up as a separate line, sometimes for the first time.

The distributors we've talked to this month describe a scramble to consolidate routes — fewer, fuller trucks, tighter delivery windows, less willingness to make a special run for a small order. That's a rational response on their end, but it changes what a small account can expect from a distributor relationship built on flexibility.

## What to check on your own numbers

A few things worth pulling up this week if you haven't already:

- **Your delivered cost, not your farm-gate or bench cost.** If freight has gone up 15–20 percent since the start of the year and your wholesale price hasn't moved, your real margin has moved without your knowledge.
- **Whether your distributor contract has a fuel surcharge clause**, and if it does, whether it's actually being applied. Some older contracts are silent on this and haven't been revisited in years.
- **Route consolidation options if you deliver direct.** Combining two smaller runs into one fuller one is unglamorous work, but it's the lever most directly under your control.

None of this is a call to panic. Energy costs move, and they've moved before. It's a call to look at the number that's easy to ignore because it isn't the one on the price sheet.
