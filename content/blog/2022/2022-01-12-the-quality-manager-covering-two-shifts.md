A member wrote in this week about a colleague we thought was worth spotlighting, even without using her name at her request. She's the quality manager at a mid-sized creamery, and for most of her career her job has looked the same: build and maintain the food-safety program, write the plans, run the swab tests, train new hires on the parts of the job that don't show up on a make sheet. This January, with two line workers out sick and a temp agency slow to fill the gap, she's also been covering floor shifts most quality managers haven't touched in years.

"I don't mind pulling a shift," she told us. "What I mind is being asked to sign off on a day I didn't actually have time to watch closely." That distinction matters more than it might sound. A quality manager's signature on a daily log means she verified the checks got done, not just that cheese got made. When she's also the one running a vat or covering a packaging line, the two roles compete for the same hours, and something has to give.

## What she's not willing to cut

She was direct about where her line is. Environmental swabbing and cooler temperature logs happen on schedule regardless of how thin the floor is that day — those don't get pushed to "when things calm down." What she has adjusted is some of the documentation review she'd normally do in real time; instead she's batching it at the end of the week, which she's the first to say isn't ideal, but is a defensible trade-off compared to skipping a swab.

She also made a point we've heard from other quality-focused members this winter: a rushed food-safety plan reviewed by a tired person is not much better than no review at all. "If I'm going to sign something, I want to actually be awake enough to catch a problem in it," she said. Her creamery has started staggering her documentation review to the start of her shift rather than the end, when she's freshest, rather than fitting it in wherever there's a gap in a long day already spent on the floor.

## What this looks like for other members

If your quality manager or food-safety lead is also covering production right now, her approach is worth borrowing even if your operation is smaller than hers: decide in advance which checks are truly non-negotiable, and be honest with yourself about which parts of the job can wait a day versus which can't. Trying to do all of it at full speed with half the usual staff is how something quietly slips, and it rarely announces itself before it becomes a real problem.

She said the one thing that's kept her from burning out is that her creamery's ownership has been upfront about the staffing gap rather than pretending it isn't affecting quality work — that alone made it easier for her to flag when she was stretched too thin. If you manage a quality lead who's covering extra shifts this winter, that kind of honesty costs nothing and buys you a lot of trust when you need it most.
