A member wrote in last week asking why we don't market the Certified Cheese Professional (CCP)
credential as a "tasting exam." It's a fair question — most people outside the industry assume
that's exactly what it is. It isn't, and misunderstanding that is the single biggest reason
candidates walk in underprepared.

The CCP credential is built to test a working knowledge of the cheese business end to end, not
palate skill. Sensory judgment is one part of it, but it sits alongside sections on production
methods, aging and affinage science, food safety and handling, and the retail and service side
of the business — pairing, storage, customer education, and inventory practices that keep a
case performing. Candidates come from every corner of the industry: retailers, cheesemongers,
cheesemakers, distributors, importers and exporters, restaurant buyers, and educators. The exam
is written to be fair to all of them, which means no single background gives you a shortcut
through the whole thing.

There's also an eligibility bar before you can sit for it at all: a set minimum of documented
paid or unpaid hours working in the cheese trade, or a mix of fewer hours plus formal coursework
and continuing education. That's worth planning around early. Members who've come up through
production sometimes have the work-hour requirement covered years before they're ready for the
exam itself; members who've come at cheese from a business or retail angle sometimes need the
coursework path instead, and that path takes longer to assemble than people expect.

## Where members underprepare

We've talked to a number of members who've sat the exam over the past few years, and the same
pattern comes up: people over-prepare on the section closest to their own job and under-prepare
everywhere else. A cheesemonger who's spent years building a palate can walk in confident about
sensory evaluation and get caught out on production chemistry questions they haven't touched
since a course years ago. A production-side member can ace the technical sections and stumble on
retail and service questions they've never had to think about day to day.

The fix members report working best is treating the exam like a survey course, not a victory lap
on your specialty. Set aside time for the section that's furthest from your actual job, not just
the one you already like.

## How to actually study for it

A few things members have told us helped:

- **Work through the full body of knowledge outline before you touch flashcards.** It's easy to
  study what's interesting instead of what's tested.
- **Study production and affinage science even if you've never made cheese.** Retail and
  cheesemonger candidates consistently say this is the section that surprised them most.
- **Practice the service and retail questions even if you run a production floor.** These
  questions test things like storage temperatures, shelf life, and pairing logic that a
  production role doesn't always cover day to day.
- **Give yourself a full practice run under the real time limit.** Candidates report on the
  Sensory Evaluation Certificate course from us as a useful, lower-stakes place to practice
  timed tasting sections before sitting the bigger exam.

The credential isn't required for most jobs in this industry, but members who've earned it tell
us it changed how buyers, employers, and even suppliers treated their judgment on a phone call.
If you're weighing whether to sit for it this year, the Education Committee keeps a short guide
to study timelines and a list of members willing to talk through their own prep experience —
worth a message before you commit to a test date. And if you fall short on your first attempt,
that's more common than members assume — candidates who don't pass are generally welcome to
retake the exam at the next scheduled sitting, so a missed attempt is a scheduling setback, not
a closed door.
