Late June through August is when most of our members' farmers market stands do the bulk of their annual volume, and it's also when the operational cracks show up — the ice you didn't budget for, the card reader that can't keep up with a line, the sample tray that ran out of picks at eleven on a Saturday morning. Our Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets course was built around exactly this stretch of the calendar, and if you haven't looked at it since you set up your stand for the first time, it's worth a second pass now that you have a season or two of your own data to compare it against.

The course covers pricing your stand separately from your wholesale price sheet, which is a mistake we still see often enough to mention every summer. A market customer is paying for convenience, a conversation, and a taste before they buy — that's worth something, and pricing a market wheel identically to a wholesale case wheel usually means underpricing the market one. This year that gap matters more than usual: with milk prices running well above where they sat a year ago, several members tell us their wholesale price sheet has already moved twice this season, and a market price that hasn't kept pace is quietly eating margin every Saturday it goes uncorrected.

## The part that's easy to skip

Cold-chain management at an outdoor stand is the section members tell us they underestimated. A cooler that holds temperature fine in a 70-degree morning can struggle by early afternoon in July heat, and a soft-ripened or fresh cheese that's been sitting at the edge of safe temperature for a few hours is both a quality problem and a food-safety one. The course walks through practical fixes — ice rotation schedules, positioning your table out of direct sun, knowing which products in your case are least tolerant of a warm afternoon and pulling those first if you're running short on ice.

There's also a module on sampling that's worth a look even for members who've been doing markets for years. A few municipalities and market associations have tightened their own sampling rules over the past couple of seasons — individually wrapped or single-use utensils, handwashing station requirements, sneeze guards on sample trays — and a stand that hasn't checked its local market's current rules recently can get an unpleasant surprise from a market manager mid-Saturday. Rules vary market to market, so this is genuinely a "check with your specific market" item, not something we can standardize for you.

A stand that ran fine with one person in May often can't keep up once July traffic hits, especially if you're also running sample tastings and answering the same pairing questions on repeat. The course includes a short section on staffing a second person for peak Saturdays purely from a throughput standpoint — how to split cutting-and-wrapping from register and tasting duties so the line moves without anyone feeling rushed into a sloppy cut or an incomplete answer to a food-safety question a customer asks.

## Before your next Saturday

If you're new to markets this year, the course is a reasonable investment before your third or fourth week out, once you've felt the actual rhythm of a market day and have real questions to bring to it. If you've been doing this for years, skim the cold-chain and sampling sections specifically — those are the two areas most likely to have shifted since you last built your setup. And if a market rule has changed on you mid-season in a way that's affecting how you can sell, our Membership & Outreach Committee wants to hear about it; it helps us track which markets are tightening rules and where members might need a heads-up before their own market does the same.
