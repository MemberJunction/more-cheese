Some members take a credential a decade. This one took two in eighteen months, and she'd probably tell you she's not in a hurry — she just doesn't like standing behind a case not knowing the answer to a question a customer just asked her.

She joined the federation a little over a year and a half ago, not long after moving from a general grocery deli counter into a dedicated cheese program at a specialty shop. Certified Cheesemonger came first, inside her first year. Sensory Evaluation followed a few months after that. Neither was required by her employer. Both, she says, came out of the same recurring moment: a regular asking why a washed-rind wheel smelled different this month than last, and her not having a confident answer.

## The case is busier than it used to be

It isn't a coincidence that this is happening now. The artisan and specialty side of the cheese case has been the bright spot of the last two years industry-wide, even as the broader dairy aisle has had a rougher stretch, and shops that leaned into that category are seeing customers who ask more, taste more, and expect the person behind the counter to keep up. That's a good problem to have, but it's still a problem — a monger who can only say "it's a good one" is leaving the harder half of the sale on the table.

"I got tired of pointing at a wheel and hoping the label did the talking," she told us. "The sensory course gave me the words for what I was already tasting. Now I can tell someone why a cave-aged wheel from a wetter spring reads a little different than last year's, instead of just agreeing that it does."

Her manager has noticed the difference too, mostly in how long customers linger at the case. A tasting note delivered with real confidence tends to turn a browsing customer into a buying one, and it turns a one-wheel sale into a request to set aside next month's shipment. None of that shows up on a certificate, but it shows up on a register tape.

## What the pace actually costs

We asked if the back-to-back credentials felt sustainable, since two certificates in eighteen months is quick by our members' usual clip. Her answer was practical rather than ambitious: she studies on slow shifts and during her commute, not on top of a full course load, and she's deliberately not stacking a third credential this year. "I'd rather actually use what I have before I add to the pile," she said. "A binder of certificates doesn't help the customer in front of me. Knowing the answer does."

That instinct — to let a credential settle into daily practice before chasing the next one — is one the Education Committee actively encourages, even though it isn't the fastest path to a longer resume line. A monger who can taste and explain a rind confidently is more useful to a shop, and to a customer, than one who's memorized three certificates' worth of material without yet applying any of it under pressure.

That's a useful note for any member eyeing a similar run through our credential list — the Certified Cheesemonger and Sensory Evaluation paths are designed to build on each other, but nothing about the federation's structure requires speed. If you're weighing your next credential, or wondering which one actually fits the counter you work behind, the Education Committee is a good first call before you commit to a course.
