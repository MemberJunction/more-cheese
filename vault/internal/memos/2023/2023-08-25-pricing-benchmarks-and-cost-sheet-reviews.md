Wholesale cheddar block prices climbed sharply through July and into early August, adding well over thirty cents a pound in a matter of weeks. That surge has landed on our membership inbox with buyers asking whether spring price lists are still valid. This memo reviews how that commodity swing intersects with our members' pricing practices, what the federation has published on the topic, and where our outreach stands as we head toward the fall buying cycle.

## The commodity swing and member impact

A run-up of thirty-plus cents on bulk cheddar block prices is not a rounding error; it is a renegotiation for any member who ties their list price to that benchmark. Market commentary points to softened milk production during hot, dry stretches that pushed herds to produce less and cost more to keep comfortable, tightening raw milk supplies for vat-scale cheesemaking. At the same time, market watchers are already asking whether the run-up went further and faster than underlying supply supports. None of this is unique to any one creamery, but it lands on every small and mid-sized creamery's spreadsheet at roughly the same time.

For members whose contracts reference a legacy cheddar benchmark, the risk is twofold. If price lists have not moved since spring, they are quietly absorbing the gap. If prices move again when the market eventually dips, they risk friction with retail and distributor buyers who expected stability. Furthermore, bulk commodity cheddar is not twelve-month clothbound specialty cheese. Treating a commodity benchmark as a direct input cost for cave-aged wheels is a dangerous arithmetic shortcut that tends to disadvantage whichever side did not check the math.

## What the public posts and internal committee channels show

Our public blog on 21 August tackled this exact dynamic, urging members to check their internal cost sheets rather than reacting to the commodity benchmark directly, and noting that the Membership & Outreach Committee has fielded similar questions before. In parallel, our course enrollment numbers for the week show thirty new enrollments taken, with the next cohort of Cheese Chemistry scheduled to start on 3 September. That suggests members are looking for structured ways to handle margin and formulation questions rather than just guessing at list prices.

However, our staff capacity is stretched. The same team handling member inquiries on pricing is also supporting event registrations for the Granite Sensory workshop coming up in Chicago on 11 October, which picked up five new registrations this week. We have two membership purchases for the week totaling 1085 gross (one Corporate and one Individual), bringing our current members at week end to 813, with total confirmed orders for the week at eight for 1704 gross and cash captured at 2029. Those are steady figures, but they leave little room for ad-hoc advisory work if pricing disputes accelerate.

## What we need

- Communications: maintain alignment between our inbound member guidance and the 21 August blog post so reps do not offer conflicting advice on benchmark pricing.
- Advocacy & Standards: prepare a brief advisory note for regional committees on how to distinguish raw-milk cave operating costs from bulk cheddar block movements.
- Office of the Chief Executive: acknowledge that staff capacity for individual member pricing consultations is capped until the September course cohorts launch.
- Membership Operations: monitor upcoming membership renewal notices to catch any accounts attempting unilateral price freezes based on outdated contract templates.
