Every year around this time, forced-air heaters in member production facilities start pulling moisture out of aging rooms, dropping relative humidity from a healthy 85 to 90 percent down into the 60s. This week's public blog post on cave management touches on the physics, but our team is already fielding direct inquiries from members whose holiday inventory is sitting in drying air. ## The winter drying cycle

When a building turns on its furnace, cold air meets heat and its relative humidity drops. If a cave shares any airflow, ducting, or a poorly sealed door with the rest of the facility, that dry air migrates straight to the wheels. This is happening earlier in December than usual because of recent weather shifts reported by members in colder regions.

Members are calling because a rind drying out on wheels destined for holiday orders is an immediate crisis. The technical fix is straightforward — a standalone humidifier on a hygrometer-controlled cycle and weatherstripping around the door — but many operations do not have the monitoring routine in place to catch the drop before the rind starts cracking.
