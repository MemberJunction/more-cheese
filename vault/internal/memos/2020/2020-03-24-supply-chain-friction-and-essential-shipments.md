**Subject:** Supply chain friction and essential shipments

State-level stay-home orders issued over the past two weeks have universally classified food and agriculture as essential businesses. For our producer members, this has prevented outright shutdowns, but it has not preserved routine operations. Reports reaching Advocacy & Standards indicate that essential status is currently colliding with inconsistent state definitions, carrier hesitation, and deferred inspection schedules. This memo outlines the operational friction members are experiencing and recommends how we structure our advocacy outreach without overstepping our remit into legal counsel.

## The reality behind essential status

While essential-business classifications keep processing lines moving, the practical movement of cheese across state lines has become unpredictable. Carriers are encountering variable interpretations at state borders, with drivers reporting delayed transit times and conflicting requirements from state to state. A delivery route that previously crossed two or three state boundaries smoothly now requires carriers to verify each state's emerging guidance before dispatch.

Concurrently, state agriculture inspectors who normally manage routine aging-room visits and raw-milk safety sign-offs are rescheduling or deferring appointments. For members whose inventory requires documented sign-offs before legally clearing for shipment, these delays directly threaten distribution windows. The federation's role here is not to provide legal determinations on state travel rules—that responsibility remains with individual members and their counsel—but to aggregate these operational blockages into a clear pattern that regulatory contacts can understand.

## What members are telling us

We have received numerous informal inquiries and reports regarding delayed shipments and inspection backlogs. However, grassroots advocacy requires concrete, documented instances rather than general frustration. We are actively instructing members to submit specific details to the Food Safety Committee: date, state, the nature of the transit or inspection delay, and whether the issue ultimately resolved. Twenty documented delays across multiple jurisdictions carry weight with regulatory bodies; a single isolated complaint does not.

Furthermore, we need to balance our reports. While the friction is real, several members operate under clear state-level guidance that facilitates smooth interstate movement. Having positive working examples allows us to demonstrate association competence and point regulators toward functional working models.

## Capacity and federation focus

Advocacy requests are arriving faster than our normal committee intake workflow can process them during this operating shock. We must maintain focus on core advocacy priorities—specifically interstate shipment rules, raw-milk aging oversight, and labeling standards—without attempting to resolve individual carrier disputes. Communications must ensure that our public messaging reflects these practical realities without causing unnecessary alarm.
