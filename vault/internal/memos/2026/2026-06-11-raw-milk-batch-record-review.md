**Subject:** Raw-milk batch record review and traceability preparation

The federal review of raw-milk aging criteria remains open, and while no finalized regulatory text is expected immediately, recent peer-reviewed findings on pathogen survival in raw-milk cheeses have shifted the compliance baseline. Inspectors and buyers are no longer relying on the sixty-day aging rule alone as a sufficient safety guarantee. Creameries that can demonstrate batch-by-batch traceability—tying specific milk pickup logs and set-point pH readings directly to individual aging shelves—are positioned to weather any eventual standard revision. Conversely, producers operating with single-room clipboard logs face rising exposure.

This memo outlines what our advocacy and education teams are seeing across the membership segment and recommends adjustments to our Food Safety & HACCP Certificate curriculum ahead of the Q3 committee meetings.

## What producers are reporting on the ground

Recent member spotlights and committee discussions highlight a widening gap between small-scale operations. Some farmstead makers have begun retrofitting aging spaces into zoned temperature areas with dedicated batch tracking. As one producer noted in our recent member outreach, the hurdle is rarely the physical square footage of the cave; it is the rigor of the paperwork. Maintaining a verifiable record of set-point pH and milk origin for every vat requires a deliberate pivot from general compliance to active process documentation.

Our advocacy actions this week reflect this shift, including ongoing testimony and labeling standard reviews. Members who have invested in granular recordkeeping are finding that their internal quality control catches process drift long before an inspector requests documentation. However, a significant portion of our smaller regional membership still relies on informal logbooks that make batch retrieval a multi-day ordeal.

## Implications for education and credentialing

Our current Food Safety & HACCP Certificate program covers baseline sanitation and hazard analysis, but it does not place sufficient emphasis on shelf-level traceability design. If regulatory agencies tighten the evidentiary burden on raw-milk aging, our curriculum must reflect that shift. We should update the module materials to include practical, low-cost documentation templates—such as whiteboard-structured spreadsheets and physical shelf-tag systems—that smaller creameries can implement without investing in enterprise warehouse software.

Furthermore, education and credentialing should coordinate with the Food Safety Committee to ensure that member inquiries regarding raw-milk exemptions are met with consistent guidance on record retention. Telling a member to follow the sixty-day rule is no longer adequate advice if their buyer demands batch-level pH verification.
