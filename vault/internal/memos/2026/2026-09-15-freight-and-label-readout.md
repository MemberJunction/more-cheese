**Subject:** Freight costs, allergen declarations, and what members are asking us

This week's federal dairy report highlights a squeeze that is reaching members' desks faster than our make sheets can absorb it. Domestic and foodservice demand is running below expectations, holiday butter demand is softer than buyers planned, and reefer freight and diesel costs are staying high. For a small farmstead creamery shipping cases two states away, that combination turns the freight bill into one of the largest lines on the invoice. We need to make sure our guidance on freight calculations and allergen labeling matches the reality our members are describing.

## The freight squeeze on small shipments

Commodity plants ship truckloads on contract and spread hauling costs over large volumes. A farmstead producer shipping a few cases to a distant distributor faces fuel surcharges and liftgate fees that scale badly. When wholesale cheese prices are flat or drifting downward as foodservice buying cools, a producer who prices delivered cases using spring assumptions is absorbing a shrinking margin.

Our public post this week points out three practical steps: checking the last ten outbound invoices to find the real freight cost per pound, consolidating drops into fuller loads, and talking to distributors in September rather than begging for favors in November. Membership Operations should be ready for inquiries from members whose numbers shock them when they do that division for the first time. The Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course covers lane-by-lane costing, and we should point callers there rather than offering informal pricing advice.

## Allergen declarations and quiet ingredients

Recalls in several states over imported cheese missing an egg-derived ingredient have prompted a wave of member questions about their own label compliance. The major food allergens—milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame—require clear declaration, but the trouble usually hides one step away from the basic milk, culture, rennet, and salt. Preservatives in brine washes, lysozymes used to control gas, and flavorings in rind washes can all introduce undeclared allergens.

We need to remind members that label drift happens when suppliers change carrier ingredients, when store scale databases fall out of sync with imported packs, or when seasonal washed-rind recipes change. Education & Credentialing should highlight that Allergen Management and the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate pathway provide structured reviews for supplier specs and cross-contact plans. Our Food Safety Committee is also gathering questions on rind wash ingredients to compile a reference list.

## Advocacy and campaign metrics

Advocacy actions this week include 10 letter campaign submissions and 5 petition signatures on labeling standards. Members are concerned that state dairy label guides and federal requirements do not always align cleanly with supplier spec sheets, especially when dealing with specialized cultures or rinds. We should keep our guidance strictly focused on what the regulations require and what courses cover, avoiding any suggestion of legal interpretation.

## What we need

- Membership Operations: route any member asking about delivered freight costs to the Pricing & Margin course or connect them with regional peers sharing trucks.
- Education & Credentialing: ensure course enrollment links for the upcoming Food Safety and Allergen Management cohorts are active and referenced in member replies.
- Communications: keep our messaging grounded in practical cost-checking and supplier verification rather than market speculation.
- Everyone: remember that our role is pointing members to verified association resources, not giving direct legal or pricing advice.
