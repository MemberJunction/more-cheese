New tariffs on dairy products moving across our largest northern border take effect this week, and the phones in Membership Operations started ringing before anyone had finished reading the customs broker summaries. Members are asking two distinct questions: whether their specific harmonization code is exposed, and whether their commercial paperwork will hold up if a shipment gets flagged at the border. Because our published blog post on the subject went live this morning, we need to make sure our internal guidance matches what is going out to members without pretending we have a policy position on tariff rates we do not negotiate.

## What members are asking this week

The inquiries fall into two buckets. First, members who export aged wheels or import specialized cultures and packaging are finding that their harmonized system codes do not match the categories listed in the new tariff schedule summaries. Second, distributors are calling to ask whether anyone is absorbing the new duties or passing them through. We have no authority over wholesale pricing or distributor contracts, and we must not offer guidance on who should absorb the cost. What we can do is point members directly to the structural documents they need to check.

This is where our standing educational and compliance assets become practical tools rather than background catalog items. Export Documentation Essentials walks through commercial invoice formatting, harmonized system codes, and certificates of origin. Members caught off guard by a customs hold this week are the exact audience for that material. We should ensure staff answering the help line are directing callers to check their paperwork line by line rather than trying to interpret customs rules over the phone.

## Advocacy alignment on small-producer rules

While trade inquiries dominate the phones, our Advocacy & Standards team is simultaneously preparing the next comment package for federal traceability and small-producer exemptions. The exemption boundary on that rule is moving again this year, and our open call for member numbers is gathering real operational data. Specifically, we need small farmstead and artisan members to submit actual hours spent on recordkeeping, potential costs of crossing volume thresholds, and recall history.

Members confused about trade compliance are often the same ones who fall just below the traceability threshold, and they frequently conflate the two issues when calling in. We must keep the messaging distinct: trade tariffs are an external border cost governed by customs brokers, whereas traceability exemptions are an internal regulatory boundary where member figures directly shape our comment filings.
