**Subject:** Export paperwork readiness and the wholesale-retail split

Early September public commentary and our own member calls this week highlight a widening operating split across the membership base. Retail cheese case sales continue their steady upward trend, particularly in pre-cut and crumbled formats suited for home cooking. Conversely, members dependent on hotel, restaurant, and specialty wholesale accounts report that reopened dining channels are ordering at lower volumes and shifting to smaller, more frequent deliveries rather than traditional weekly drops. While small creameries struggle to adapt their production schedules to these micro-deliveries, an increasing number are also testing cross-border export orders to absorb excess capacity. Unfortunately, our data shows that the primary obstacle for these first-time exporters is not tariff rates or quota limits, but basic administrative errors on certificates of origin and sanitary forms.

## The administrative bottleneck in cross-border shipments

Members attempting to diversify their revenue streams by shipping internationally have encountered severe shipment delays due to clerical mistakes. Recent advisory notices and member feedback point to recurring failures: commercial invoices listing producer marketing names instead of receiving-country tariff nomenclature, health certificates reflecting lapsed facility inspection dates, and multi-style cheese shipments unaccompanied by itemized sanitary documentation. Each error transforms what should be a routine fulfillment into a multi-day customs detention. Because current market conditions compel creameries to dispatch smaller, more frequent export consignments rather than single annual bulk shipments, these paperwork errors multiply rapidly and consume disproportionate administrative hours.

We must ensure our educational offerings directly address this operational friction. The Export Documentation Essentials course—specifically the active Brook cohort—already covers these exact documentation standards, but we need to verify that enrolled members are translating theoretical compliance into pre-printed templates. Furthermore, our advocacy work on raw-milk aging rules and labeling standards must remain aligned with these practical realities so that regulatory compliance does not become an insurmountable hurdle for small-scale producers.

## Operational alignment for Q4

As we head into the final quarter, input costs are tightening. Milk supply growth has lagged behind last year's late-summer pace, firming up farmgate prices and squeezing margins for creameries operating on tight wholesale contracts. Producers who fail to account for rising raw material costs while managing smaller delivery drops risk operating at a loss. Communications must ensure that public-facing guidance and blog articles continue to direct members toward practical operational adjustments—such as direct-to-consumer alternatives and standardized shipping templates—rather than generalities about market recovery.
