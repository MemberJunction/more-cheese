**Subject:** Milk cost pressures, export volume, and the specialty cheese make sheet

Federal trade data released this month indicate that U.S. dairy exports set an all-time record, with skim milk powder and dry whey posting their strongest volumes ever and cheese registering its second-best export month on history. While these macro figures are reported by agricultural desks, their direct consequence is landing in the make rooms of small-batch creameries represented by the federation. Strong export demand pulls heavily on the national milk supply, exerting a quiet but persistent upward pressure on input costs for domestic cheese production, even for producers who never export a single wheel.

We have received a cluster of calls from members this week inquiring whether this export surge is a temporary anomaly or a structural shift in their monthly milk statements. The data suggests it is not a one-month spike; export growth has been building steadily over recent quarters, and Class III futures have firmed correspondingly. Processors are actively expanding powder and whey capacity to service international orders, meaning the competition for raw milk solids will likely persist. This memorandum reviews what these dynamics change on the make sheet and evaluates how our educational offerings and membership outreach should respond.

## Regional divergence in landed milk costs

National trade averages obscure the uneven reality in the field. Member inquiries this week reveal starkly divergent local conditions. A Midwest creamery reported that component pricing barely shifted on their most recent statement, whereas a Northeast affineur purchasing milk on a spot basis absorbed an immediate cost increase that forced a mid-negotiation wholesale price adjustment.

This divergence complicates any association-wide messaging. A price increase driven by spot-market milk volatility cannot be managed with a blanket recommendation. Producers need to separate temporary input noise from a structural baseline shift before altering retail or wholesale price lists. If landed milk costs remain elevated across two consecutive statements, that is a reliable signal to reprice. If it is restricted to a single invoice, it is more likely regional noise working through the pooling formula.

## Supporting members under margin pressure

Our public communication touches on these pressures, but the internal operational burden falls largely on Membership Operations and Education & Credentialing. Members facing squeezed margins from rising milk costs on one side and softer retail or tasting-room traffic on the other require practical guidance rather than general market summaries.

Several long-time producers highlighted a reliable internal habit: maintaining a running log of landed milk cost alongside finished-cheese pricing, updated monthly rather than reconstructed from memory during buyer pushback. Integrating this practice into our advisory conversations transforms a defensive pricing negotiation into a documented, objective discussion.

Furthermore, the *Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese* curriculum addresses precisely this decision-making process—evaluating when to hold, when to reprice, and how to communicate cost-driven adjustments to wholesale buyers without jeopardizing account relationships.

## What we need

- Membership Operations: compile regional member feedback regarding spot milk pricing disparities to identify which co-op pooling formulas are currently absorbing export pressure.
- Education & Credentialing: ensure upcoming cohort promotions highlight margin management tools for small-batch producers.
- Office of the Chief Executive: review draft advocacy talking points on dairy pricing reform ahead of next month's committee meetings.
