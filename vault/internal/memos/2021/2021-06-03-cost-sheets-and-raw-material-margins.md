**Subject:** Cost sheets, Class III softness, and specialty cheese margins

Following this week's public blog post on the federal supply-and-demand forecast, we need to examine what Class III moving off its spring high actually means for our small and mid-sized creamery members. The national production estimate rose again in May, and the benchmark most cheesemakers watch when they price a make sheet has softened further as extra milk reached plants. This memo reviews the margin opportunity against our current membership numbers and outlines what we should tell members who ask how to re-calculate their cost per pound.

## The reality behind the national indicator

A national production number is an average of very different herds and very different milk contracts. Members on fixed-price contracts with their processor will not see this softness reach their bottom line at all this quarter. Conversely, members buying on a spot or near-spot basis are the ones most likely to feel it directly, for better or worse depending on which side of the transaction they sit on. Regional basis has been unusually noisy this year, and what the national indicator does and what a local co-op bills can diverge by more than usual in a supply glut. We need to be careful that our communications do not encourage members to assume a margin improvement without checking actual invoices.

## Wholesale read versus input costs

The read we are getting from wholesale accounts is more encouraging than the price chart alone suggests. Several members tell us that order volumes finally look like a normal spring rather than a cautious one. Cheese counters are restocking, and a couple of members who cut SKU counts last year are adding lines back for summer. One distributor member described it simply: the case is full again, and people are buying what is in it. However, feed, packaging, and freight have all moved since most farmstead and artisan operations last built their cost model, making this month a prime moment to re-verify make-sheet margins.
