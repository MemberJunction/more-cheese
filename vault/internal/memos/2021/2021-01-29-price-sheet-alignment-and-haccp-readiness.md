**Subject:** Price sheet alignment and HACCP readiness after the January surplus

The public commentary published this week on cold-storage cheese stocks and softening Class III milk prices has generated immediate inbound inquiries from small and mid-sized creameries. Producers who watched the Class III number drop by more than seven dollars a hundredweight between November and December are asking whether they should slash their direct-to-consumer and wholesale price sheets. Concurrently, our office continues to field calls from members whose food safety lead departed over the holidays, leaving behind an undocumented HACCP plan. This memo outlines our operational response to both pressures and coordinates our outreach for February.

## The pricing disconnect and cost-based defense

When commodity cheese inventories sit higher than twelve months prior and bulk prices move down, the temptation for specialty producers is to match the downward drift. As noted in association communications, commodity block cheese and the aged, named wheels produced by our membership do not occupy the same economic tier. Members need to decouple their price sheets from the futures board and anchor them firmly to their actual milk, aging, and packaging costs. Spot milk arrangements are lagging the headline index, and packaging costs have not softened. We need Membership Operations and the Membership & Outreach Committee volunteers to point inquirers toward cost-of-production pricing rather than commodity reactivity.

Furthermore, producers who ship fluid milk should be directed to federal margin coverage risk-management tools before the sign-up period closes. Several members who utilized these programs last spring reported that payouts mitigated exactly this sort of margin squeeze. We should ensure our member portal links directly to USDA risk-management guidance.

## HACCP continuity and knowledge retention

The second recurring crisis involves institutional memory. When a food safety lead leaves a small creamery, the binder left behind is frequently two revisions out of date, and the operational logic exists solely in one person's head. The Food Safety & HACCP Certificate remains our most effective tool to rebuild that framework. The curriculum covers hazard analysis, critical control points, monitoring, corrective actions, verification, and recordkeeping—the seven principles required whether the plan is five years old or five weeks old.

For farmstead and small-scale processors, the layering of preventive controls—process, sanitation, allergen, and supply-chain controls—onto classic HACCP can be overwhelming. The certificate provides worked examples and templates that save creameries roughly a week of reconstruction labor. We must encourage creameries to enroll a second staff member immediately, treating redundancy in food safety knowledge as essential as redundancy in make-room equipment.

## Capacity and coordination

Both issues require cross-functional alignment. Membership Operations is handling soft-pricing queries while Education & Credentialing manages certificate inquiries. We closed the week of 25 January with 433 current members, three individual membership purchases, zero new certification enrollments, and eight course completions. With no paid workshop registrations or exam enrollments recorded this week, our administrative capacity is currently dedicated to advisory support rather than processing heavy event volumes.

## What we need

- Membership Operations: ensure volunteer committee members are briefed on the cost-based pricing guidance outlined in the market blog.
- Education & Credentialing: highlight the Food Safety & HACCP Certificate templates in upcoming member broadcast digests to address turnover vulnerabilities.
- Office of the Chief Executive: approve a joint advisory bulletin for mid-February combining margin coverage enrollment reminders with food safety documentation checklists.
