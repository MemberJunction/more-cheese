**Subject:** Interstate shipment documentation and 60-day curing logs

A member's shipment was held at a distribution warehouse two states over last month because her paper trail did not clearly establish the federal 60-day curing requirement for raw-milk cheese. She knew the rule, and her label stated an age range, but she lacked a contemporaneous document tying that specific lot to a make date clearing the threshold. The shipment sat for two days. This is the third time this winter we have heard of a member hitting an informal inspection hurdle at a receiving dock over documentation. This memo sets out what the Standards Committee is collecting and what guidance we need to put in front of the membership before spring shipping peaks.

## Why this is breaking down for members who know the rule

Selling raw milk across a state line is prohibited under federal rules, but raw-milk cheese is the carve-out provided it has been cured for at least 60 days at a controlled temperature. Most producer members know this. The friction is entirely in the paper trail. Enforcement and documentation expectations vary widely by where the shipment lands. Some states have clear guidance; others leave it to the receiving party's judgment, meaning a lot can clear one state's dock and get held at another over a minor paperwork gap. A member shipping to three regional accounts may face three different informal standards for what proof looks like.

Regulatory attention to dairy standards has been active this winter, making distributors and warehouse managers noticeably more cautious. When a nervous warehouse manager calls asking for proof, an age range on a label is not enough. Producers need a batch log that links each shipped lot to its exact make date and calculates the specific clearance date.

## What the public post on this topic does and does not say

Our communications team published a short piece on interstate shipments this week. It lays out the 60-day rule and urges members to keep clean lot-by-lot logs. It correctly points out that we are not drafting new federal rules. However, it does not solve the operational problem: members do not have a standard template for a curing log that satisfies multiple state inspectors.

We need to bridge that gap before more trucks idle at docks. The Standards Committee is collecting real examples of held shipments and distributor demands through the member portal. We are using those examples to build a model batch log that producers can adopt without inventing their own paperwork format.

## Capacity and next steps

Our committee meetings and advocacy actions are currently tracking 134 total actions for the year across topics like dairy pricing reform, FSMA small-producer exemptions, and labeling standards. Adding a clean, standardized curing log template to our resources does not require a new federal filing, but it does require our Food Safety Committee to review the template before we publish it to the member portal.

We are not asking for headcount. We are asking for alignment across our teams so that when members call asking what documentation holds up at a warehouse dock, Membership Operations and Advocacy give them the exact same answer.
