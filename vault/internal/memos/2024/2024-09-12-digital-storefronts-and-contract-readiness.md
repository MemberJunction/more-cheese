**Subject:** Digital storefronts, contract reopeners, and member price reviews

This week's member inquiries reflect two separate commercial pressures that are intersecting on the same set of balance sheets. On the wholesale side, the federal dairy market news report and our early September spot board commentary have prompted members to ask whether they should reopen winter contracts tied to wholesale cheddar blocks and barrels. On the direct-to-consumer side, members preparing for holiday gift boxes are asking about cold-chain shipping costs and packaging. The Events Team is running Webinar: Digital Storefronts 2024 next Tuesday, September 17, to address the online sales half of this equation, but our membership team is fielding the margin half right now. This memo reviews where our data stands as of Thursday, September 12, and what we need from other teams before next week's sessions.

## Wholesale contract re-evaluations

Our recent communications noted that wholesale cheddar prices climbed through August and early September while national milk output cooled. This convergence has led several producer members to ask if they can reopen supply contracts. We must be entirely clear with members that ICF does not set prices or negotiate contracts. However, the Membership & Outreach Committee has correctly advised members to inspect their reopener clauses rather than letting agreements renew automatically.

The risk for smaller creameries is that a contract locked in when milk prices were softer does not reflect current block and barrel levels or the actual cost of forage and feed. While feed costs have stayed soft relative to climbing milk prices—producing a favorable tailwind for producer margins this fall—labor shortages and vat-side staffing issues remain acute. A higher cheese price on the spot board is not an automatic fix for an understaffed vat room.

## Direct-to-consumer fulfillment and packaging

For smaller producers selling direct or through regional distributors, the immediate concern is not wholesale contracting but mail-order logistics. As outlined in the upcoming webinar, the questions we hear every autumn are identical: how to choose insulated packaging and ice packs that survive two or three days in a truck without destroying the rind, how to price shipping so cold-chain costs do not eat the margin, and whether to build an independent storefront or list through an existing specialty retailer.

The timing of next Tuesday's webinar aligns directly with this shift. We need to ensure that the panel addresses the reality of batch-to-batch variability in handmade cheese. A product listing that does not account for natural seasonal shifts in moisture and texture invites customer complaints when a wedge tastes different from the description.
