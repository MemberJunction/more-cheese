**Subject:** Farmers market dairy licensing and cottage food gaps

As farmers market season opens across the country this week, our membership team has taken three calls from farmstead producers who received market stall permits and assumed their existing wholesale processing licenses covered retail sales off-site. They do not. States continue to treat dairy as a higher-risk category that sits entirely outside cottage food exemptions, and market managers are asking for documentation that our members are scrambling to pull together. This memo reviews the operational friction we are seeing and outlines what we need to coordinate across teams before the weekend rush hits.

## What the calls are telling us

The gap isn't a lack of licensing overall—our members hold proper dairy processing approvals for their primary facilities. The confusion arises when a producer tries to operate a folding table at a public market without a specific direct-sale endorsement or mobile retail rider on their state processing license. Two callers were nearly shut down at the market gate because the inspection stamp on their cheese wrapping referenced a stationary processing plant rather than a retail stand. Furthermore, temperature enforcement in the field is tightening; inspectors are checking hourly temperature logs for coolers rather than accepting a passive thermometer.

Simultaneously, the federal margin protection program enrollment window closed on 29 April. Our advocacy volunteers spent the month pushing members to sign up, but several small operations called us on the final day because they lacked a bookkeeper to track federal deadlines. Between the dairy margin coverage deadline and the farmers market permit surprises, our smallest farmstead members are spending their make days chasing paperwork.

## What the public posts have touched

Our communications team published guidance this week on market permits and cottage food laws, noting that dairy is excluded from low-risk home production rules. That post is already driving traffic to our *Direct-to-Consumer & Farmers' Markets* course. However, Education & Credentialing should note that enrollments for courses stood at zero for the week ending 5 May, meaning the readers are clicking the blog but not converting into immediate course registrations. We should evaluate whether our call to action on the blog needs to be sharper or if members are simply too busy with spring milking to sit through a module right now.

## Capacity and cash flow

On the financial side, the week of 29 April brought 6 new membership purchases (3 Enthusiast and 3 Individual) generating $924 in gross sales, alongside 25 confirmed orders totaling $5,605, with total cash captured reaching $7,325 across all channels. We also logged 8 registrations for the ICF Annual Conference 2024 in Des Moines, bringing total conference registrations up to 351 so far. Cash flow is steady, but staff time is heavily skewed toward answering individual member questions about permits and federal forms rather than proactive outreach.

## What we need

- Communications: verify that the links from this week's blog posts point directly to active course registration pages rather than general catalog overviews.
- Education & Credentialing: review whether we can issue a targeted email digest to farmstead members highlighting the direct-to-consumer course before the next market weekend.
- Advocacy & Standards: compile a quick-reference list of state agriculture department contacts for dairy vendor permits so our team can hand them to callers without digging through state websites.
- Membership Operations: track all market-related licensing inquiries this month so we can feed them into the Q2 membership outreach review.
