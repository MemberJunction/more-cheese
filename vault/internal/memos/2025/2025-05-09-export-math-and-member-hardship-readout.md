## What the export math means for small producers

The public guidance correctly notes that buyers overseas are more willing to absorb landed-cost increases for cheeses with a distinct provenance and a dedicated following. However, our conversations with members this week suggest that distinction is cold comfort if the producer relies on a large distributor whose margins are already compressed. Producers shipping to destination markets carrying separate tariffs are finding that brokers are demanding price concessions that wipe out the producer's margin. A member cannot easily absorb a tariff-driven price gap unless their cheese commands absolute loyalty in the export case.

## Feed and energy costs driving herd decisions

Beyond export markets, the more acute distress reported to our committee this spring is domestic. Feed and energy inflation has forced several small farmstead operations to evaluate whether they can sustain their current herd size. One member milking a mixed herd of about forty cows shared that she chose to sell eight animals this spring rather than drop one of her core aged cheeses or raise wholesale prices past what her accounts would carry. Her arithmetic was straightforward: feed costs scale directly with herd size, whereas dropping an established cheese line means restarting years of trust-building with buyers.
