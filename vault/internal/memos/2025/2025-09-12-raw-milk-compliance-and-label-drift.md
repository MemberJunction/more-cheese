**Subject:** Raw-milk compliance, state-line drift and label templates for fall

The Food Safety Committee has spent the summer tracking how Arkansas, Utah and North Dakota rewrote their raw-milk statutes this year, and none of the changes line up with each other or with the federal ban on interstate raw-milk sales. The gap between what is legal to make at home and what is legal to ship across a state line is generating a steady volume of member inquiries, particularly from small farmsteads trying to maintain mail-order operations without running afoul of the federal prohibition on unpasteurized interstate commerce.

## The three-state divergence

The legislative updates in Arkansas, Utah and North Dakota highlight the operational trap our members face. Arkansas removed its monthly volume cap and widened the definition of raw-milk products to include cheese, kefir and colostrum under a single license. Utah opened retail sales beyond farm-gate direct transactions. North Dakota expanded its product list but explicitly restricted all sales to intra-state direct transactions, prohibiting wholesalers, retailers and any interstate movement.

For a small creamery sitting near a state boundary, these rules create an administrative hurdle. A farmstead member in the upper Midwest who sells aged raw-milk wheels through a mail-order catalog now has to maintain multiple labeling templates for the exact same cheese. The tag that satisfies a direct local pickup in an unlicensed state does not cover the legal requirements for a box shipped across state lines, forcing producers into a corner where they either pasteurize for shipping or restrict their market.

## Advocacy tracker and committee work

Our advocacy numbers reflect this sustained pressure. To date, we have recorded 57 advocacy actions around interstate cheese shipment and 45 around raw-milk aging rules, alongside 23 letter campaigns and 17 petition signatures specifically targeting shipment boundaries. Members are not asking us to rewrite the federal aging rules; they are asking for a reliable, current state-by-state reference sheet that outlines what is legal at home, what is legal to ship, and what changed during the 2025 legislative sessions.

The Food Safety Committee is preparing another round of comments for this fall, focusing on practical compliance rather than theoretical legal reform. We are also compiling a list of jurisdictions where new statutes have passed on paper but implementing regulations are still months away from active enforcement. Members operating in those transition zones have told us they need clear guidance rather than speculative interpretations.
