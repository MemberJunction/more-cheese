**Subject:** Herd count fallout and margin calculation gaps for farmstead members

Today's blog post on farmstead herd counts has drawn immediate, quiet agreement from several small-producer members who are running their own mid-year breakeven checks. The core issue is not an acute crisis like a barn fire or a disease scare, but a slow accumulation of input costs that national benchmarks fail to capture. While August's average feed cost per hundredweight of milk was reportedly the lowest in nearly five years, our members report that cooling, labor, and vet expenses have crept up steadily since 2021, and milk prices have drifted downward under the weight of abundant regional supply. This memo reviews how those discrepancies affect our support messaging and where our education offerings need adjustment.

## The gap between national averages and farm ledgers

The federal margin-support program has largely failed to trigger payments for most of the year because its formula relies on a national feed cost benchmark that does not reflect what our farmstead members actually pay. When a producer sits down with an accountant to look at cwt by cwt numbers, they find their actual feed bills, energy bills, and veterinary costs sit meaningfully above the federally assumed averages. That explains why the macroeconomic reports show reasonable margins while individual creameries are quietly culling low-producing animals or renegotiating contracts. Members are realizing that relying on national figures gives a false sense of security.

This reality directly impacts how we discuss pricing and contracts with wholesale buyers. A producer facing flat milk prices but lower individual feed costs has some room to negotiate, but only if they know their actual per-wheel and per-animal breakeven points. Generalizing about market trends without localized numbers leads to bad contract concessions.

## Connecting members to the right tools

We need to ensure our outreach points members toward practical association tools rather than vague reassurance. The Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese course was built specifically for this kind of per-animal, per-wheel analysis, designed for producers who do not employ a full-time controller. Furthermore, our regional reps have reviewed numerous contract structures across the membership this summer and can help identify onerous distributor terms before contracts are signed. Communications should ensure our public and member communications consistently direct producers to these specific resources.

Meanwhile, membership purchases for the week of August 11 show a steady baseline: 21 membership purchases totaling 4,203 gross dollars, bringing our total current membership count to 1,254 at week's end. Out of those purchases, 15 are first-time members, categorized across SmallBusiness (3), Individual (17), and Enthusiast (1). While new acquisition remains functional, retention depends heavily on helping these small operators protect their margins through the upcoming fall season.

## What we need

- Education & Credentialing: review the upcoming cohort schedule for Pricing & Margin for Specialty Cheese to ensure availability aligns with fall contract renewal windows.
- Advocacy & Standards: integrate actual member cost discrepancies into our ongoing feedback regarding federal margin-support formulas and milk price benchmarking.
- Communications: reinforce our practical tool callouts in upcoming member digests rather than general commentary on market supply.
