Following the federal government's monthly supply-and-demand report this week, which cut the 2025 all-milk price forecast by a full dollar from twenty-two dollars and sixty cents down to twenty-one dollars and sixty cents per hundredweight, this memo examines the compounding effect of feed, energy, and milk price pressures on small farmstead creameries. Our membership count stands at 1,127 members at week end, with 16 membership purchases booked during the week generating $4,010 in gross sales. However, the volume of hardship inquiries and informal payment extension requests indicates that headline membership stability masks acute regional distress among our smallest producers.

## The reality behind the milk forecast reduction

A dollar drop per hundredweight is not an abstract statistical adjustment for a small creamery processing local milk herds; it strikes directly at the operating margins of producers already carrying heavy debt loads from winter feed and energy cost inflation. The Class III forecast, which anchors cheese-milk pricing across our primary growing regions, fell even more sharply than the topline number. Members who locked in high feed contracts last autumn are now discovering that spring milk contract renewals are offering less relief than anticipated, leaving them with narrow or negative margins on aged inventories maturing in their caves.

Our advocacy intake logs for the week reflect this anxiety. We recorded four letter-campaign actions and three petition signatures concerning dairy pricing reform, alongside six petition signatures and four letter-campaign actions addressing labeling standards. While these totals show active member engagement, they also reveal that producers are looking to the federation for regulatory intervention and pricing transparency that go beyond standard educational webinars. The Coalition Meeting on dairy pricing reform held this week brought forward several regional reports of creamery consolidations and quiet liquidations that have not yet reached public reporting.

## Internal operational friction and resource allocation

The Events Team and Membership Operations are currently managing two upcoming workshops — Alpine Farm Shop Retail with five registrations taken this week and Prairie Costing with six registrations taken this week — while handling an unusual volume of membership renewal deferrals. The free webinar on apprenticeship programs held on March 12 brought in zero paid registrations, as expected for free programming, but generated a surge of follow-up inquiries regarding workforce retention and labor costs in small processing rooms. Our current staffing structure makes it difficult to parse routine renewal queries from genuine distress notifications without causing delays in response times.

Furthermore, the disconnect between our membership purchase figures and actual operating cash flow requires tighter coordination. Total confirmed orders for the week reached 27 with a gross value of $5,865, and cash captured stood at $4,615 against zero cancellations. While these numbers look healthy on a weekly summary sheet, they include several multi-tier corporate and small business purchases that do not reflect the micro-dairy segment where closures are concentrated. If we continue to treat membership retention as a uniform metric across enthusiasts, small businesses, and corporate suppliers, we will miss the early warning indicators of member lapse.
