**Subject:** Standards Committee readout on small-producer exemption threshold

This memo provides an internal readout on the advocacy items touching raw-milk aging rules and food safety exemptions as we enter the final two weeks of the year. The public blog post published this morning addresses the qualified-exemption threshold under the food safety rules, pointing out that dollar thresholds set several years ago no longer track actual volume because milk, packaging, and labor have all grown more expensive. Our committee inboxes have received several small producer notes this fall, confirming that gross sales on invoices are climbing purely on price inflation rather than added wheel count.

## What the current advocacy record shows

The broader federation advocacy record for the year shows 32 total actions across letter campaigns, petition signatures, and coalition meetings. Within that total, food safety rules and small-producer exemptions represent a significant portion of our member engagement, specifically 10 actions on raw-milk aging rules and 7 actions on Food Safety Modernization Act small-producer exemptions. Members are worried that the renewal cycle will push them out of the lighter compliance path on paper alone, without a single additional wheel leaving the creamery.

The core challenge we are explaining to members is that the exemption was originally designed to distinguish farmstead operations selling direct to consumers and local retailers from large industrial plants shipping across multiple states. That distinction remains sound, but the fixed monetary cap fails to account for a decade of cost growth. When a creamery's actual production units remain flat while its gross revenue climbs past the statutory line due to input price inflation, the rule stops measuring scale and starts penalizing inflation.

## Committee alignment and member outreach

The Standards Committee is compiling redacted accounts from working creameries to build a factual record before any formal comment window opens. We are asking members who write in to share unit histories rather than general complaints, because regulators require concrete production data to evaluate threshold adjustments. This is an area where association dues deliver direct value: individual small creameries do not have the internal resources to track threshold history against a decade of milk and labor cost data, but a coordinated trade association can assemble those examples into a coherent submission.

We must ensure our internal records and upcoming committee agendas reflect the same cautious tone found in our public communications. We are not promising that the threshold will move; we are promising that if the agency reopens the rule, we will have documented member evidence ready rather than scrambling after the fact.
